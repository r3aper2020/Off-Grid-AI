## Knot Tying and Rope Management
**Chapter 8**

The rope is a vital piece of equipment to the mountaineer. It is used to address many of the problems and hazards encountered in the mountains. When climbing, rappelling, or building rope installations, the mountaineer must know how to utilize and maintain this equipment.

Misuse can cause serious injury or death. This chapter discusses equipment, terminology, knots, hitches, rope management techniques, care, and maintenance.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SECTION I – TYPES OF ROPE, WEBBING, AND CARABINERS
8-1. Mountaineering equipment is engineered to be lightweight but strong enough to provide a wide margin of safety. Each item is tested to high standards. In most cases, user error is to blame when this equipment fails. Soldiers should regularly check and inspect mountaineering equipment. Equipment should be replaced when there is any obvious damage, or it has exceeded the manufacturer’s lifecycle recommendation.

8-2. All mountaineering equipment used in training should be constructed and tested to a recognized standard. The International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation, or Union des International Alpine Association (UIAA), is the certifying body that defines the standards for mountaineering and climbing equipment. This safety commission develops the standards for mountaineering and climbing equipment recognized throughout the world. In Europe, the European Conformity, or Conformité Européene (CE), and the European Committee for Standardization establish standards across all industries. They publish standards that align with the UIAA standards for all climbing and mountaineering equipment produced in Europe. The United States has no specific standards, but American manufacturers have their equipment tested to receive UIAA certification. Any equipment used for mountaineering should be marked with either the UIAA safety label or the CE standard that it meets.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: ROPES AND CORDS
8-3. Ropes and cords are Soldiers’ lifelines in the mountains. Soldiers should understand the types of ropes and cords and their applications. All ropes and cords used in mountaineering and climbing today are constructed with the same basic configuration. The construction technique is referred to as a kernmantle rope, which is a rope constructed with a load-bearing core of twisted synthetic fibers and a protective outer sheath of interwoven synthetic fibers. The core of fibers provides the strength of the material, which is protected by a woven sheath like parachute cord. (See figure 8-1.)

![Figure 8-1. Kernmantle rope construction](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-1.webp)

8-4. There are many types of fibers and ropes that each have unique characteristics, making them suitable for specific tasks. The two main categories of rope are static and dynamic. Military mountaineers need to be able to differentiate between the two and know how each rope should be used.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: STATIC ROPE
8-5. A static rope is a kernmantle rope designed for minimal stretch under load, primarily used for hauling, lowering, and anchor systems construction. It has a core (kern) that is laid straight, giving it low stretch (2 to 8 percent). They are constructed with a tightly woven sheath (mantle) and are more durable than dynamic ropes. Static ropes are preferred for rappelling, rescue operations, load hauling, and rope installations. They should never be used in situations where the rope is shock loaded (for example, lead climbing). Typical diameter for static rope is between 9 and 11 millimeters (mm). Static rope is issued in 600 feet (183 meters) spools. While the minimum tensile strength for static ropes for military use is 4,500 pounds, the recommended working load is often 25 percent of the actual strength of that rope. The tensile strength for the 9.5 mm-issued low-stretch kernmantle rope is approximately 6,000 pounds. The length needed is dependent on the specific task being conducted. A 45-meter (150 feet) static has been the standard military length since the introduction of nylon ropes, but some tasks or obstacles may require a longer rope. The length of rope over 100 meters is a challenge to carry and manage.

8-6. The military purchases black static ropes to prevent a static rope from being mistaken for a climbing rope; however, static ropes are manufactured in all colors and patterns. A rope of unknown history or construction should not be used for any application.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: DYNAMIC ROPE (CLIMBING ROPE)
8-7. A dynamic rope is a kernmantle rope designed to stretch and absorb force, primarily used to protect climbers from fall injuries. All climbing should be protected with dynamic ropes. Even short falls on static rope can cause serious injury. Standards for climbing ropes are established by the UIAA, and all climbing ropes should be certified.

8-8. Dynamic ropes come in various diameters, but for most military applications, 9 to 10 mm provide a good compromise of durability and weight. The climbing ropes issued in the assault climbing team kits (ACTKs) are 60-meters long but can be cut shorter to meet mission or training requirements. Military climbing ropes are usually produced with olive drab or tan sheaths.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: CORD
8-9. Cord is thinner in diameter than rope. All cord is static and constructed in the same kernmantle structure as larger rope. It is used for constructing anchors, as well as most components in technical rope systems.

Military mountaineering applications work best when Soldiers use 6- to 7-mm diameter cord. It is issued to units on a 200-meter spool and then cut down. It is commonly used in two configurations. Most mountaineering tasks can be accomplished with these two lengths. Units that conduct operations that require ascending a rope or extensive glacier travel may cut additional cords to length for specific tasks:

Cordelette—6-meters long, any appropriate joining knot for anchor construction and general use.

Prusik cord—1.8-meters long, joined with a double fisherman’s bend, for friction hitches.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: WEBBING AND SLINGS
8-10. Webbing is a flat or tubular nylon material that ranges in widths from 9 to 25 mm and has many uses in mountaineering. (See figure 8-2.) This material comes on a spool; it can be cut to a desired length for various purposes. It can be joined or presewn to form slings.

8-11. Slings are presewn or tied loops of webbing. (See figure 8-3, page 152.) A sling is often attached to a harness to act as a personal safety, to extend a rappel device, or attach to a fixed rope. A sling rigged with two carabiners is a runner. Lead climbers use runners to clip the climbing rope to pieces of protection. Most sewn slings are 60 cm and are usually made of nylon.

Sewn slings are made in several lengths: 30 cm, 60 cm, 120 cm, and 180 cm.

Sewn slings are also manufactured with high-strength, low-stretch materials. These are lighter than nylon, do not absorb water, and are more resistant against UV deterioration. However, they are very

static, exhibit very low-melting points, and are not recommended for use as personal safeties or friction hitches.

8-12. If sewn slings are unavailable, tubular webbing can be used to create tied slings. Tubular webbing can be cut to length 175 cm (69 inches) and joined with a water knot to create a sling that is 60 cm (24 inches).

Tied slings can be used for any task as sewn slings but require additional inspection. Tied slings can also be untied and retied around anchors and are more adjustable than sewn slings. Tubular webbing can also be used to create expedient harnesses.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: CARABINERS
8-13. A carabiner is a D-shaped or oval-shaped metal link, with one spring-hinged side, that is used in mountaineering as a connector between soft materials and to capture a freely running rope. A carabiner is one of the most versatile pieces of equipment available to the mountaineer. This simple piece of gear is the critical connection between anchors, ropes, and mountaineers. (See figure 8-4.)

8-14. Most carabiners are constructed of aluminum. Carabiners are produced in several different shapes. Oval carabiners were once popular for general mountaineering because their symmetry makes them good for many purposes. Most carabiners used today are offset D-shaped carabiners, which offer the best compromise of weight to strength and ease of clipping and racking. (See figure 8-4.)

![Figure 8-4. Carabiner components](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-4.webp)

8-15. A carabiner’s design allows it to be loaded in opposite directions along the spine. (See figure 8-4.) When loaded improperly, the strength of a carabiner is reduced significantly. When using a carabiner, care should be taken to avoid cross loading the carabiner across the gate and spine and to avoid tri-loading. (See figure 8-5, page 154.)

8-16. There are two types of carabiners used in mountaineering: the locking carabiner and the nonlocking carabiner.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: LOCKING CARABINERS
8-17. Locking carabiners feature a barrel-shaped sleeve that prevents the gate from opening. They are used where an accidental carabiner opening could cause system failure. Any live load should be attached to an anchor with a locking carabiner. Locking carabiners are used for rappel hook ups, personal safeties, and the primary attachments in most rope systems. (See figure 8-5, page 154.)

![Figure 8-5. Improper carabiner loading](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-5.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: NONLOCKING CARABINERS
8-18. Most carabiners do not lock. Locking mechanisms add weight and slow the manipulation of carabiners.

Nonlocking carabiners are used in most mountaineering applications. They should be used where there is more than one attachment and accidental gate opening will not cause system failure.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SECTION II – BASIC TERMINOLOGY
8-19. Understanding basic terminology is important when learning how to tie knots. The terms explained in this section are the most common in military mountaineering. (See figure 8-6, page 156.)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: RUNNING END
8-20. A running end is the loose or working end of the rope.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: STANDING END
8-21. The standing end is the static, stationary, or nonworking end of the rope.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: BIGHT
8-22. A bight of rope is a bend in the rope in which the standing end is parallel to the running end and the rope does not cross itself.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: LOOP
8-23. A loop is a bend of the rope in which the rope crosses itself. When the running end crosses over the top of the standing end, it is an overhand loop. When the running end crosses under the standing end, it is an underhand loop.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: TURN
8-24. A turn wraps around an object, providing 360° contact.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: ROUND TURN
8-25. A round turn wraps around an object one and one-half times so that the running and standing end are parallel. A round turn is used to hold an anchor or knot in place.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: HALF HITCH
8-26. A half hitch is a turn with the running end crossing over the standing end.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: TAIL
8-27. The tail is the excess portion of the running end of the rope when the knot is completed. If a knot requires an overhand safety, the length of the tail is measured from the safety knot to the end of the rope. A safety knot is a backup knot tied to prevent a primary knot from slipping loose.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: DRESSING
8-28. Dressing a knot is arranging all the knot parts, removing unnecessary kinks, twists, and slack so that all rope parts of the knot are secured and tight.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SETTING
8-29. Setting a knot is removing all unnecessary slack from the knot. This ensures that the knot is tight and minimizes the chances of the knot becoming untied.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SECTION III – KNOT TYING AND CONSIDERATIONS
8-30. The term knot is commonly used to describe numerous categories. A knot is the fixed portion of a rope that has been manipulated around itself, another object, or both during the process of being tied for some purpose. Sometimes it is referred to as a “hard knot” since its condition will remain intact until actively untied. Knots are self-contained and self-supporting and do not need another object or rope to maintain its integrity. Hitches are not considered knots. Yet, for simplicity, they are generalized into a single category.

Knots should never be considered permanent. It is possible that they can loosen or become less secure under certain conditions. Knots should be inspected regularly. Many knots can accomplish the same task; therefore, it is important to understand these considerations when choosing the appropriate knot to tie.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: TYPES OF KNOTS
8-31. There are several broad types of knots that are used in mountaineering. Military mountaineers should be familiar with the following types and terms at a minimum.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: BENDS
8-32. Bends are used to join the ends of rope, cord, or webbing. When tied correctly, they are considered safe and strong enough for many applications. Specific joining knots should be used for certain situations and applications. Take care to learn the purpose of each joining knot and to have a clear understanding of where, when, and why to use each one.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: HITCHES
8-33. Hitches are like knots but are tied around another object or rope. Without an object like a carabiner or another rope, a hitch would not retain its form and fall apart.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: FRICTION HITCHES
8-34. A friction hitch is a type of hitch tied with webbing or cordage around a rope that can be moved easily while no force is applied to it but cinches and becomes fixed when tensioned. Friction hitches grip the rope while under load, but they can be moved along the rope when unloaded. There are several friction hitches, each with different applications. Using the wrong friction hitch can be dangerous or create additional problems.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: LOOP KNOTS
8-35. Loop knots are used to create fixed or adjustable loops in rope or webbing. Some loops are directional to prevent the distortion of a knot when loaded.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SPECIALTY KNOTS
8-36. These knots do not fit the above criteria but are still useful to know. They may only serve one specific purpose for mountaineering.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: KNOT TYING CONSIDERATIONS
8-37. There are several factors to consider when selecting knots for use or training. Military mountaineers do not need to be able to tie every possible knot that can be used in mountaineering. They just need to be able to tie an appropriate knot for each application they may face in their duties. When selecting knots for use in mountaineering rigging, selecting simpler knots that are easier to tie, untie, and inspect are preferable to complex specialty knots, even if the complex knot is a little more effective. When considering teaching knot tying techniques to Soldiers and new military mountaineers, knots that are easier to teach and learn are preferred, as are multipurpose knots. Ultimately, the number and complexity of knots that military mountaineers may need to be proficient with increases as the scope and complexity of their technical mountaineering responsibilities and duties increase.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: STRENGTH
8-38. Introducing a knot reduces the rope’s strength by a quarter to almost half of its original strength. Certain knots retain more strength than others do. This depends on knot structure. (See table 8-1 for some examples of the remaining rope strength associated with common knots.)

![Table 8-1. Knotted kernmantle rope retained strength (single strand)](img:25-knots-rope/tab-8-1.webp)

8-39. Choosing a knot based on its strength alone is not always the best choice. Using knots that are easy to tie and teach are ideal. The ability to untie knots after being loaded is important for the reuse and recovery of that material.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: EASE OF INSPECTION
8-40. Efficiently inspecting knots is important to ensure they have been tied properly and safely. Choosing knots that are difficult to inspect or that can easily be tied incorrectly heightens the chance of human error and increases risk.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: CONSERVATION OF MATERIAL
8-41. Using as little material as possible is the desired end state in knot tying. Some knots use more material than others do even if they accomplish the same purpose. It is important to recognize this when choosing the appropriate knot for a given application.

Versatility, ease of tying, untying, and teaching were the primary factors considered for selection.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: FIGURE-EIGHT SLIP KNOT
8-45. The figure-eight slip knot forms an adjustable bight in a rope. (See figure 8-8.)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-47. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

The knot is in the shape of a figure eight.

Both ropes of the bight pass through the same loop of the figure eight.

The sliding portion of the rope is the long working end of the rope.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: FIGURE EIGHT ON A BIGHT (FIGURE-EIGHT LOOP)
8-48. The figure eight on a bight is used to form a fixed loop in a rope. (See figure 8-9.) In certain situations or systems, this knot can be tied with two bights to form two fixed loops (double figure eight on a bight).

This is achieved by folding the cord or rope to form two bights and prior to tying the figure eight.

![Figure 8-9. Figure eight on a bight](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-9.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-50. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

The loop is the desired size.

The ropes in the loop are parallel and do not cross over each other.

The knot is tightly dressed.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: FIGURE-EIGHT RETRACE (REROUTED FIGURE EIGHT)
8-51. The rerouted figure-eight knot produces the same result as a figure-eight loop. However, by tying the knot in a retrace, it can be used to tie into a harness or fasten the rope to objects. (See figure 8-10.) It is also called a figure-eight retrace. This is the preferred method to tie into a climbing rope.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-52. The following steps are used to tie a figure-eight retrace:

STEP 1. Tie an inline figure-eight knot, leaving a long enough running end to go around the object or through the harness, and then back through the knot. Leave this knot loose to allow the rope to be routed back through itself easily.

STEP 2. Take the running end and place it through the attachment point on a harness or around an anchor.

STEP 3. With the running end, insert the rope back through the loop of the knot in reverse.

STEP 4. Retrace the knot around the wrap and back to the standing part.

STEP 5. Remove all unnecessary twists and crossovers; dress the knot down.

![Figure 8-10. Rerouted figure-eight knot](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-10.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-53. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

- A doubled rope runs side by side in the shape of a figure eight.

The knot forms a fixed loop around a fixed object or harness.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: TWO-LOOP FIGURE EIGHT
8-54. The two-loop figure eight is used to form two adjustable, fixed loops in a rope. (See figure 8-11, page 162.) This knot is useful because it forms two independent loops that are easily adjustable to different lengths.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-55. The following steps are used to tie a two-loop figure eight:

STEP 1. Using a doubled rope, form an 18-inch bight in the left hand with the running end facing the left.

STEP 2. Grasp the bight with the right hand and make a 360° turn around the standing end in a counterclockwise direction.

STEP 3. With the working end, form another bight, then place that bight through the loop just formed in the left hand.

STEP 4. Hold the bight with the left hand, and then place the original bight (moving toward the left hand) over the knot.

STEP 5. Dress the knot down.

![Figure 8-11. Two-loop figure eight](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-11.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-56. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

There is a double figure-eight knot with two loops that share a common locking bar.

The two loops must be adjustable by means of a common locking bar.

The common locking bar is on the bottom of the double figure-eight knot.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: DIRECTIONAL FIGURE EIGHT
8-57. The directional figure-eight knot forms a single, fixed loop in the middle of the rope that lays back along the standing part of the rope. (See figure 8-12.)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-58. The following steps are used to tie a directional figure eight:

STEP 1. Create an overhand loop, with the loop facing the opposite direction of the finished knot.

STEP 2. With the overhand loop formed, tie a figure-eight knot around the standing part.

STEP 3. When dressing the knot down, the running end and the bight must be together.

![Figure 8-12. Directional figure eight](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-12.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-59. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

The loop should be large enough to accept a carabiner but no larger than a helmet-size loop.

The tail and bight must be together.

The figure eight is tied tightly.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: ALPINE BUTTERFLY
8-60. The alpine butterfly knot forms a single, fixed loop in the middle of the rope. (See figure 8-13, page 164.) The knot can be loaded in either direction or is easy to untie after use.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-61. The following steps are used to tie an alpine butterfly knot:

STEP 1. Take a bight of rope and twist it 360°.

STEP 2. Using the end of the bight, bring it up and back toward the two standing ends that exit at the back of the twisted bight just created. Careful to avoid untwisting the bight while doing this.

STEP 3. Bring the bight back through the portion of the twisted bight that created a hole where the first twist in the bight is located. Pull the bight through that hole.

STEP 4. Next dress down and tighten the knot.

![Figure 8-13. Alpine butterfly knot](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-13.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-62. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

The knot should have a bight of rope exiting two opposing bights of rope.

The opposing bights should be clean and untwisted.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: FIGURE-EIGHT BEND
8-63. The figure-eight bend is used to join the ends of two ropes of equal or unequal diameter up to a 5-mm difference. (See figure 8-14.) This joining knot should be the primary knot used to join cord. It can be untied after loading, which allows the material to be used for other applications.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-64. The following steps are used to tie a figure-eight bend:

STEP 1. Form a bight in the running end of the rope.

STEP 2. With the other hand, grasp the running end (short end) and make a 360° turn around the standing end.

STEP 3. Place the running end through the loop just formed creating an inline figure eight.

STEP 4. Route the running end of the other rope back through the figure eight starting from the original rope’s running end. Trace the original knot to the standing end.

STEP 5. Remove all unnecessary twists and crossovers. Dress the knot down.

![Figure 8-14. Figure-eight bend](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-14.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-65. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

There is a figure eight with two ropes running side by side.

The running ends are on opposite sides of the knot.

There is an approximate 4-inch tail.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: WATER KNOT
8-66. The water knot is used to attach the ends of webbing. (See figure 8-15.) The water knot is also known as a ring bend or tape knot.

![Figure 8-15. Water knot](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-15.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-68. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

There are two overhand knots, one retracing the other.

There is no slack in the knot, and the working ends come out of the knot in opposite directions.

There is an approximate 4-inch tail.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: FLAT OVERHAND
8-69. The flat overhand is used for joining two ropes that are being used to rappel. (See figure 8-16.) Additionally, it is used as a stopper knot at the end of the ropes to prevent Soldiers from rappelling off the end of the ropes.

![Figure 8-16. Flat overhand](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-16.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-71. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

- A double overhand dressed without twists.

- Minimum 18-inch tails.

- Knot should be hard to the touch, not loose, or sloppy.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: DOUBLE FISHERMAN’S KNOT
8-72. The double fisherman’s knot is used to join the ends of a rope of the same diameter. (See figure 8-17.) Once loaded, it can become very difficult to untie. Avoid using this knot if it must be untied after use.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-73. The following steps are used to tie a double fisherman’s knot:

![Figure 8-17. Double fisherman’s knot](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-17.webp)

STEP 1. With the working end of one rope, tie two wraps around the standing part of another rope.

STEP 2. Insert the working end (step 1) back through the two wraps and draw it tight.

STEP 3. With the working end of the other rope, which contains the standing part (steps 1 and 2), tie two wraps around the standing part of the other rope, routing the wraps in the opposite direction as the first knot (the working end in step 1). Insert the working end back through the two wraps and draw tight.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
8-74. The following must be true for the knot to be correct:

Two double overhand knots securing each other as the standing parts of the rope are pulled apart.

Four rope parts on one side of the knot form two X patterns; four rope parts on the other side of the knot are parallel.

Ends of rope exit knot opposite each other with approximately 4-inch tails.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SQUARE KNOT
8-75. The square knot is used to join the ends of rope of equal diameter while under tension. (See figure 8-18, page 170.) This joining knot should only be used when it is impossible to tie a more secure joining knot like the figure-eight bend or double fisherman’s knot.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-76. The following steps are used to tie a square knot:

STEP 1: Holding one working end in each hand, place the working end in the right hand over the one in the left hand.

STEP 2: Pull it under and back over the top of the rope in the left hand.

STEP 3: Place the working end in the left hand over the one in the right hand and repeat step 2.

STEP 4: Dress the knot down and secure it with an overhand knot on each side of the square knot.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Checkpoints
![Figure 8-18. Square knot](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-18.webp)

There are two interlocking bights.

The running end and standing part are on the same side of the bight formed by the other rope.

The running ends are parallel to and on the same side of the standing ends with 4-inch minimum tails after the overhand safeties are tied.

8-78. The bowline is used to tie the end of a rope around an anchor. It may also be used to tie a single fixed loop at the end of a rope. (See figure 8-19.)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: PRUSIK
8-90. The prusik is a friction hitch that can be loaded in either direction. When force is applied to this friction hitch, it is very difficult to move or loosen. It can be tied with the middle or end of a cord.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tying the Knot
8-91. The prusik can be tied with a cordelette to an installation rope. An installation rope is the primary load-bearing rope or strand in a rope system. ## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Middle-of-the-Rope Prusik

STEP 2. Reach down through the 12-inch bight. Pull up both working ends and lay them over the installation rope. Repeat this process twice more, making sure that the working ends pass in the

middle of the first two turns. Now there are six turns and a locking bar working across them on the installation rope.

STEP 3. Cross over the standing part of the cord with the working end of the cord.

SECTION IV – ROPES: PREPARATION, CARE, INSPECTION, COILING,

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: CARRYING, AND THROWING
8-127. The service life of a rope depends on the frequency of use, applications (rappelling, climbing, rope installations), speed of descent, surface abrasion, terrain, climate, and quality of maintenance. Ropes may fail if misused, placed over sharp edges, or not properly inspected prior to use.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: PREPARING ROPES
8-128. The mountaineer must select the proper rope for the task to be accomplished according to type, diameter, length, and breaking strength. It is important to prepare all ropes before departing on a mission.

Avoid rope preparation in the field.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: PACKAGING
8-129. New rope comes from the manufacturer in different configurations, boxed on a spool in various lengths, or coiled and bound in some manner. New ropes should be carefully unspooled or uncoiled to avoid kinks and prevent tangling.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Coil
8-130. A coil is a secure configuration of rope that allows for easy transport without tangling.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Precut
8-131. Precut ropes are usually packaged in a protective cover, such as plastic.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: SECURING THE ENDS OF ROPE
8-132. If still on a spool, the rope must be cut to the desired length. All ropes fray at the ends unless they are bound or seared. Both static and dynamic rope ends are secured in the same manner. The ends must be heated to the melting point to attach the inner core strands to the outer sheath. By fusing the two together, the sheath cannot slide backward or forward. Ensure that this is only done to the ends of the rope. If the rope is exposed to extreme temperatures, the sheath could be weakened, along with the inner core, reducing overall tensile strength. The ends may also be dipped in enamel or lacquer for further protection.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: CARE AND MAINTENANCE
8-133. The rope is a climber’s lifeline. The rope must be maintained and used properly. Climbers should use the following general guidelines when handling ropes:

- While in use, do not allow the rope to touch sharp edges. Rope is cut more easily when under tension.

If the rope must be used over a sharp edge, pad the edge for protection and inspect often.

- Never allow one rope to continuously run over another. The friction causes the nylon to melt.

- Never leave a rope knotted or tightly stretched for longer than necessary. Doing so reduces the

strength and life of the rope over time.

- UV radiation (sunlight) tends to deteriorate nylon over long periods. This becomes important if rope

installations are left in place for months.

- Do not step on or drag ropes on the ground unnecessarily. Small particles of dirt grind between the

inner strands slowly cutting them.

- All ropes should be washed periodically to remove dirt and grit and rinsed thoroughly. Army-issued rope washes are made from short pieces of modified pipe that connect to any faucet. Pinholes within the pipe force water to circulate around and scrub the rope as the Soldier slowly feeds it through the washer. Ropes should not be stored wet. Dry wet ropes by uncoiling and hanging them in large loops off the ground. Never dry a rope with high heat or in direct sunlight.

- When not in use, ropes should be loosely coiled and hung on wooden pegs rather than nails or other metal objects. Storage areas should be relatively cool with low-humidity levels to prevent mildew

or rotting. Rope may also be loosely stacked and placed in a rope bag and stored on a shelf. Avoid storage in direct sunlight, as the UV radiation deteriorates the nylon over long periods.

Inspect the rope before each use for frayed or cut spots, mildew or rot, or defects in construction (new rope).

- Do not mark ropes with paints or allow them to touch oils or petroleum products. Some of these

weaken or deteriorate nylon.

- Never subject the rope to high heat or flame. Heat and flame significantly weaken rope.

- Never use a mountaineering rope for any purpose except mountaineering.

Each rope should have a corresponding rope log DA Form 5752-R (Rope Log [Usage and History]), which is also a safety record. On the form, annotate use, terrain, weather, application, number of falls, dates, and so on. Annotate each time the rope is used on the form. DA Form 5752-R is authorized for local reproduction on 8 1/2- by 11-inch paper.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: INSPECTION
8-134. Ropes should be inspected before and after each use, especially when working around loose rock or sharp edges.

- Although the core of the kernmantle rope cannot be seen, it is possible to damage the core without damaging the sheath. Check a kernmantle rope by carefully inspecting the sheath before and after use while the rope is being coiled. When coiling, be aware of how the rope feels as it runs through the hands. Immediately note and tie off any lumps or depressions felt.

- Damage to the core of a kernmantle rope usually consists of filaments or yarn breakage that results in a slight retraction. If enough strands rupture, a localized reduction in the diameter of the rope results in a depression that can be felt or even seen. Check any other suspected areas further by putting them under tension (the weight of one person, approximately 185 pounds standing on a prusik tensioning system is about maximum). This procedure emphasizes the lump or depression by separating the broken strands and enlarging the dip. If a noticeable difference in diameter is obvious, retire the rope immediately.

- Many dynamic kernmantle ropes are quite soft. They may retain an indentation occasionally after an impact or under normal use without any trauma to the core. When damage is suspected, patiently inspect the sheath for abnormalities. Damage to the sheath does not always mean damage to the core. Inspect carefully.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: COILING AND CARRYING ROPE
8-135. Use the butterfly or mountain coil to coil and carry the rope. Each is easy to accomplish and results in a minimal number of kinks, twists, and knots later during deployment.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: BUTTERFLY COIL
8-136. The butterfly coil is the quickest and easiest technique for coiling. (See figure 8-37.)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Coiling
8-137. To start the butterfly coil, grasp both ends of the rope and begin back stacking, ensuring the ends of the rope are not buried at the bottom of the stack. Find the center of the rope and form a bight. With the bight in the left hand, grasp both ropes and slide the right hand out until there is approximately one arm-length of rope. Place the doubled rope over the head, draping it around the neck and over the shoulders. Ensure that it hangs no lower than the waist. Soldiers ensure the rest of the doubled rope is in front of them and continue making doubled bights and placing them over the head in the same manner as the first bight. Coil alternating from side to side (left to right, right to left) while maintaining equal-length bights. Continue coiling until approximately two arm-lengths of rope remain. Remove the coils from the neck and shoulders and hold the center in one hand. Wrap the two ends upwards around the coils with a minimum of three complete wraps, ensuring that the first wrap locks back on itself.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: Tie Off and Carrying
8-138. Take both strands from the tails of the rope, forming a double bight and pass it through the apex of the coils. Pull the loose ends through the doubled bight and dress it down. Ensure that the loose ends do not exceed 2 meters. In this configuration, the coiled rope is secure enough for hand carrying or carrying in a rucksack or for storage. To carry on the body, place the butterfly coil on the back, with one tail over each of the shoulders, bring back directly under the arms and wrap at least once around the body, ensuring to capture all the coils. Secure the ends with a square knot, which does not need overhand safeties. (See figure 8-37 for a farmer’s tie off.)

![Figure 8-37. Butterfly coil with farmer’s tie off](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-37.webp)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: MOUNTAIN COIL
8-139. To start a mountain coil, grasp the rope about 1 meter from the end with one hand. Run the other hand along the rope until both arms are outstretched. Grasping the rope firmly, bring the hands together, forming a loop, which is laid in the hand closest to the end of the rope. This is repeated, forming uniform loops that run in a clockwise direction, until the rope is completely coiled. The rope may be given a one-quarter twist as each loop is formed to overcome any tendency for the rope to twist or form figure eights.

8-140. To finish the mountain coil, form a bight about 30-cm long with the starting end of the rope and lay it along the top of the coil. Uncoil the last loop and, using this length of the rope, begin making wraps around the coil and the bight, wrapping toward the closed end of the bight. Make six to eight wraps to adequately secure the coil, and then route the end of the rope through the closed end of the bight. Pull the running end of the bight tight, securing the coil.

8-141. The mountain coil may be carried in either the pack (by forming a figure eight), doubling it and placing it under the flap, or by placing it over the shoulder and under the opposite arm, slung across the chest. (Figure 8-38 shows how to coil a mountain coil.)

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: COILING CORDS AND SLINGS
![Figure 8-38. Mountain coil](img:25-knots-rope/fig-8-38.webp)

8-142. Length of cord, webbing, or rope less than 8 meters may be coiled so that they can be hung from the harness. Hold the ends of the cord or the joining knot in the left hand. Coil the doubled rope into 6- to 8-inch loops until an approximate 12-inch bight is left. Wrap that bight around the coil, ensuring that the first wrap locks on itself. Wrap until there is just enough bight left to feed through the bights formed at the top of the coil. Dress it down tightly. Now the piece of rope or webbing may be hung from a carabiner on the harness or attached to a gear sling and slung over the shoulder. The same can be done with slings.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: UNCOILING, BACK STACKING, AND STACKING
8-143. When the rope is needed for use, it must be uncoiled and back stacked on the ground properly to avoid kinks and snarls. Choose a location that is clear of sharp rocks, sticks, or other items that could damage the rope or cause snarls when deploying the rope. Also, avoid wet or muddy areas. When these locations are unavoidable, using a tarp, poncho, or rope bag to place under the rope when stacking can provide protection.

8-144. Untie the tie off and lay the coil on the ground. Single back stack the rope to minimize kinks and snarls. (This is also useful when moving the rope a short distance and coiling is not desired.) Take one end of the rope in the left hand and run the right hand along the rope until both arms are outstretched. Next, lay the end of the rope in the left hand on the ground, ensuring that the standing end of the rope is laid off to the side and is not buried under the stack. With the left hand, regrasp the rope next to the right hand and continue laying the rope on the ground.

8-145. The rope should be laid or stacked in a neat pile on the ground to prevent it from becoming tangled and knotted when throwing the rope, feeding it to a lead climber, and so on. This technique can also be started using the right hand.

## Knot Tying and Rope Management: THROWING THE ROPE
8-146. Before throwing the rope, it should be back stacked to prevent it from tangling during deployment.

The bottom end of the rope should be anchored to prevent complete loss of the rope over the edge when it is thrown. Several techniques can be used when deploying a rope.

8-147. If the cliff is steep enough, it may be unnecessary to throw the rope. Back stack the rope down the cliff face. The rope is less likely to be carried by the wind or to be caught in trees. It is more likely to be hung up on ledges and shrubs on the face than when thrown. The rope should be thrown at a low angle and blocky terrain.

8-148. To throw the rope, take the strand on the top of the stack and begin to create butterfly coils in the dominant hand. Create enough coils so that there is sufficient weight to carry the rest of the rope down when it is thrown. It is time consuming and almost never necessary to coil the entire length of rope to throw it. The rope is best thrown underhand and slightly up and out from the edge. Throwing the rope too high could cause it to be hung up in tree branches. At a training site where there may be people below, the thrower should confirm that no one is below and sound off with the following warning as they throw: ROPE.

8-149. When windy weather conditions prevail, adjustments must be made. In a strong crosswind, the rope should be thrown angled into the wind so that it lands on the desired target. The stronger the wind, the harder the rope must be thrown to compensate. If the wind is too strong, sometimes it is best to single back stack the rope in a pack. The first Soldier who descends the rope should guide it down the fall line. During the descent, the rope is stacked in such a way that it exits cleanly from the pack to avoid becoming tangled.

Another technique is to coil the rope freely on the shoulders of the first Soldier, allowing it to easily uncoil as the Soldier descends.

8-150. This Soldier needs to incorporate a hands-free backup during the rappel to allow stopping and using both hands to manage the rope, if necessary.
