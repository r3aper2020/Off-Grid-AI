## Environmental Injuries
**Chapter 3**

Operations in mountainous areas will expose Soldiers to a variety of harsh environmental conditions not found in lowland areas. Soldiers exposed to these conditions are at a heightened risk of becoming a casualty. This risk increases with lack of experience and training. Troops conducting operations in mountainous regions often encounter cold temperatures that can degrade physical performance, impact morale, and create cold-weather casualties. The lack of oxygen in high-altitude environments impairs many aspects of normal Soldier tasks. Soldiers may develop altitude illnesses that can produce debilitating symptoms and require immediate MEDEVAC. If not accounted for in the planning process, these conditions will negatively impact the mission. Soldiers will become fatigued sooner in this environment and must reduce their pace or lighten their load to remain healthy and effective. With experience and proper training, Soldiers will become more durable and lethal in this environment.

## Environmental Injuries: SECTION I – ENVIRONMENTAL INJURY PREVENTION
3-1. Units operating in mountainous regions are exposed to various types of injuries and illnesses unassociated with other areas. Medical considerations are like those for other environments; however, there are some unique aspects of mountain operations to consider to provide effective support.

## Environmental Injuries: ILLNESSES AND INJURIES
3-2. Most injuries in the mountain environment are orthopedic and soft-tissue injuries. These include sprains, strains, abrasions, contusions, and fractures. Skills in basic first aid are essential to all Soldiers operating in the mountains and should be reinforced with regular sustainment training. (See TB MED 505 for more information.) Medical personnel should receive training in identifying and treating these prior to deployment.

## Environmental Injuries: TREATMENT AND EVACUATION
3-3. In harsh mountain weather, the most important course of action is to provide injured Soldiers with medical aid as soon as possible. Immediate first aid is given on site. Due to rough terrain, medical units can seldom reach unit aid stations by vehicle to evacuate casualties. Litter bearers must move casualties to the rear where they can be evacuated by ground or air to clearing stations. The victim must be protected from the weather and shock during transportation. Rendezvous points are coordinated with medical units as far forward as possible. All litter bearers must receive training in evacuation techniques and first aid. Lightly wounded personnel may need assistance to move over rough terrain. (See chapter 15 for CASEVAC TTP in mountainous terrain.)

## Environmental Injuries: INJURIES RELATED TO COLD EXPOSURE
3-4. There are several common injuries associated with cold temperature exposure. Some are more commonly associated with hot weather but present just as serious a threat in cold conditions.

## Environmental Injuries: DEHYDRATION
3-5. Dehydration is the loss of bodily fluids that prevents or slows normal bodily functions. This is usually caused by overexertion and improper water intake. Although the mechanism that causes dehydration is not specifically cold temperatures, the environment and the body’s natural response to the cold often lead to Soldiers becoming dehydrated. Dehydration precedes all cold-weather injuries and is a major symptom of acute mountain sickness (AMS). Dehydration contributes to poor performance in all physical activities, even more so than lack of food. Cold-weather requirements for water are no different from in the desert. In fact, they may exceed desert requirements because of the increased difficulty in moving with extra clothing and through the snow. At high altitudes, the air is dry. Combined with a rapid rate of breathing, as much as 2 liters

of liquid may be lost each day through respiration. A Soldier needs about 3 to 6 liters of water each day to prevent dehydration when living and performing physical labor in a cold or mountainous environment.

Leaders should monitor their Soldiers’ water intake, since motivation to drink cold water in a cold environment is low. Coffee and tea are diuretics and cause excessive urination, which contributes to dehydration. They should be avoided. However, providing other noncaffeinated hot liquids is a good way to encourage Soldiers to drink more water. The adequacy of liquid intake can best be judged by the color of urine and volume. Dark, amber-colored urine instead of light yellow or the absence of a need to urinate upon awakening from a night’s sleep is an indicator of dehydration. Thirst is not a good indicator of hydration.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-6. Factors that contribute to dehydration in cold weather are—

The thirst mechanism does not function properly in cold weather.

- Water is often inconvenient to obtain and purify.

The air in cold climates and at high altitudes lacks moisture.

Cold causes frequent urination.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-7. Symptoms of dehydration include darkening urine, decreased amounts of urine being produced, dry mouth, tiredness, mental sluggishness, lack of appetite, headache, fainting, rapid heartbeat, dizziness, higher temperature, upset stomach, and unconsciousness. The symptoms of dehydration resemble those of hypothermia. To distinguish between them, open the victim’s clothes and feel the stomach. If the stomach is cold, the victim is probably hypothermic; if it is warm, the victim is probably dehydrated. However, this test is inconclusive since cold-weather dehydration can also lead to total body cooling. The cold environment may act as a diuretic and impair the body’s ability to conserve fluids. Cold diuresis is an increase in urine output, which is the body’s response to cold-weather immersion. This can contribute to dehydration, which is the precursor to several other cold-weather injuries, both freezing and non-freezing.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-8. Prevent dehydration by consuming 3 to 6 liters of fluids each day (forced drinking in the absence of thirst is mandatory) and avoid caffeine and alcohol. Keep the victim warm and treat them for shock. In advanced cases, administer fluids by mouth if the victim is conscious. Ingesting snow will contribute to loss of body heat. Allow the victim to rest. If the victim fails to improve within 1 hour or is unconscious, evacuate to a medical facility immediately.

## Environmental Injuries: HEAT INJURIES
3-9. Heat injuries, although associated with hot weather, can occur in cold-weather environments. Planning, periodically inspecting personnel clothing (ventilation) and equipment, balancing water and food intake, and resting can prevent most heat injuries.

## Environmental Injuries: Heat Cramps
3-10. Heat cramps are muscle spasms that result from loss of large amounts of salt and water through exertion. Heat cramps are caused by an accumulation of lactic acid in the muscles and a loss of salt through perspiration.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factor
3-11. Strenuous exertion causes the body to rise in temperature and to produce heavy perspiration.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-12. Symptoms of heat cramps include pain and cramping in the arms, legs, back, and stomach. Victims sweat profusely and cannot quench their thirst.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-13. Have the victim rest in a cool, shady area, breathe deeply, and stretch the cramped muscle as soon as possible to obtain relief. Loosen the victim’s clothing and provide cool water to drink. Monitor the victim’s condition and seek medical attention if pain and cramps continue.

## Environmental Injuries: Heat Exhaustion
3-14. Heat exhaustion is rapid water and salt depletion that can occur after exposure to high temperatures or intense exertion. It is often accompanied by dehydration. Heat exhaustion may occur when Soldiers exert themselves in any environment and overheat. The blood vessels in the skin become so dilated that they reduce blood flow to the brain and other organs.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-15. Factors that contribute to heat exhaustion are strenuous activity in hot areas, troops that are not acclimated, inappropriate diet, and insufficient water or rest.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-16. Symptoms of heat exhaustion may resemble fainting but may also include weakness, dizziness, confusion, headache, cold clammy skin, and nausea. The victim may also have a rapid but weak pulse.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-17. Move the victim to a cool, shady area and loosen clothing and boots. Have the victim drink water and, if possible, and if weather conditions are conducive, immerse the victim’s body in water to aid in cooling.

Elevate the victim’s legs to help restore proper circulation. Monitor the victim’s condition, and seek medical attention if symptoms persist. Without proper intervention, heat exhaustion can lead to heat stroke.

## Environmental Injuries: Heat Stroke
3-18. Heat stroke occurs when the core body temperature exceeds 104°F, commonly a result of prolonged exposure to or physical exertion at higher temperatures. Heat stroke is a life-threatening situation caused by overexposure to the sun. The body is so depleted of liquids that its internal cooling mechanisms fail to function.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-19. Factors that contribute to heat stroke are prolonged exposure to direct sunlight, overexertion, dehydration, and depletion of electrolytes.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-20. Symptoms of heat stroke include hot, dry skin; dizziness; confusion and incoherency; headache; nausea; seizures; breathing difficulty; a slow pulse; and loss of consciousness.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-21. Cool the victim immediately and restore breathing and circulation. If the victim is conscious, administer water. If possible, and if weather conditions are conducive, submerge the victim in water to reduce temperature, treat for shock, and prepare for immediate evacuation.

## Environmental Injuries: SOLAR INJURIES
3-22. Solar injuries can happen in warm or cold weather. These types of injuries can be just as incapacitating as most other injuries but usually are not fatal. Peak hours of ultraviolet (UV) radiation are between the hours of 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. Because of the long wavelengths of UV light, cloudy days can be more dangerous than sunny days. On sunny days, the Soldier takes more care due to the bright conditions. On cloudy days, the Soldier tends not to wear sunglasses or sunscreen.

## Environmental Injuries: Sunburn
3-23. Excessive UV radiation from sunlight is a serious threat to military personnel operating in high mountain regions during daylight hours because of several factors increasing its damaging potential.

UV exposure can cause significant short-term disability to military personnel through severe sunburn.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-24. Decreased UV filtering by the thinner atmosphere at high altitude causes an increased intensity of exposure (approximately 4 percent for every 1,000 feet of elevation increase) and a shift of the UV spectrum to more harmful short wavelengths. Reflection from snow and ice increases the intensity of exposure (approximately 75 percent of incident radiation is reflected). Soldiers vary greatly in their risk of sunburn based on their skin type and pigment.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-25. Symptoms of sunburn are painful, burning, red, or blistered skin with slight swelling. The skin may be warm to the touch. In severe cases, chills, fever, and headaches may occur.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-26. Limiting skin exposure with clothes and sunscreen (sun protection factor 15 and above) will prevent most sunburns. Applying to skin areas that are normally shaded, such as the eyelids and under the chin, is necessary to reduce the likelihood of sunburn from light reflected off snow and rocks. Opaque agents, such as zinc oxide, red veterinary petrolatum, or titanium dioxide; can be used for high-risk body areas, such as the nose, lips, and tops of the ears. The agents should be used carefully in a tactical situation where they could make a Soldier more visible to opposing forces. Diagnosis, prevention, treatment, and prognosis of solar radiation injuries are the same as at low altitude. (See TB MED 505.)

## Environmental Injuries: Snow Blindness
3-27. Snow blindness results when solar radiation “sunburns” the eyes. Snow blindness can occur in a few hours of exposure, and there is no sensation of abnormality other than brightness as a warning that eye damage is occurring.

3-28. Using eyewear or goggles that block more than 90 percent of UV radiation will help prevent snow blindness. To prevent injury from reflected light, protective eyewear should have side shields. Additionally, eyewear should have a safety cord or strap to prevent it from being lost accidentally. If sunglasses are not available, opaque eye covering (for example, tape-covered eyeglasses) with narrow, horizontal slits provide adequate field-expedient eye protection.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-29. Eyes may feel painful and gritty, and there may be tearing, blurred vision, severe light sensitivity, and headache. Symptoms are worst for 6 to 8 hours after exposure and resolve in 48 to 72 hours.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-30. Treating snow blindness involves patching the eye and controlling the pain. Cool compresses over the eyes help alleviate some discomfort. Dilating the pupil and using ocular antibiotics are helpful. Pain control may require oral analgesics, sedatives, or both. The prognosis is generally good, although snow blindness may take several days to resolve. The pain and visual decrements will require temporary, but significant, duty limitations. In severe cases, permanent damage can occur.

## Environmental Injuries: BLISTERS
3-31. Blisters often only cause minor discomfort but can become severe enough to restrict a person’s physical activity and mobility temporarily. In some cases, friction blisters have developed into serious complications and infections requiring antibiotics and medical treatment. As one of the most common injuries to the military mountaineer, blisters present a notable adverse impact to mobility and mission readiness.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-32. Rough and sloped terrain increases friction generated between the skin and footwear during foot movements, leading to a higher incidence of blister development.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-33. Blisters are raised, painful, fluid-filled protrusions.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-34. Before a blister forms or when a Soldier first notices one, cover a hotspot with moleskin (over the area and beyond it). Use tincture benzoin to help the moleskin adhere to and toughen the skin. Once a blister has formed, cover it with a dressing large enough to fit over the blister, and then tape it. Never drain blisters unless they are surrounded by redness or if draining pus indicates infection. If this occurs, drain the blister from the side with a clean, sterile needle. After cleaning with soap and water, gently press out the fluid, leaving the skin intact. Make a doughnut of moleskin to go around the blister and apply to the skin. For toe blisters, wrap the entire toe with adhesive tape over the moleskin. (Toenails should be trimmed straight across the top, leaving a 90° angle on the sides. This provides an arch, so the corners do not irritate the skin.)

## Environmental Injuries: CARBON MONOXIDE POISONING
3-35. This is the replacement of oxygen in the blood with carbon monoxide, preventing adequate oxygenation of cells in the body.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factor
3-36. A contributing factor is inhaling fumes from burning fuel, such as fires, stoves, heaters, and running engines, without proper ventilation.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-37. Symptoms are like other common illnesses and include headaches, fatigue, excessive yawning, nausea, dizziness, drowsiness, confusion, and unconsciousness. Death may occur. Three visible symptoms are bright red lips, mouth, and inside of the eyelids.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-38. Remove the victim from the source of contamination; administer oxygen, if available; and evacuate to a medical facility. Severe complications may develop even in casualties who appear to have recovered. If the victim is unconscious, administer rescue breathing and cardio-pulmonary resuscitation (CPR) as needed.

## Environmental Injuries: COLD-WEATHER CONSIDERATIONS
3-39. Military mountain operations can occur in the coldest weather and with minimal logistical support, leaving troops under-equipped for hostile conditions. Individuals in these situations must rely on their prior training and strong leadership in cold stress management. This training can determine whether the mission will succeed or fail with either few or high casualty rates. Individuals in these situations often are not fully prepared, and, consequently, unit commanders and trainers must actively plan to prevent cold casualties.

Soldiers need to be confident that they can master the environment by using preventive measures. Lessons learned from previous cold-weather deployments must be emphasized. Leaders must learn their unit’s capabilities and manage cold exposure relative to the provided guidance. Soldiers must be prepared to survive, move, and fight in winter conditions. Intense cold affects the mind as well as the body. Simple tasks take longer to perform and take more effort than in a temperate climate. When weather conditions become extreme, the problems of survival become more significant. Warmth and comfort become top priorities. The effects of extreme cold and the probability of injury are magnified by a lack of proper diet and sleep. The most important measure in preventing cold-weather injuries is the education of personnel and their leaders.

## Environmental Injuries: TYPES
3-40. Cold injuries may be divided into two types: freezing and non-freezing. The freezing type is known as frostbite. The nonfreezing type includes hypothermia, chilblain, and immersion foot. Cold injuries result from impaired circulation and from cold and ice forming upon the tissues of the body. Temperature alone is not a reliable guide as to whether a cold injury can occur. Low temperatures are necessary for cold injuries to occur, but freezing temperatures are not. Wind speed can accelerate body heat loss under both wet and cold conditions.

## Environmental Injuries: PREVENTATIVE MEASURES
3-41. Commanders should ensure the following measures are taken to prevent cold-weather injuries:

Soldiers’ uniforms are kept as dry as possible and are protected from the elements.

Soldiers are educated on proper use of clothing systems to avoid the effects of overheating and perspiration (layer dressing and ventilation).

The buddy system is used to watch for early signs of cold-weather injuries.

- All Soldiers waterproof their equipment.

The rate of movement should be slow, deliberate, and careful. Soldiers should not move out at a force march pace and then be stationary after they have perspired heavily. Soldiers should not wear excessive cold-weather clothing while moving.

## Environmental Injuries: RISK FACTORS
3-42. Several demographic, behavioral, and past history characteristics increase risk for cold-weather injuries.

## Environmental Injuries: Body Composition
3-43. Individuals with a high amount of body surface area relative to their body mass (long and lean) lose more body heat in the cold than individuals with a small surface area relative to their body weight (short and stocky) do. The reason for this is that the principal means of heat loss in the cold is at the skin surface. This effect is somewhat reduced in short, stocky people because a large body mass maintains a constant temperature better than a small body mass.

## Environmental Injuries: Sex
3-44. The peripheral (for example, hands and feet) cold-injury rate for female Soldiers is two times higher than the rate for males. Sex differences during cold exposure are almost entirely attributable to women’s generally greater body fat content and thicker fat layer under the skin compared to men of comparable age and weight. Women typically have a greater surface area but smaller total body mass (thus lower total body heat content) than men do.

## Environmental Injuries: Race
3-45. African-American male and female Soldiers are two to four times more likely to suffer a cold-weather injury versus their Caucasian counterparts. This increased risk is consistent across all career management fields, which suggests that occupational exposure does not account for the differences between African-Americans and Caucasians. Differences between races may be due to cold-weather experience, but more likely they are due to anthropomorphic considerations (longer, thinner digits) and perhaps greater surface-area-to-mass ratio.

## Environmental Injuries: Fitness and Training
3-46. Overall, physical training and fitness levels appear to have only minor influences on cold management.

Fitter individuals can sustain more physical activity at a higher intensity and longer duration; therefore, they produce more heat from exercise for longer periods when compared to less fit individuals.

## Environmental Injuries: Fatigue
3-47. Physical fatigue can impair shivering and peripheral vasoconstriction responses during cold exposure.

Therefore, physical fatigue can increase the Soldier’s risk for hypothermia. Fatigued Soldiers cannot sustain sufficient activity levels to maintain body temperature during severe cold exposure.

## Environmental Injuries: Age
3-48. Soldiers who are 45 years of age or older may be less cold tolerant than younger people, either because of a decline in physical fitness or because of reduced vasoconstriction and heat conservation in comparison to their younger counterparts.

## Environmental Injuries: Dehydration
3-49. Dehydration can increase susceptibility to cold injury by decreasing the ability to sustain physical activity (particularly if accompanied by heat stress from exercising in heavy clothing) and, therefore, decreasing the ability to sustain thermal balance. In addition, dehydration might impair cognitive function and cause Soldiers to use poor judgment. Dehydration has not been shown to reduce peripheral skin temperatures and does not impair shivering.

## Environmental Injuries: Sustained Operations
3-50. Exertional fatigue, sleep restriction, and underfeeding are common stressors during sustained operations and together impair a Soldier’s ability to maintain thermal balance in the cold. The metabolic heat production response degrades and impairs the ability to sustain exercise performance. Soldiers engaged in sustained operations also may make more judgment errors when tired and increase their exposure and risk of injury. A given cold stressor is more likely to induce cold injury after a long period of sustained operations compared to earlier in the operation. This emphasizes the need for planned rest, sleep, feeding, and recovery before conducting stressful operations in the cold.

## Environmental Injuries: Food and Nutrient Consumption
3-51. Consuming too little nutrients can result in hypoglycemia (low blood sugar), which can impair shivering. Hypoglycemia contributes to an inability to sustain physical activity and the associated exercise-induced heat production during cold exposure. No food for 48 hours, even in the absence of hypoglycemia, results in an impaired ability to shiver and increased risk of hypothermia.

## Environmental Injuries: Alcohol
3-52. Although alcohol may impart a sense of warmth, any peripheral vasodilation increases heat loss and the risk of hypothermia. Alcohol can cause hypoglycemia, which will decrease shivering responses and the ability to sustain physical work. In addition, alcohol blunts the senses and impairs judgment, so the Soldier may not feel the signs and symptoms of a developing cold injury. Soldiers must not consume alcohol while exposed to cold weather.

## Environmental Injuries: Nicotine
3-53. Smoking or chewing tobacco can increase susceptibility to frostbite by increasing vasoconstriction in the periphery (for example, the hands). Heavy smokers (two to three packs per day) have a 30-percent higher incidence of peripheral cold injury, perhaps because nicotine reduces the body’s response to the cold.

Smoking and chewing will be discouraged during cold-weather operations.

## Environmental Injuries: Previous Cold Injuries
3-54. If Soldiers have had previous cold injuries, they are at higher risk for subsequent cold injuries.

## Environmental Injuries: Associated Injury
3-55. Injuries resulting in shock or blood loss reduce blood flow to extremities and may increase the injured individual’s susceptibility to cold injury, which, in turn, can accelerate shock.

## Environmental Injuries: COLD-WEATHER INJURIES
3-56. A cold-weather injury is any preventable injury resulting from exposure to cold environmental conditions. Some of the most common nonfreezing cold-weather injuries are described in paragraphs 3-57 through 3-75.

## Environmental Injuries: Hypothermia
3-57. Hypothermia is the lowering of the body core temperature at a rate faster than the body can produce heat. Hypothermia may be caused by exposure or by sudden wetting of the body, such as falling into a lake or being sprayed with fuel or other liquid. Hypothermia can occur even on moderate days with temperatures of 40°F to 50°F with little precipitation if heat loss exceeds heat gain, and the Soldier’s condition can deteriorate. Hypothermia is classified as mild (core temperature above 90°F or 32°Celsius [C]) or severe (core temperature below 90°F or 32°C). An individual is considered “clinically hypothermic” when their core temperature is less than or equal to 95°F.

3-58. Methods of heat loss:

Radiation—direct heat loss from the body to its surrounding atmosphere is called radiation heat loss.

The head can radiate up to 80 percent of the total body heat output. On cold days, personnel must keep all extremities covered to retain heat. Heat loss from extremities accounts for the largest amount of heat lost from the body.

Conduction—direct transfer of heat from one object in contact with another (being rained on or sitting in snow).

Convection—loss of heat due to moving air or water in contact with the skin. Wind chill is convection cooling. Clothing that ventilates, insulates, and protects must control the layer of warm air next to the skin.

Evaporation—evaporation of perspiration causes heat loss. Wet clothing can cause heat loss by conduction and evaporation. Dressing in layers allows Soldiers to remove or add clothing as needed.

Respiration—when cold air is inhaled, the air is warmed, then exhaled, resulting in a significant source of heat loss.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-59. Factors that contribute to hypothermia are—

Poor nutrition.

- Dehydration.

- Diarrhea.

- Decreased physical activity.

- Accidental immersion in water.

- High winds.

Change in weather.

Inadequate types or amounts of clothing.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-60. Body temperature refers to the core (rectal) temperature. Symptoms become progressively worse as the body temperature drops. 96°F:  Uncontrollable shivering (about 96°F).

Inability to care for self.

Clumsiness (stumbling or falling).

Slow reactions.

Shivering (begins about 2° below normal body temperature).

Sluggish thinking.

Irrational thought.

False sense of warmth.

Cold skin.

Pale or bluish skin.

Inability to perform simple tasks.

 Mental confusion.  Difficulty speaking. 95°F to 90°F:  Apathy.  Amnesia.  Hallucinations.  Decreased heart and respiratory rate.  Weak pulse.  Dilated pupils.

Slurred speech.  Visual disturbance. 90°F to 85°F. Shivering may stop. In addition, a Soldier may experience—  Muscular rigidity.  Disorientation.

Exhaustion. 85°F and below:  Muscle rigidity.  Unconsciousness.

Comatose state.

Faint vital signs.

Faint or impalpable pulse.

Breathing possibly too shallow to observe.

Irrationality.

Incoherence.

Loss of contact with the environment.

## Environmental Injuries: Prevention
3-61. Prevent hypothermia by using the buddy system to watch each other for symptoms and consume adequate amounts of liquids, rest, and eat properly.

## Environmental Injuries: Avoidance
3-62. Hypothermia can be avoided by dressing in layers, which permits easy additions or removal to prevent overheating, becoming too cold, or getting wet or windblown. If Soldiers are in a situation that precludes staying warm and dry, they should seek shelter. Eating sugary foods and physical activity helps to produce body heat.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-63. Treatment methods vary based on the severity of the hypothermia. Mild cases—if Soldiers show symptoms of hypothermia, prevent additional heat loss by getting them into a shelter, if available; removing wet clothing; and replacing it with dry, insulated clothing. If they are capable, feed Soldiers fast energy food and hot liquids and increase their physical activity level to generate heat. If they are unable to move, insulate them from the ground, and consider directing warm Soldiers to share a sleeping bag (cover head) with injured Soldiers to transfer body heat. This method of warming can compromise warmer Soldiers’ core temperatures as well if they are not sufficiently fed, warm, and healthy. Make a diagnosis (for example, by obtaining a rectal temperature). Rehydrate the Soldier with warm liquids, sweets, and food. If the tactical situation allows, build a fire. Above all else, keep Soldiers conscious until their vital signs are normal, and seek medical assistance.

3-64. Severe cases—if the victim is unconscious or appears dead without any obvious injury, prevent further heat loss. Avoid rapidly rewarming an unconscious victim, which can create problems. Evacuating as soon as possible is preferable. At all times, the victim should be handled gently so as not to cause the cold blood from the extremities to rush to the heart. Immediately transport the victim to the nearest medical facility.

Field reheating is not effective and may be hazardous. Provide artificial respiration if breathing stops. If no pulse is detectable, be aware that in hypothermia there is often effective circulation for the victim’s hypothermic state. In such a case, cardiac compression (such as CPR) may be fatal. The exception is acute hypothermia with near drowning.

3-65. Use the following treatment methods based on the severity of the victim’s hyperthermia:

Breathing warm, moist air is the fastest way to warm the inside of the body. If breathing steam is not possible, place tubing under the rescuer’s shirt so the victim will still breathe warm, moist air.

This process can be done while on the move. In addition to breathing moist, warm air, the victim must be gradually rewarmed using external heat sources. Padded hot water bottles or heated stones should be placed in the armpits.

If conscious, the victim can be given warm, sugary drinks.

- All bodily systems in hypothermia are brittle so treat the victim gently. While making these attempts, try to evacuate the victim. Severe complications may arise as the body temperature rises, which may result in cardiac arrest even though the victim seems to be doing well.

## Environmental Injuries: Immersion or Trench Foot
3-66. The circulatory and nervous systems of the feet are damaged from prolonged exposure to cold and wet at above freezing temperatures. This can happen wearing boots or not. A Soldier may not feel symptoms until the injury has already begun.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-67. Factors that contribute to immersion or trench foot are—

Stepping into water that comes over the boot tops.

- Not changing socks often enough.

Improper hygiene.

Prolonged exposure (3 to 5 days).

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-68. Symptoms of immersion or trench foot include the sensation of tingling, numbness, and then pain. The toes are pale and feel cold and stiff. The skin is wet and soggy with the color turning from red to bright red, progressing to pale and mottled, and then grayish blue. As symptoms progress and damage appear, the skin becomes red and then bluish or black. Swelling may occur. Because the early stages of trench foot are not painful, Soldiers must be constantly aware to prevent it.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-69. To prevent this condition, keep the feet dry and clean. Change socks often, drying the insides of boots, massaging the feet, and using foot powder. Drying the feet for 24 hours usually heals mild cases. Moderate cases usually heal within 3 to 5 days. The feet should be handled gently―not rubbed or massaged. They should be cleaned with soap and water, dried, elevated, and exposed to room temperature. Victims must stay off their feet and seek medical attention. Severe cases, when feet are not allowed to dry, are evacuated as a litter casualty.

## Environmental Injuries: Chilblain
3-70. Chilblain (also known as pernio or kibe) is a superficial cold injury typically occurring after approximately 1 to 5 hours in cold-wet conditions, at temperatures below 50°F. A discolored rash or small red spots may appear on the skin, most often on the fingers but can commonly occur on the ears, face, and exposed skin. The lesions are swollen, tender, itchy, and painful. Upon rewarming, the skin becomes

inflamed, red, and hot to the touch, and swollen with an itching or burning sensation that may continue for several hours after exposure.

## Environmental Injuries: Frostbite
3-71. Frostbite is the freezing or crystallization of living tissues due to heat being lost faster than it can be replaced by blood circulation or from direct exposure to extreme cold or high winds. Exposure time can be minutes or instantaneous. The extremities are usually the first to be affected. Damp hands and feet may freeze quickly since moisture conducts heat away from the body and destroys the insulating value of clothing. Heat loss is compounded with intense cold and inactivity. Frostbite can be prevented with properly maintained and used clothing and equipment. The extent of frostbite depends on temperature and duration of exposure.

Frostbite is one of the major nonfatal cold-weather injuries encountered in military operations but does not occur above an ambient temperature of 32°F.

## Environmental Injuries: Categories of Frostbite
3-72. Superficial (mild) frostbite involves only the skin. The layer immediately below usually appears white to grayish with the surface feeling hard, but the underlying tissue is soft. Deep (severe) frostbite extends beyond the first layer of skin and may include the bone. Discoloration continues from gray to black, and the texture becomes hard as the tissue freezes deeper. This condition requires immediate evacuation to a medical facility.

## Environmental Injuries: Contributing Factors
3-73. Factors that contribute to frostbite are—

Below-freezing temperatures.

Skin contact with super cooled metals or liquids.

- Dehydration.

- Use of caffeine, tobacco, or alcohol.

- Neglect.

## Environmental Injuries: Symptoms
3-74. Symptoms of frostbite vary and may include a cold feeling, pain, burning, numbness, and, in the final stages, a false sense of warmth. The skin first turns red, then pale. The skin may be bluish in color and then may appear frosty or waxy white. The skin may feel hard, be immovable over the joints and bony prominences, or be frozen. Identifying deep versus superficial frostbite is difficult to determine and often requires 3 to 7 days after rewarming for medical personnel to diagnose. Blisters, swelling, and pain may occur after thawing.

## Environmental Injuries: Treatment
3-75. Using the buddy system is one of the primary ways to prevent frostbite. Buddies must watch each other for symptoms of frostbite and provide mutual aid if frostbite occurs. Frostbite should be identified early with prompt first aid care applied to prevent further damage:

Treat early signs of frostbite by rewarming with skin-to-skin contact or by sheltering the body part under the clothing next to the body. Do this immediately. If tissues have frozen, evacuate the victim before they thaw. If the feet are involved, evacuate the victim as a litter patient.

Thawing of a frostbitten victim is a hospital procedure. If the victim has frostbite with frozen extremities, protect the frozen parts and evacuate as a litter patient.

If frostbite is not recognized before it thaws, do not let the area re-freeze since this causes more damage. The most often affected body parts are the hands, fingers, toes, feet, ears, chin, and nose.

If evacuating victims as a litter case is not possible and the body part has not yet thawed, have the victims walk out on their own. Walking out on frozen feet is better than having them thaw and refreeze. Self-evacuation may be tactically necessary. Walking on frozen feet does less harm than walking on thawed feet.

If reheating is inevitable, do not overheat the affected body parts near flame; the warming temperature should not be greater than normal body temperature. Do not rub the parts; the crystallized tissues may break internally and cause more damage. Do not pop blisters; cover them with a dry, sterile dressing. Keep the victim warm (apply loose, bulky bandages to separate toes and fingers).

- Once a part is rewarmed, it will become painful. Pain may be managed with narcotic analgesics.

- Once the foot is rewarmed, it will swell and putting the boot back on will not be possible.

## Environmental Injuries: SECTION II – ALTITUDE EFFECTS, INJURIES, AND ACCLIMATIZATION
3-76. As altitude increases, the overall atmospheric pressure decreases. Decreased pressure is the underlying source of altitude illnesses. Whether at sea level or 20,000 feet, the surrounding atmosphere has the same percentage of oxygen. As pressure decreases, the body has a much more difficult time passing oxygen from the lungs to the red blood cells and thus to body tissues. This lower pressure means lower oxygen levels in the blood and increased carbon dioxide levels.

## Environmental Injuries: ACCLIMATIZATION
3-77. When the unit arrives at an operation area, all personnel require a period of conditioning and acclimatization. The schedule should allow for longer and more frequent periods of rest. The rigors of establishing an assembly area exhaust most personnel who are not acclimated. Water, food, and rest should be considered priorities, ensuring enough while individual metabolisms and bodies become accustomed to functioning at higher elevations. Since the acclimatization process cannot be shortened, and the absence of acclimatization hampers the successful execution of operations, planning for deployments to higher elevations should consider the following:

- Above 8,000 feet, a unit should ascend at a rate of 1,000 to 2,000 feet per day. Units can leapfrog,

taking extended rest periods.

- Units should not resort to using pharmaceutical pretreatment with carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, such as acetazolamide. These drugs have side effects that mimic the signs and symptoms of AMS. Inexperienced medics may have difficulty recognizing the differences between the side effects of the drug and a condition that could possibly be life threatening. Additionally, these drugs are diuretics, which results in higher hydration levels (at least 25-percent increase per Soldier per day). These higher hydration levels create a larger logistical demand on the unit by requiring more water, more time to acquire water, more water purification supplies, and, if in a winter environment, more fuels for melting snow and ice for water. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, such as acetazolamide, are effective in treating mild and severe AMS. These drugs should accompany attached medical personnel because they can treat the Soldier suffering from AMS and, although rest may be required, evacuation may not be needed.

- Do not move troops directly to high altitudes even if allowances can be made for inactivity for the first 3 to 5 days before mission commitment. Moving troops directly to high altitude can increase the probability of altitude sickness. Even if inactivity follows deployment, the incidence of altitude sickness is more likely than with a gradual ascent.

## Environmental Injuries: ACUTE MOUNTAIN SICKNESS
3-78. AMS is the body’s response to physical distress from difficulty adjusting to lower oxygen levels at high altitude. AMS is a temporary illness that can affect any Soldier, regardless of mountaineering experience. Soldiers are subject to this sickness at altitudes as low as 5,000 feet. Incidence and severity increase with altitude and when quickly transported to high altitudes. Disability and ineffectiveness can occur in 50 to 80 percent of the troops who are rapidly brought to altitudes above 10,000 feet. At lower altitudes, or where ascent to altitudes is gradual, most personnel can complete assignments with moderate effectiveness and little discomfort.

3-79. Personnel arriving at moderate elevations (5,000 to 8,000 feet) usually feel well for the first few hours; a feeling of exhilaration or well-being is typical. There may be an initial awareness of breathlessness upon exertion and a need for frequent pauses to rest. Irregular breathing can occur, mainly during sleep; these changes may cause apprehension. Severe symptoms—such as nausea, sluggishness, fatigue, headache,

dizziness, insomnia, depression, uncaring attitude, rapid and labored breathing, weakness, and loss of appetite—may begin 4 to 12 hours after arriving at higher altitudes.

3-80. A headache is the most noticeable symptom of AMS and may be severe. Even when a headache is not present, some loss of appetite and a decrease in tolerance for food occurs. Nausea, even without food intake, occurs and leads to less food intake. Vomiting may occur and contribute to dehydration. Despite fatigue, personnel are unable to sleep. The symptoms usually develop and peak by the second day. They gradually subside over the next several days so that the total course of AMS may extend from 5 to 7 days. In some instances, the headache may become incapacitating, and the Soldier should be evacuated to a lower elevation.

3-81. Treatment for AMS includes the following:

- Oral pain medications, such as ibuprofen or aspirin.

- Movement to lower altitudes (at least 1,000 feet lower) to alleviate symptoms, which provides for a

Rest.

Frequent consumption of liquids and light foods in small amounts.

more gradual acclimatization.

Realization of physical limitations and slow progression.

Practice of deep-breathing exercises.

- Use of acetazolamide in the first 24 hours for mild to moderate cases.

3-82. AMS is nonfatal, although if left untreated or further ascent is attempted, development of high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) or high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) can be seen. Severe persistence of symptoms may identify Soldiers who acclimatize poorly and, thus, are more prone to other types of mountain sickness.

## Environmental Injuries: HIGH-ALTITUDE PULMONARY EDEMA
3-83. HAPE is a swelling and filling of the lungs with fluid, caused by rapid ascent. HAPE occurs at high altitudes and limits the oxygen supply to the body. HAPE—

- Occurs under conditions of low-oxygen pressure, is encountered at high elevations (over 8,000 feet)

and can occur in healthy Soldiers.

Can cause death. Incidence and severity increase with altitude. Except for acclimatization to altitude, no known factors indicate resistance or immunity. Few cases have been reported after 10 days at high altitudes. For Soldiers who remain at the same altitude, the incidence of HAPE is less frequent than that of AMS. No common indicator dictates how a Soldier will react from one exposure to another.

## Environmental Injuries: CONTRIBUTING FACTORS
3-84. The following factors are associated with the development of HAPE:

- A history of HAPE.

- A rapid or abrupt transition to high altitudes.

- Anxiety.

Strenuous physical exertion.

Exposure to cold.

## Environmental Injuries: SYMPTOMS
3-85. The following symptoms are associated with the development of HAPE:

Progressive dry coughing with frothy white or pink sputum (this is usually a later sign) and then coughing up blood.

Cyanosis—a blue color to the face, hands, and feet.

- An increased ill feeling, labored breathing, dizziness, fainting, repeated clearing of the throat, and

development of a cough.

Respiratory difficulty, which may be sudden, accompanied by choking and rapid deterioration.

Progressive shortness of breath, rapid heartbeat (pulse 120 to 160 beats per minute), and coughing (compared to others who arrived at the same time to that altitude).

Crackling, cellophane-like noises (known as rales) in the lungs caused by fluid buildup. A stethoscope is usually needed to hear them.

- Unconsciousness, if left untreated. Bubbles form in the nose and mouth, and death results.

## Environmental Injuries: PREVENTION
3-86. HAPE is prevented by good nutrition, hydration, and gradual ascent to altitude (no more than 1,000 to 2,000 feet per day to an area of sleep). A rest day, with no gain in altitude or heavy physical exertion, is planned for every 3,000 feet of altitude gained. If Soldiers develop symptoms despite precautions, immediate descent is mandatory to receive necessary treatment, rest, warmth, and oxygen. They should be quickly evacuated to lower altitudes. A descent of 300 meters may help; do not delay manual descent to await air evacuation. If untreated, HAPE may become irreversible and cause death. Cases that are recognized early and treated promptly may expect to recover with no aftereffects. Soldiers who have had previous attacks of HAPE are prone to second attacks.

## Environmental Injuries: TREATMENT
3-87. Treat HAPE with—

Immediate descent (2,000 to 3,000 feet minimum), if possible. If not, then treat it in a hyperbaric chamber.

Rest (litter evacuation).

Supplemental oxygen, if available.

## Environmental Injuries: HIGH-ALTITUDE CEREBRAL EDEMA
3-88. HACE is the accumulation of fluid in the brain, which results in swelling and a decrease in brain function that may result in death. HACE is caused by a rapid ascent to altitude without progressive acclimatization. Preventing HACE is the same as for HAPE. HAPE and HACE may occur in experienced, well-acclimated mountaineers without warning or obvious predisposing conditions. Both types of edemas can be fatal. When the first symptoms occur, immediate descent is mandatory.

## Environmental Injuries: CONTRIBUTING FACTORS
3-89. The following factors are associated with the development of HACE:

Rapid ascent to heights over 8,000 feet.

- Aggravation by overexertion.

## Environmental Injuries: SYMPTOMS
3-90. Symptoms include mild personality changes, paralysis, stupor, convulsions, coma, inability to concentrate, headaches, vomiting, decrease in urination, and lack of coordination. The main symptom of HACE is a severe headache. Another manifestation of HACE is a headache combined with any other physical or psychological disturbances. Headaches may be accompanied by a loss of coordination, confusion, hallucinations, and unconsciousness. These may be combined with symptoms of HAPE. Victims are often mistakenly left alone since others may think they are only irritable or temperamental; no one should ever be ignored. The symptoms may rapidly progress to death. Prompt descent to a lower altitude is vital.

## Environmental Injuries: PREVENTION
3-91. HACE is prevented by good nutrition, hydration, and gradual ascent to altitude (no more than 1,000 to 2,000 feet per day to an area of sleep). A rest day, with no gain in altitude or heavy physical exertion, is planned for every 3,000 feet of altitude gained. If a Soldier develops symptoms despite precautions, immediate descent is mandatory to receive prompt treatment, rest, warmth, and oxygen. They should be quickly evacuated to lower altitudes. A descent of 300 meters may help; manual descent is not delayed awaiting air evacuation. If untreated, HACE may become irreversible and cause death. Cases that are recognized early and treated promptly may expect to recover with no aftereffects. Soldiers who have had previous attacks of HACE are prone to second attacks.

## Environmental Injuries: TREATMENT
3-92. Treat HACE with—

Supplemental oxygen.

Rapid descent and medical attention.

- Use of a hyperbaric chamber if descent is delayed.
