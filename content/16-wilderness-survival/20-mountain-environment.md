## Mountain Environment
**Chapter 1**

Around the world, mountain ranges form natural borders and boundaries. Historically, they have formed the edge of human settlement and wilderness; today, mountains still define many national borders and conflict regions. Mountain environments are demanding and tough, with conditions that interfere with all warfighting functions. To fight in the complex terrain of mountainous regions, military mountaineers must become familiar with the significant challenges that this environment presents. This rugged terrain will prove to be an overwhelming challenge to the untrained and inexperienced Soldier. Military mountaineers must understand how mountain environment characteristics affect them, the mission, their equipment, and the enemy during military operations. Rough terrain slows movements and canalizes troops into narrow corridors. Steep slopes make vehicle traffic possible only on roads that require extensive engineering and are vulnerable to attack and natural hazards, such as rockslides and avalanches. Harsh weather limits air resupply and close air support. Radio communications can only be maintained by securing exposed ridge tops. Key terrain often requires significant effort to secure and an even larger commitment to sustain. To succeed in this environment, Soldiers must be prepared mentally and physically.

## Mountain Environment: SECTION I – MOUNTAIN TERRAIN
1-1. Mountains exist all over the world and their character is defined by their climate and geology. Regardless of their differences, a common feature of all mountains is rugged terrain. This rugged, broken environment presents additional challenges to movement and survival to those experienced in most combat environments.

Operations in the mountains require Soldiers to be physically fit and leaders to be experienced in this terrain. (See figure 1-1, page 2.) Problems arise in moving men and transporting loads up and down steep and varied terrain. Operational success is contingent on our ability to understand the environment, recognize hazards, and anticipate problems.

## Mountain Environment: DEFINITION: TYPES AND FORMATIONS OF MOUNTAINS
1-2. Mountains are landforms that rise more than 500 meters above the surrounding plain and are characterized by steep slopes. They may rise abruptly from the plains to form a giant barrier or ascend gradually as a series of parallel ridges extending unbroken for great distances. Cliffs and precipices may be near vertical or even overhanging. Mountains may have isolated peaks, rounded crests, eroded ridges, and high plains. They may be cut by valleys, gorges, and deep ravines. High rocky crags with glaciated peaks and year-round snow-cover exist in mountain ranges at most latitudes along the western portion of the Americas, in Europe, and in Asia.

## Mountain Environment: MOUNTAIN BUILDING
1-3. Understanding the processes and the conditions under which mountains form helps us to understand their structures and hazards. The two primary mechanisms for mountain building are volcanic and tectonic activity. Volcanoes are constructed from lava and ash, which begin within the earth as magma. Tectonic activity causes plates to collide, heaving up fold mountains, or to pull apart and crack, forming fault-block mountain ranges.

## Mountain Environment: TYPICAL MOUNTAIN STRUCTURES
1-4. The massive slabs composing the outer layer of the earth are called tectonic plates. Their movement relative to each other creates earthquakes, volcanoes, ocean trenches, and mountain ridge systems. The different horizontal and vertical stresses that create mountains usually produce complex patterns. Each type of stress produces a typical structure, and most mountains can be described in terms of the following structures:

- Dome mountains—a simple, upward bulge of the crust forms dome mountains. They are usually the result of the upward movement of magma and the folding of the rock layers overhead. Erosion may strip away the overlying layers, exposing the central igneous core. Fault-block mountains—faulting, or cracking of the crust into large chunks, often accompanies upwarp, a broad low arching of the surface, which results in fault-block mountains. The motion of chunks along these faults creates various forms of mountains. Fold mountains—tectonic forces, in which continental plates collide or ride over each other, have given rise to the most common mountain form: fold mountains. When erosion strips down the overburden of rock from folded mountain ranges, the oldest, central core is all that remains.

- Volcanic mountains—along convergent plate boundaries, volcanic activity increases. As volcanic activity is forced underneath an overriding neighbor, continental crust melts and turns to magma within the mantle. Since it is less dense than the surrounding material, it rises and erupts to form volcanoes. Complex mountains—most ranges are complex mountains with portions that have been subject to several processes. These processes occur at both the macro and the micro level. One massive fold can make an entire mountain peak; however, there are folds measured by rope length, and tiny folds found within a handhold.

## Mountain Environment: EROSION AND GLACIATION
1-5. Even as mountain massifs are being pushed up, they begin to crumble and wear away. The two main processes that shape mountains are erosion and glaciation.

1-6. Erosion is when precipitation scours soil and loose rock from the steep slopes of mountains. Cycles of freeze-thaw exert tremendous force in cracks and fissures in the rock. These forces break apart softer rock and wash the fragments away down creeks and drainages, forming many of the gullies and smaller terrain features on the mountains.

1-7. Glaciation is when glaciers are rivers of ice and rocks that slowly move down mountains. They form when the rate of snowfall exceeds the rate of melting in summer months. After accumulations over hundreds of years, the snow compresses into ice that can range from ten to several hundred feet thick. Glaciers can be small and only cover a portion of a mountain, or they can be massive with a series of glaciers covering a mountain range. They are a powerful force for carving and moving rock and form many of the large features we observe in the mountains.

## Mountain Environment: TERRAIN FEATURES AND TERMS
1-8. Common geologic terminology includes—

Summit, peak—the highest point.

Spire—a tall, narrow rock formation, resembling a steeple.

Ridge—a line of high ground with height variations along its crest.

- Valley—low ground bordered on the sides by higher ground. A valley may or may not contain a

stream course. A valley generally has maneuver room within its confines.

- Hanging valley—a valley whose lower end is high on a sheer wall of a larger valley into which it flows. Saddle—a dip or low point along the crest of a ridge. A saddle is not necessarily the lower ground between two hilltops; it may be a break along an otherwise level ridge crest. Pass—any route from one valley over higher ground to another valley. Usually, a relatively low point along a ridge. Many regional synonyms exist to describe pass, such as the word notch in New England. Col—the low point on a ridge joining two peaks. Glaciologists reserve this term for gaps of glacial origin, but others use it much more generally. Cirque—a bowl-shaped valley high on a mountain, usually of glacial origin. Also referred to as simply a bowl.

- Draw—like a valley, except that it normally is a less developed stream course. The ground slopes

upward on each side and toward the head of the draw.

- Gully—a channel caused by erosion, especially by water running down a slope. A gully is generally

narrower and steeper than a draw.

Couloir—a narrow snow- or ice-filled gully.

Spur—a usually short, continuously sloping line of higher ground, normally jutting out from the side of a ridge.

- Arête—a narrow, rocky spur.

Buttress—a prominent feature that juts out from a rock or mountain, like the buttress that supports the roof of a cathedral.

- Gendarme—a steep-sided tower along a ridge, guarding or blocking the way.

Shoulder—a lateral protrusion on a mountain, or a point on the mountain where the slope changes, forming a convex shape.

- Headwall—vertical or near-vertical section of slope at the uphill end (head) of a valley, ravine, or

cirque.

Cliff—a vertical or near-vertical slope.

Ledge—a narrow, flat spot along an otherwise vertical face.

Snow field—a field of perennial snow.

## Mountain Environment: SECTION II – MOUNTAIN TERRAIN COMPOSITION
1-9. Mountain terrain composition can substantially affect the speed and safety of movement during military operations. An understanding of compositional factors is important to determine appropriate movement techniques, appropriate protective techniques, and realistic estimations of movement time.

## Mountain Environment: VEGETATION
1-10. Surface composition is highly variable in mountainous terrain. Slopes can be steep, broken, rocky, thickly vegetated, and ice or snow covered. Any of these conditions can have a significant impact on military operations, and often more than one of these conditions are found concurrently.

## Mountain Environment: CLIMATE
1-11. Major mountain ranges force air masses and storm systems to drop significant amounts of rain and snow on the windward side of the range. As air masses pass over mountains, the leeward (opposite of the windward side) slopes receive far less precipitation than the windward slopes. It is common for the climate on the windward side of a mountain range to be humid and the climate on the leeward side to be arid. This

phenomenon affects both coastal and inland mountains. As a result, vegetation and forest characteristics are different between these areas.

## Mountain Environment: ARID REGIONS
1-12. In areas receiving little rainfall, there may be little vegetation. Much of the terrain is composed of hard ground, which is firmly compacted, rocky soil. Any existing vegetation is minimal and provides minimal protection from the elements.

## Mountain Environment: DENSE FOREST
1-13. In most temperate mountain ranges, the lower elevations are covered in dense forest. These areas can provide good concealment and protection from weather. Steep slopes may require greater levels of fitness and more time to move through than woodlands in flat terrain, but the tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTP) for moving and fighting remain the same.

## Mountain Environment: THICK BRUSH
1-14. Near tree line, high winds, ice buildup, and poor soil cause the trees to grow in dense mats. The branches may grow together so tightly that Soldiers may have to walk above or crawl below the tangle of branches.

Natural breaks, such as old rockslides, creek beds, and existing trails may provide corridors for movement.

Concealment is excellent as the outline is broken well, but any movement can be easily visible.

Small-diameter spruce and fir trees provide limited cover from small arms fire.

## Mountain Environment: GRASSY SLOPES
1-15. Higher in the mountains, soil depth and quality deteriorate, and conditions become too harsh for trees.

Slopes with enough soil are often covered with grass tussocks and low shrubs. Passing above tree line, the first major change in hazards occurs. Without trees and vegetation, there is the possibility of long, tumbling falls that cannot be arrested. Grassy slopes are slippery after rain, new snow, and dew. After long, dry spells, clumps of slope tend to become fragile and break away. From a tactical standpoint, moving above tree line changes the exposure of our elements. Open areas can be observed from long distances. Limited cover means large elements can be decisively engaged in open areas by relatively small elements in advantageous terrain or by indirect fires directed by observation posts. Tactical maneuver above tree line favors small elements, using terrain masking and surprise.

## Mountain Environment: ROCK
1-16. Steep slopes have few features that hold soil. Where soil is washed away, there is only exposed rock.

Rock comes in many forms and is classified by origin and mineral composition:

Igneous rocks—igneous rocks form from cooled magma. The two main categories are intrusive and extrusive:

Plutonic (intrusive) rocks—slow crystallization from deeply buried magmas generally means good climbing, since the minerals formed are relatively large and interwoven into a solid matrix.

Weathering develops protrusions of resistant minerals, which make for either a rough-surfaced rock with excellent friction or, if the resistant crystals are much larger than the surrounding matrix, a surface with numerous knobby holds.

 Granite—granite is a very hard rock and provides good climbing. It produces less rockfall than softer rock, but when rock breaks away, it may be as large boulders. After a rain, granite dries quickly. Most protection is found in cracks. Face climbing can be found; however, it is not easily protected.

 Volcanic (extrusive) rocks—explosive eruptions eject molten rock so quickly into the air that it hardens into loose aerated masses of fine crystals and uncrystallized glass (obsidian). When this ash consolidates while molten or after cooling, it is called tuff—a weak rock that breaks down quickly and erodes easily. Quieter eruptions, from which widespread lava flows from large fissures, produce basalt. Basaltic rocks are fine-grained and often sharp-edged.

Sedimentary rocks—sedimentary rocks are born high in the mountains, where erosion grinds down debris and moves it down to rivers for transportation to its final deposition in valleys, lakes, or oceans. As sediments accumulate, the bottom layers are solidified by pressure and by mineral

cements precipitated from percolating groundwater. Gravel and boulders are transformed into conglomerates, sandy beaches into sandstone, beds of mud into mudstone or shale, and shell beds and coral reefs into limestone or dolomite. Although, in general, sedimentary rocks are much more friable than those cooled from molten magmas, pressure and cementing often produce solid rocks.

Chalk and limestone—chalk and limestone are slippery when wet. Limestone is usually solid; however, conglomerate-type stones may be loose. Limestone has pockets, face climbing, and cracks.

Sandstone—sandstone is usually soft, causing handholds and footholds to break away under pressure. Chocks placed in sandstone may or may not hold. Sandstone should be allowed to dry for a couple of days after rain before climbing on it; wet sandstone is very soft. Most climbs follow a crack. Face climbing is possible, but any outward pull breaks handholds and footholds.

Sandstone is usually difficult to protect.

- Metamorphic rocks—these are igneous or sedimentary rocks that have been altered physically or chemically by the tremendous heat and pressures within the earth. After sediments are solidified, high heat and pressure can cause their minerals to recrystallize. Shale changes to slate or schist, sandstone and conglomerate into quartzite, and limestone to marble. These changes may be minimal, only slightly altering the sediments, or extensive enough to produce gneiss, which is almost indistinguishable from igneous rock. Higher degrees of metamorphism or metamorphism of the right rocks provide a solid climbing surface. Examples are slate and gneiss, which can be firm and brittle in the same area (red coloring indicates brittle areas). Rock fall danger is high, and small rocks may break off when pulled or when pitons are placed.

## Mountain Environment: MOVEMENT ON ROCK
1-17. As softer rock and materials are eroded, the remaining rock formations are often steeper and more prominent than the surrounding surfaces. These cliffs, buttresses, and rocky arêtes often form terrain features that can mask maneuver, and their weaknesses form the unlikely avenues of approach used for infiltration and attacks. Moving over rock is risky and the consequences of falling can be high. A fall often cannot be arrested and leads to death or injury. The movement skills for negotiating steep rock faces and the technical skills for protecting that movement comprise a major portion of mountaineering training.

## Mountain Environment: SCREE
1-18. Scree refers to small rocks, from pebble size to dinner-plate size. It accumulates in areas abundant with weak, brittle rock. These small stones do not settle into stable slopes. Climbing scree is inefficient because it is unstable, with each step sliding down as weight is placed upon it. Firmer footing may be found around the edge of rock outcroppings. Scree can be descended quickly by sliding on one’s feet but should be done so under control.

## Mountain Environment: TALUS
1-19. Talus refers to rocks larger than a dinner plate but smaller than boulders. They can be used as steppingstones to ascend or descend a slope. The stability of talus can vary. Time, exposure to weather, snow, and foot traffic all help to shift rocks into positions where they are more secure and dislodge insecure rocks.

More recent rockfall is often less stable, and it is more likely that large rocks will be displaced as one moves through it. Rockfall can be a hazard, but the most prevalent hazard is loose footing, which causes lower orthopedic injuries. Ascending talus is more efficient than ascending scree and requires less energy.

## Mountain Environment: BOULDERS
1-20. Boulders are rocks that are larger than can be carried. They indicate good rock quality in the surrounding cliffs. In general, boulders form into relatively stable fields where Soldiers may need to hop from boulder to boulder and can do so without dislodging them. Boulders can be in precarious positions and can be catastrophically destructive if they roll. The large spaces between boulders and their solid nature can provide effective cover.

## Mountain Environment: MANAGING ROCKFALL
1-21. Blocks and scree at the base of a cliff can indicate recurring rockfall. Light-colored spots on the rock wall above may indicate the impact of falling rock or areas where large portions of rock have recently

detached. Avoiding boulder fields, talus, and cliffs that show extensive signs of rockfall is the best way to avoid the threat of rockfall. Gullies and couloirs, while offering breaks in steeper terrain, also act as funnels for falling rock or ice. When traveling suspect areas, alert the group of the hazard and choose a path that does not place Soldiers in a position below others who may dislodge rocks. In small groups, keeping close together prevents falling rock from generating enough force to cause harm. At other times, the best measure may be to use sheltered stances, exposing only one or two Soldiers to the hazard at a time. During thaws in the spring and early summer, timing may be the best tool. Avoid traveling in gullies and other areas prone to rockfall during the heat of the day, when rocks may be shifting positions.

## Mountain Environment: RIVERS
1-22. Mountains are home to several water sources, including freshwater springs, lakes, and streams.

Mountain streambeds may be dry or contain a significant amount of water. Steep contours make for fast moving water; small streams can be dangerous to cross. Water level fluctuates rapidly with storm runoff or snowmelt. Flash floods are possible after heavy rain. The likelihood of a flash flood after heavy rain can be greater during spring snowmelt. A storm that produces heavy rain higher on the mountain could produce a flash flood lower on the mountain. It is important to be aware of this risk and plan accordingly.

## Mountain Environment: SNOW
1-23. In most of the mountain ranges on earth above 5,000 feet, snowfall potential exists year-round.

## Mountain Environment: TYPES OF SNOW
1-24. There are many different types of snow with their own unique characteristics. Each type presents various challenges during military operations.

## Mountain Environment: New Snow
1-25. New snow makes easy terrain slick and dangerous. Maneuvering elements leave tracks that show their routes and relative size. It makes roads impassable for units that cannot clear them of snow and with vehicles that are not equipped with tire chains for traction. The distance for foot movements is significantly reduced for troops without snowshoes, skis, and over-the-snow vehicles. Movements are slowed even for properly trained and equipped troops. Steep, snow-covered terrain presents the risk of avalanches.

## Mountain Environment: Firm Spring Snow (Firm Snow)
1-26. As snow goes through cycles of warming and cooling, it settles into a denser snowpack. A snowpack is the entire mass of snow covering a given area of terrain. Dense, old snow can provide a uniform surface that covers obstacles. The character of the snow can vary greatly with temperature and sun exposure. Snow is generally firm during the night and early morning, providing efficient travel but an increased risk of long sliding falls. The snow softens with warmer temperatures and direct sunlight.

## Mountain Environment: Soft Snow
1-27. Soft snow can be easier to negotiate without stiff boots and crampons, and the fall hazard is reduced.

However, if it is too soft, travel becomes more difficult. Movement on steeper slopes requires an ice axe, crampons, and the necessary training with this equipment. (See chapter 6, section II.)

## Mountain Environment: ICE
1-28. Ice forms in a few different ways as described below. Any movement on ice demands crampons. (See chapter 6, section II.)

- Alpine ice forms in steep, sheltered couloirs and on glaciers, where firm snow consolidates into a mass of ice. It forms in a plane at the angle of the slope. Even at moderate angles, it can be physically demanding due to the lack of stances for rest and the hardness of the ice.

- Waterfall ice forms when ambient temperatures are cold enough to freeze water quickly and water flows or seeps over cliffs and exposed rock faces. Waterfall ice can form much steeper flows than

alpine ice, often with freestanding vertical columns or overhangs where drips freeze quickly.

However, waterfall ice also forms bulges and steps that allow stances to rest and place protection.

- Verglas is a thin sheet of ice that forms when rain or dripping water freezes on colder rocks. Verglas

is too thin to climb like ice and makes conventional rock climbing impossible.

## Mountain Environment: GLACIERS
1-29. When enough ice accumulates in the peaks, it begins to flow down the mountain. Valleys in the high mountains may be buried under massive glaciers and present additional hazards, such as hidden crevasses and icefalls. Dismounted movement across glaciers is hazardous; however, glaciers often provide the easiest passage through the more technical terrain on surrounding slopes and faces. (See chapter 14 for information on glacial travel.)

## Mountain Environment: SECTION III – TERRAIN ANALYSIS
1-30. Terrain analysis is critical to every decision and task when planning an operation in mountainous terrain. Substantial experience moving and living in a wide variety of terrain is essential to effective terrain analysis. Military mountaineers can use the standard Army acronym for observation and fields of fire, avenues of approach, key terrain, obstacles, and cover and concealment (OAKOC) as a guide during terrain analysis.

## Mountain Environment: OBSERVATION AND FIELDS OF FIRE
1-31. Observation in mountains varies extensively due to weather and ground cover. The dominating height of mountainous terrain and sparse vegetation generally permits excellent long-range observation.

Well-placed elements in the high ground can typically observe and affect much of the terrain. However, rapidly changing weather with frequent periods of high winds, rain, snow, sleet, hail, and fog can limit visibility. The rugged nature of the terrain often produces dead space at midranges.

1-32. Low cloud cover at higher elevations may neutralize the effectiveness of operations established on peaks or mountaintops. High wind speeds and sound often mask the noises of troop movement. Several operations may need to be established laterally, in-depth, and at varying altitudes to provide visual coverage of the battle area.

1-33. Conversely, the nature of the terrain can provide concealment from observation. This concealment can be obtained in the dead space. Mountainous regions are subject to intense shadowing effects when the sun is low in relatively clear skies. The high contrast from light to shaded areas considerably reduces visual sharpness within shaded regions. These shadowed areas can provide increased concealment when combined with other camouflage, and they should be considered in maneuver plans.

1-34. Fields of fire, like observation, are excellent at long ranges. However, dead space is a problem at short ranges. Rocky, broken terrain favors dispersed teams that can cover the many approaches to their position.

Range determination is deceptive in mountainous terrain. Soldiers must routinely train in range estimation in mountainous regions to maintain their proficiency.

## Mountain Environment: AVENUES OF APPROACH
1-35. The rugged nature of terrain creates many natural obstacles. Maneuver elements are constrained to the few movement corridors for which they have the appropriate training and equipment to negotiate. Mountains usually favor the defense. Units in dominating terrain can ward off large attacks easily. However, the difficulty of sustaining forces in the high ground, their exposure to bad weather, as well as the many approaches created by the rugged terrain means defending forces cannot cover all avenues of approach. They focus their limited combat power on the approaches from which they expect an attack. Historically, large attacks along easy approaches in the mountains have failed. Attacks are more likely to succeed by using troops that can exploit unexpected avenues of approach, with adequate reconnaissance, rehearsals, and the element of surprise.

## Mountain Environment: KEY AND DECISIVE TERRAIN
1-36. Mountain warfare is a fight to control roads, and the lines of communication (LOCs) located in valleys.

Controlling them enables the movement of road-bound critical supplies and heavy combat assets, such as tanks that must transit through the mountains to other fronts of the battlefield. The decisive terrain for control of these LOCs is often the passes. Here, the valleys narrow and rise, limiting the maneuver of armor and mechanized units. The terrain canalizes these forces into terrain dominated by the surrounding heights. Here, these assets experience degradation of their maneuver and firepower capability, making them vulnerable to attacks from mobile and light mountain infantry forces. The unit that holds this decisive terrain can protect its own heavy assets as it moves through mountainous terrain and destroys enemy assets attempting to take the same route.

1-37. The high ground offers a tactical advantage to whoever holds it. In the valley, cover shrinks when engaged from an elevated position. On the heights, even relatively small cover gives protection from direct fires from the valley and using ridge crests allows units to maneuver while masked by terrain. However, the tactical advantage of holding high ground is also resource intensive. Occupying and then sustaining mountain positions require a significant commitment of support that may make long-term occupation difficult or impossible.

## Mountain Environment: OBSTACLES
1-38. Mountain terrain is defined by its rugged character. Cliff bands, steep gullies full of loose rock, and other natural obstacles exist throughout the terrain. All can be used to slow or canalize opposing forces into decisive engagement areas. In difficult terrain, fewer resources may be needed to improve obstacles.

## Mountain Environment: COVER AND CONCEALMENT
1-39. When moving in the mountains, outcroppings, boulders, heavy vegetation, and intermediate terrain can provide cover and concealment. The digging of fighting positions and temporary fortifications is difficult because soil is often thin or stony. Selecting dug-in positions require detailed planning. Some rock types, such as volcanic tuff, are easily excavated. In other areas, boulders and other loose rocks can be used for building hasty fortifications. In alpine environments, snow and ice blocks may be cut and stacked to supplement dug-in positions.

1-40. Above tree line, lack of vegetation significantly reduces concealment. Movement requires careful terrain analysis and route planning, using terrain to mask movement from enemy observation. Hide sites and defensive positions built in locations with good observation angles are often exposed; they require careful camouflaging.

## Mountain Environment: MOUNTAIN CLASSIFICATIONS
1-41. The military uses two systems to classify mountain terrain: operational terrain levels and dismounted mobility classifications. Operational terrain levels describe the tactical advantages and disadvantages based on their difficulty to access and secure. The dismounted mobility classification describes the individual mobility skills required to move safely and effectively through that terrain. These classifications are crucial tools for planners and leaders. They enable the appropriate terrain analysis and help to facilitate planning feasible and realistic operations in mountain environments.

## Mountain Environment: OPERATIONAL TERRAIN LEVELS
1-42. Level I terrain is located at the bottom of valleys and along the main LOCs see table 1-1 and figure 1-2, page 10. These areas typically contain resources, population centers, or mobility corridors that are vital to mission success. Opening, blocking, or controlling these regions and LOCs is therefore always a tactical objective in mountain combat operations. At this level, maneuver space is restricted, but armored forces can operate effectively along valley roads and trails. Infantry and armored forces are typically task-organized together at this level to ensure freedom of movement and protection along LOCs. While armor provides mobility and firepower within the confines of the valley floor, infantry and other high mobility units secure adjacent high ground. Units operating in level I terrain must consider how higher terrain—particularly

level II—can influence their mission, including exposure to enemy observation, fires, and maneuver from above.

1-43. Level II terrain consists of the highlands that overlook and influence level I terrain. Level II terrain is often found between valley floors and the higher mountain peaks. Units occupy level II terrain because it provides a tactical advantage through observation, fires, and maneuver over the valleys below. In mountain operations, control of level II terrain, rather than the actual summit, often determines key terrain. However, units positioned in level II must also account for how higher level III terrain may allow enemy forces to observe, flank, or otherwise influence their position. Infrastructure in level II terrain is typically sparse or nonexistent. If present, roadways may include narrow roads or trails that follow ridgelines or cross saddles.

Such roadways often serve as secondary LOCs.

1-44. Level III terrain includes the dominant terrain of summit regions. These areas typically do not allow forces to directly influence level I terrain, but they may dominate or affect operations in level II. Operations in level III terrain are challenging due to extreme environmental exposure, limited infrastructure, and separation from adjacent units and logistical support. However, level III terrain can provide acclimatized troops with advanced mountaineering training with opportunities to support forces in level I and II terrain.

Such supporting activities include flanking enemy positions, infiltrating along unexpected avenues of approach, and striking key vulnerabilities such as LOCs, logistics nodes, air defense sites, or command infrastructure.

## Mountain Environment: DISMOUNTED MOBILITY CLASSIFICATION
1-45. Most mountainous terrain severely restricts mobility. The dismounted mobility classification is a tool that rates the terrain based upon difficulty of movement and consequence of a fall and provides general guidelines on risk management. It uses the same categories as the Yosemite Decimal System (YDS), which is used to rate climbing in the United States. It is used across all types of mountainous terrain and defined as a rough framework for communicating the appropriate risk treatment and required technical systems and resources for negotiating the terrain. It is subjective, and risk treatment will vary based upon the group, surface conditions, and weather.

1-46. 1st-class terrain is defined as gentle slopes or easy trails. This terrain is negotiated with simple walking techniques. This can be anything from established trails to cross-country movement over terrain with stable footing and moderate grades. Mobility challenges in this terrain may include dense vegetation, stream crossings, and challenging route planning. Risks are limited to navigation errors and hazards posed by stream-crossings, weather, and altitude. (See table 1-2.)

1-47. 2nd-class terrain involves off-trail scrambling in steeper, more rugged terrain, often with extensive scree, talus, or boulder fields. Hands are often used for balance. Good physical conditioning is required, but no technical solutions are needed for safe movement. Route-finding skills are even more important for efficient and safe travel across or around terrain that is more difficult, vegetation, stream-crossings, and rockfall hazards. The risk of injury from slips and rockfall is moderate, but generally, the likelihood and consequence of a fall is low. Moving a casualty will generally require simple rope systems. (See table 1-2.)

1-48. 3rd-class terrain requires scrambling. Hand and footholds are numerous and easy to find, requiring limited climbing experience. Movement may be easy, but falls can cause serious injury. Advanced route-finding skills are necessary to avoid difficult and dangerous terrain. Experienced mountaineers will usually travel unroped. Using fixed ropes or belays are required for Soldiers moving during limited visibility, bad weather, or when carrying heavy loads. All Soldiers moving in 3rd-class terrain should have training in individual mobility, good physical fitness, and proficiency with rope systems used. Route finding and risk decisions should be made by someone with formal mountaineering training. (See table 1-2.)

1-49. 4th-class terrain requires moderate to difficult scrambling. Holds are numerous but must be tested and are frequently smaller and more difficult to find than in 3rd-class terrain. The terrain is often broken, with short, steep steps of less than 15 meters, interspersed with blocky ledges. It is often exposed. Serious injury or death can occur if an unroped fall occurs. Climbing should be protected by belays or fixed ropes. Advanced military mountaineers are required to find routes and provide belays or fix ropes for less experienced troops where necessary. It requires significant time and resources to manage risk for large elements. It is more efficiently traveled by small teams. 4th-class terrain is the most difficult terrain in which to evaluate and manage risk. (See table 1-2.)

1-50. 5th-class terrain is climbing terrain. Significant injury or death can occur from an unroped fall.

Climbing is protected by belay techniques and intermediate protection. Steep rock often requires that anchors be built with rock gear. Advanced military mountaineers are trained to lead climbs to negotiate this terrain.

Large groups can descend through bands of 5th-class terrain more quickly by rappelling. Ascent requires more time and equipment, as well as proficiency in rope ascending across the entire element. (See table 1-2.)

1-51. Operations conducted in 1st- and 2nd-class terrain require little to no mountaineering skills. Special teams may require some training and ropes for moving casualties. Operations in 3rd-, 4th-, and 5th-class terrain require a higher level of mountaineering skills throughout the element for safe and efficient movement. Elements moving through terrain obstacles are easily fixed. Operations moving through exposed terrain should have plans for maintaining security and for contingencies during the movement.

## Mountain Environment: SECTION IV – MOUNTAIN WEATHER
1-52. Weather presents its own hazards in the mountains. It also increases the hazards posed by terrain. On routes with snow or glacial hazard, variations in temperature and cloud cover can significantly affect speed and safety. Strong winds can make exposed ridges unclimbable. Rain, in addition to causing discomfort and potentially hypothermia, makes rock slick and increases the likelihood of a fall. Rain washing through debris in gullies increases the danger of rockfall. Summer thunderstorms move quickly and hammer exposed ridges and peaks with lightning.

1-53. Bad weather increases hazard and physical discomfort. Civilian mountaineers plan climbs with “weather windows” to allow flexibility. Military mountaineers often have less flexibility in plans and timelines. Some weather hazards can be managed by more conservative terrain choices, additional cold-weather clothing and equipment, and training focused on producing self-reliant Soldiers who can endure poor conditions. Plans should be flexible with multiple layers of redundancy for sustainment. Bad weather may force early resupply or the adjustment of operations to accommodate the lack of ground and aerial resupply.

## Mountain Environment: MOUNTAIN WEATHER FACTORS
1-54. Weather analysis is the collection, processing, evaluation, and interpretation of relevant military aspects of weather. It is the evaluation of forecasted weather effects on operations. The military aspect of weather— visibility, wind velocity, precipitation, cloud cover, temperature, humidity, and atmospheric pressure—and their effects on operations remain the same as in other environments. However, elevation and terrain with significant relief create effects unique to the mountain environment. Weather in the mountains follows larger patterns, but local factors cause weather systems to produce more precipitation and make the effects of storms and instabilities greater and longer lasting. (See table 1-3, page 14.) (See ATP 2-01.3 for more information on weather analysis.)

## Mountain Environment: ATMOSPHERIC PRESSURE, TEMPERATURE, HUMIDITY
1-55. Changes in atmospheric pressure are the underlying cause of many factors. Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the atmosphere in an area. As elevation increases, pressure is reduced, causing lower air density.

Aircraft capabilities and human performance decrease. Sunlight is less filtered, causing quicker warming and cooling.

1-56. Reduced pressure also affects temperature. Air temperature decreases as elevation increases in the mountains at the adiabatic rate or about 3 degrees (°) Fahrenheit (F) to 5 °F for every 1,000 feet of elevation gain. Cooler air holds less moisture. As air masses pass over mountains, they are lifted by the terrain—a process known as orographic lift. If the air cools to its saturation temperature, its water vapor condenses and becomes a visible cloud. Lenticular clouds, named for their lens shape, are cloud caps that often form above peaks because of orographic lift and precipitates such as fog, rain, or snow. This process increases the severity and length of storms and produces unique localized weather.

1-57. Mountain air is incredibly dry. Dehydration is more likely in the mountains if Soldiers are not properly hydrated. (See chapter 3 for more information.)

## Mountain Environment: VISIBILITY, CLOUD COVER, AND PRECIPITATION
1-58. On clear days, high elevations provide opportunities to see great distances. However, minor atmospheric disturbances produce fog, clouds, and precipitation that reduce visibility. Weather systems that would not influence operations in the lowlands often do in the mountains. Clouds are common in mountain environments and may form quickly. Precipitation occurs more often on the windward side of mountain

ranges than on the leeward side. Snow is common in the mountains and may occur during any season at elevations above 1,500 meters (5,000 feet).

1-59. Local thunderstorms often pass quickly but bring driving snow and sudden wind squalls. Lightning is common in mountain storms and normally discharges on high points and prominent features. Peaks, ridges, rock walls, isolated trees, fixed wire installations, cracks that guide water, cracks filled with earth, shallow depressions, shallow overhangs, and rock needles should be avoided; shelter should be obtained around dry, clean rock without cracks.

1-60. Storms caused by widespread atmospheric disturbances often involve strong winds and heavy precipitation. These conditions may last several days longer than in the lowlands. Low temperatures, high winds, and blinding snow often accompany winter storms in alpine regions.

## Mountain Environment: WIND VELOCITY
1-61. Mountains more frequently create variation in wind than in most other environments. Valleys often protect Soldiers from strong winds, but the ridges and passes of mountains are seldom calm. Wind velocity generally increases with altitude and is intensified by mountainous terrain. Wind speed increases when winds are forced over ridges and peaks or when funneled through narrowing mountain valleys, passes, and canyons. (This is known as the Venturi effect.) Exposed mountainsides and summits are especially susceptible to strong winds. Topography usually controls local wind direction.

1-62. When the wind speed is doubled, the force on an object buffeted by the wind is quadrupled; therefore, bivouacs or patrol bases should be established in areas not subject to extreme winds. Colder air temperatures along with wind produce wind chill. Soldiers in a static security posture in these conditions are more likely to experience cold-weather injuries. (See chapter 3 for cold-weather injuries.)

1-63. Warming and cooling cycles cause local air movements. Valley breezes moving up a slope are more common in the morning and are known as anabatic winds; descending mountain breezes known as “katabatic winds” are more common in the evening.

1-64. Mountain weather can be a dangerous obstacle to operations or a valuable aid, depending on how well it is understood and to what extent advantage is taken of its characteristics.

1-65. Weather often determines the success or failure of a mission. Military operations plans must be flexible.

The clouds that often cover mountaintops and fog that covers valleys are excellent tools for concealing movements normally made during darkness or in smoke. Limited visibility can be used as a combat multiplier.

1-66. The safety of most high mountain regions, especially in winter, depends upon the change of a few degrees of temperature above or below the freezing point. Ease and speed of travel depend mainly on the weather. Terrain that can be crossed swiftly and safely one day may become impassable or dangerous the next due to snowfall, rainfall, or warmer temperatures; the reverse can happen just as quickly. The prevalence of avalanches depends on terrain, snow conditions, and weather factors.

1-67. Weather affects Soldiers’ abilities to see enemies, terrain, and teammates, as well as their ability to move through the terrain. Access to weather information during the planning process drives tactical decisions.

Whether patrolling or static, military mountaineers should constantly evaluate the following:

- What is the weather currently?

- What was the weather recently and when did it change last?

- When is the next forecasted change coming?

- How does it affect us?

## Mountain Environment: WEATHER FORECASTING
1-68. Reports from other localities and from any weather service, including the Air Force, Navy, or the National Weather Service, are helpful. Weather at various elevations may be quite different because cloud

height, temperature, and barometric pressure will all be different. There may be overcast and rain in a lower area, with mountains rising above the low overcast into warmer clear weather. To be effective, a forecast must reach the small-unit leaders who are expected to utilize weather conditions for missions. Normally located within a chief of staff intelligence office or battalion or brigade intelligence office and designated as a special staff officer, the U.S. Air Force staff weather officer provides and coordinates weather support and services. (See AR 115-10 and AR 5-25 for additional guidance.)

1-69. Weather patterns form at the continental scale and involve many factors. Individuals on the ground cannot create weather forecasts from locally observed conditions and it is unrealistic to expect them to do so.

It is critical that weather data produced at higher levels is pushed down to forces on the ground. However, individuals on the ground must recognize that there is inherent uncertainty in all forecasts, and local factors may produce unexpected conditions. Paying attention to changing trends can prevent Soldiers from being unprepared for changing conditions. Many of the trends that indicate changing weather are easily observed.

1-70. Monitoring changes in barometric pressure provides one of the reliable indicators of changing weather.

High-pressure systems bring cooler, drier weather. As air pressure rises, it prevents moisture from rising and forming in the atmosphere, allowing for clearer skies and cooler temperatures. Lower air systems bring warmer, wetter weather and atmospheric instability. As air pressure falls, it allows moisture to rise and condense, allowing clouds to form.

1-71. Pay attention to key indicators of changes. Analog altimeters measure barometric pressure and can be used to identify an increase and decrease in pressure when stationary for a prolonged period. (See chapter 7 for information on altimeters.)

## Mountain Environment: DETERIORATING TRENDS
1-72. Military mountaineers must pay careful attention to indicators of deteriorating weather trends. The following are common indicators:

- Marked wind direction shifts. A high-pressure system wind flows clockwise. A low-pressure system wind flows counterclockwise. The closer the isometric lines are, the greater the differential of pressure and the greater the wind speeds.

Changes in obstructions to visibility.

Increasing cloud coverage.

Increase in precipitation. A steady drizzle is usually a long-lasting rain.

Lowering cloud ceilings.

- Marked wind speed increases.

- Marked cooler temperature changes, which could indicate that a cold front is passing through.

- Marked increase in humidity.

- Decrease in barometric pressure indicates a lower-pressure system is moving through the area. This

can be measured using a digital or analog altimeter.

## Mountain Environment: IMPROVING TRENDS
1-73. Predicting coming favorable weather conditions is important during operations planning. The following are common indicators of favorable weather trends:

Steady wind direction, which indicates no change in weather systems in the area.

Clearing of obstructions to visibility.

- Decreasing wind speeds.

- Decreasing or ending precipitation.

- Decreasing cloud coverage.

- Decreasing humidity.

Increasing height of cloud ceilings.

Changing temperature slowly gets warmer.

Increasing barometric pressure indicates that a higher-pressure system is moving through the area.

This can be measured using a digital or analog altimeter.

## Mountain Environment: CLOUDS
1-74. At the boundaries of major weather systems where different air masses collide, warm air is lifted into the atmosphere, causing clouds and precipitation. Fronts create distinctive cloud formations. Warm fronts, where the incoming warm air gradually displaces cold air below, present as steadily thickening and lowering clouds over a few days. Cold fronts create an uplift that is more violent and may come as a single, larger storm system.

## Mountain Environment: High-Level Clouds
1-75. High-level clouds (above 6,000 meters) are formed of ice crystals, with a fibrous structure and blurred outlines. The sky is often covered with a thin veil that partly obscures the sun or, at night, produces a ring of light around the moon. Cirrus clouds indicate moisture aloft and the approach of a traveling storm system.

Precipitation is often 24 to 36 hours away. As a storm approaches, the high-level clouds thicken and lower.

## Mountain Environment: Mid-Level Clouds
1-76. Mid-level clouds (2,000 to 6,000 meters) appear more distinct than high-level clouds because of a mixed composition of water droplets and ice. Mid-level clouds often form in advance of a cold front. Puffy, mid-level clouds on a warm, humid summer morning are commonly followed by thunderstorms later in the day. “Fair weather” cumulus are clouds scattered rather than even in a blue sky and suggest arrival of high pressure and clear skies.

## Mountain Environment: Low-Level Clouds
1-77. Low-level clouds (below 2,000 meters) are mostly composed of water droplets. Most precipitation originates from low-level clouds because rain or snow usually evaporates before reaching the ground from higher clouds. Low-level clouds usually indicate impending precipitation.

## Mountain Environment: Vertical Development (Cumulonimbus)
1-78. Large, vertical development clouds often form on the steep, leading edge of a cold front. They can develop as individual towers or form a line of towers called a squall line. Their formation is fueled by vigorous convective updrafts. These storms tend to develop during the afternoon and early evening when the effects of heating from the sun are the strongest.
