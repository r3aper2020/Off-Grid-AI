## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment
**Chapter 4**

Units receiving mountain training show a marked advantage in harsh mountain environments than those that do not. Normal activities, such as navigating, communicating, and moving, require specialized techniques. To gain confidence and experience, Soldiers must conduct realistic mountain training. They should be expected to perform under any environmental conditions. Extended training exercises test the sustainment mechanisms in place for the unit and expose the Soldier to the isolation common to mountain operations. Training should reflect the harsh mountain environment and should focus on the following:

Fitness and nutrition.

- Ability to survive and operate in a cold-weather environment.

- Resupply methods.

- Bivouac considerations.

Soldier load management.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SECTION I – PHYSICAL TRAITS OF THE MILITARY MOUNTAINEER
4-1. Operations in mountainous terrain are extremely taxing on the human body. The combined effects of harsh weather, difficult dismounted movements, and elevation require Soldiers to be in superior physical condition. The more adapted Soldiers are to the conditions in which they fight, the greater chance they have of success in executing their mission. To adapt to the mountain environment, Soldiers must train in similar environmental conditions and simulate the physical workload of operations in mountainous terrain.

Simulating the stresses of mountain warfare on the Soldier’s body in a training environment allows necessary adaptations to be made.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: PHYSICAL TRAINING FOR THE MOUNTAIN ENVIRONMENT
4-2. Physical fitness training considerations can be broken down into two categories:

Fitness aspects, which are important to Soldiers’ abilities to perform in mountainous terrain.

Fitness training programs that should guide commanders in developing their unit’s capability to perform effectively in mountainous terrain.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: INDIVIDUAL FITNESS CONSIDERATIONS
4-3. Operating in the mountains demands a higher level of fitness from the individual Soldier. Even basic movement techniques require a higher level of exertion and as the terrain becomes increasingly technical, so too does the physical demand. This; coupled with increased loads, adverse environmental conditions, and altitude effects; can render a Soldier with substandard fitness ineffective. The mountain Soldier must possess the capacity to reach the objective with the energy to fight while moving safely through hazardous terrain.

4-4. A Soldier must demonstrate proficiency in many facets of fitness to prepare for mountain operations.

Soldiers must have a holistic approach to their workout programming and selection. They should not focus entirely on one aspect of fitness. Mountain Soldiers must train for their job with a level of dedication like that of professional athletes training to compete. There are many similarities between mountain Soldiers and athletes, but there are key differences as well. Athletes train to compete in a known sport, governed by rules.

Mountain Soldiers may be faced with unknowns and have a less defined concept of how they will need to perform during mountain operations. These differences drive the well-rounded, holistic approach to fitness required for Soldiers who may fight in mountainous terrain.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Aerobic Capacity
4-5. Aerobic capacity allows the mountain Soldier to move for long periods and helps determine potential capacity of endurance for long movements. Aerobic capacity refers to the Soldier’s ability to use oxygen to create energy for extended periods. Dismounted movements and the carrying of a casualty in mountainous terrain are examples of tasks requiring endurance. Training in a low-intensity, high-volume manner conditions the aerobic energy system and develops the necessary levels of endurance. FM 7-22, table 6-2, covers training such as ability group runs, terrain runs, foot marches, and endurance training machines to help increase aerobic endurance for unit members.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Anaerobic Capacity
4-6. Anaerobic capacity allows a Soldier to sprint or have high levels of exertion for a short period. Little oxygen is needed for this type of energy production. The anaerobic energy system can create large amounts of force for a short period. Mountain Soldiers require a high level of anaerobic capacity to move under fire and perform tasks that require a short burst of energy. Training in a high-intensity, low-volume manner conditions the anaerobic energy system and provides the necessary levels of explosive energy. FM 7-22, table 6-2, covers training such as 30:60s, hill repeats, and shuttle runs to help condition the anaerobic energy system of unit members.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Resistance Training
4-7. Resistance training increases the performance of the muscle tissue found in Soldiers’ bodies and increases bone density. Properly trained muscle tissue protects Soldiers’ joints during mountain operations, leading to greater durability in this harsh environment. Resistance training can affect the body in different ways. Mountain Soldiers must use resistance training correctly. Soldiers who operate in mountainous terrain should avoid excess bulking of their muscle tissue as this decreases their endurance capabilities. Soldiers do not need large muscles to have strength; adequate strength can be developed using resistance training to gain lean muscle mass. Strength is primarily a function of the brain’s connection to the muscles (neuromuscular pathways), not the size of the muscles. Soldiers should use resistance training to increase the function and durability of the body, not aesthetics. This training should be designed to increase muscular endurance, lean muscle mass, and strength. A master fitness trainer can design training programs to help individual Soldiers and commanders achieve their desired results. FM 7-22, table 6-2, covers training, such as the climbing drill, push-up and sit-up drill, and the strength and conditioning circuit, to guide Soldiers through types of resistance training.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: UNIT FITNESS CONSIDERATIONS
4-8. The commander should develop a conditioning or training program to bring a unit to a level where it can operate successfully in mountain conditions. Training priorities should be established. As with all military operations, training is a major influence on the success of mountain operations. Fitness is the foundation upon which all other mountain skills are built. Proper levels of fitness increase the lethality and durability of Soldiers operating in mountainous terrain.

4-9. A physical training program for operations in mountainous terrain should not be taken lightly. This type of program is time-consuming and requires buy-in from all levels of the deploying unit’s chain of command.

Exercise equipment that allows for proper training should be obtained. Prior to conducting this training, Soldiers should assess their fitness levels. This assessment is an important tool for commanders and master fitness trainers to understand what is needed in the exercise program. Once an exercise program is generated, a unit may begin to train. The program should focus on increasing Soldiers’ physical abilities to perform their job in a tactical and mountainous environment.

4-10. U.S. forces do not routinely train in mountainous terrain. Therefore, extensive preparations are needed to ensure individual and unit effectiveness. Units should be conditioned physically and psychologically before undertaking rigorous mountain operations. Units must work well as a team to cope with the terrain, environment, and enemy situation. Certain factors should be considered:

- What are the climatic and terrain conditions of the area of operations?

- How much time is available for conditioning and training?

- What additional personnel will accompany the unit? Will they be available for training and

conditioning?

- What is the unit’s current level of physical fitness?

- Does the unit have master fitness trainers creating exercise programs specific to the mountain

environment?

Is there an exercise facility that will allow Soldiers to physically train themselves using the master fitness trainer’s program?

- What is the level of individual expertise in mountaineering?

- What type of operations can be expected?

- Which areas in the United States most closely resemble the area of operations?

- Are predeployment areas and ranges available?

- Does the unit have trainers with experience in mountain warfare?

4-11. Physical capability is not solely gained during 1 hour per day in the gym. The participating Soldier must have the discipline to adhere to proper nutrition planning and get adequate rest. To maximize recovery during this period, Soldiers should sleep 8 to 9 hours during a 24-hour period to minimize the chance of injury and increase performance gains.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: NUTRITION FOR THE MOUNTAIN ENVIRONMENT
4-12. The food Soldiers eat directly correlates to the energy levels they expend, including their ability to recover from strenuous activity as well as many other factors. An understanding of how different types of nutrients affect the body will help the Soldier eat better. Soldiers participating in an intense physical training program should not diminish their gains from the training with poor nutrition. When physical training and proper nutrition are combined, Soldiers have more success in achieving and sustaining the fitness required for operations in mountainous terrain.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: HYDRATION IN THE MOUNTAIN ENVIRONMENT
4-13. Water is vital to the health and performance of the mountain Soldier. Dehydration decreases cognitive and physical performance. Soldiers commonly face conditions, such as cold weather and high elevations, in mountainous terrain. Both elements affect the body’s ability to stay properly hydrated. A cold climate decreases the sensation of thirst and causes cold-weather diuresis (excessive urination). These both decrease fluid levels in the body and can lead to a performance-limiting state of dehydration. High elevation combined with cold temperatures can further dehydrate a Soldier. Less oxygen leads to an increased respiration rate.

While breathing, Soldiers exhale water vapor. Rapidly breathing dry air decreases fluid in the body.

4-14. Soldiers and leaders should ensure the availability and consumption of fluids. Hydrating before, during, and after periods of exertion allows for adequate levels of performance and acclimatization. Other factors necessary for maintaining fluid balance in the body include consuming appropriate levels of sodium, electrolytes, and other necessary nutrients. Bananas and other fruits are great examples of electrolyte rich snacks.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: MACRONUTRIENTS
4-15. Nutrients are compounds in foods essential to life that provide the body with energy and building blocks for repair and growth. Nutrients required in large amounts are called macronutrients. Proteins, fats, and carbohydrates are the three types of macronutrients that must be consumed in certain proportions to maintain a healthy body. These important nutrients allow the human body to perform and acclimate in mountainous environments.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Proteins
4-16. Protein is a macronutrient responsible for maintaining and restoring muscles, providing key enzymes, and strengthening immunity. Protein also helps preserve lean muscle mass and increase strength and endurance. Protein serves many functions that support physical activity. Protein repairs muscle after exertion and creates energy during prolonged periods of submaximal exertion. The recommended requirement is 0.8 grams of protein for every 2.2 pounds of body weight, but this can fluctuate with activity levels. Protein requires water for digestion and may facilitate dehydration. Proteins provide the body with about 4 calories of energy per gram.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Fats
4-17. Fats are critical to physical performance, recovery from exercise, and cardiovascular health. Fats are the body’s natural and most concentrated source of stored food energy. Main sources of fats are meats, nuts, butter, eggs, milk, and cheese. Fats should comprise 25 to 30 percent of the total daily caloric intake. Fats require more water and oxygen to digest than protein. Fats provide the body with approximately 9 calories of energy per gram. Fat is the most energy dense of the three macronutrients. It is harder for the body to use fat in an efficient manner to produce energy during periods of exertion. Fats are harder to digest at higher altitudes.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Carbohydrates
4-18. Carbohydrates are the chief energy source for high-intensity physical activity, and they are important for meeting overall energy needs. Carbohydrates are an important source of calories and provide the body with dietary fiber and a variety of micronutrients. Nutritionally, the most useful sources of carbohydrates are foods, such as unrefined grains, vegetables, and fruit. Carbohydrates should comprise up to 50 percent of the total daily caloric intake. Carbohydrates provide the body with around 4 calories of energy per gram.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: BALANCED DIET
4-19. Eating a balanced diet provides the energy needed to conduct daily activities and to maintain internal body processes. A balanced diet containing adequate amounts of macronutrients, vitamins, and minerals ensures an efficient metabolism. Since operations in mountainous terrain are strenuous and demand high-energy use, a balanced diet is necessary. A proper diet requires Soldiers to be aware of how many calories they must consume. Soldiers of different ages, weights, and sexes have different nutritional needs.

Caloric requirements differ between Soldiers, especially when accounting for levels of activity.

4-20. Mountain Soldiers should take the time to understand their own caloric requirements. (See figure 4-1.) Individual variables coupled with a variety of environmental conditions, cold weather, or altitudes make it difficult to generalize a Soldier’s daily requirement, and it could range from 4,500 to 6,000 calories a day.

4-21. Vitamins allow chemical reactions in the body to happen so that they may use the energy found in the three macronutrients. Without these reactions, the energy from the calories in the macronutrients cannot be turned into adenosine triphosphate that the cells in our body use for energy, allowing them to function.

Vitamins and minerals are found in vegetables, fruits, and meats. There are many different micronutrients, and it can become hard to track ingestion of all types. Physically active Soldiers should pay special attention to consume adequate amounts of iron, vitamin D, and antioxidants through a balanced diet. If a diet deficient in fresh foods containing essential vitamins and minerals is not available during a deployment, vitamin supplements are a suitable substitute.

4-22. Extra food should be carried in case resupply operations fail. Food should be lightweight, easy to digest, and be able to be eaten hot or cold. Meals, ready to eat meet these criteria and provide the basic food groups.

Commanders may consider supplementing meals, ready to eat with breakfast bars, fruits, juices, candies, cereal bars, and chocolate. Bouillon cubes can replace the required salt intake, and it can warm cold bodies and stimulate the appetite. Hot beverages of soup, juices, powdered milk, and cider should also be considered.

Since coffee, tea, and hot chocolate are diuretics, consuming these beverages should not be relied upon for hydration (see AR 40-25).

4-23. Calories from carbohydrates, protein, and fat digest, absorb, and metabolize at different rates in our body. The timing of nutrient intake will affect the individual’s physical, behavioral, and cognitive well-being.

High-energy snacks help maintain performance during long movements. Soldiers should also consume a high-carbohydrate snack to replenish energy stores and maximize recovery immediately following foot movements or hard physical work.

4-24. Certain drugs, medications, alcohol, and smoking have adverse effects on the circulation, perspiration, hydration, and judgment of Soldiers. They should be avoided, if possible, when operating in extremely cold conditions or at high altitudes.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: NUTRITION OF ELEVATION
4-25. Success in mountain operations depends on proper nutrition. Because higher altitudes affect eating habits, precautions must be taken. If possible, at least one hot meal each day should be eaten, which may require personnel to heat their individual rations. (See USARIEM TN-01/4 for more information on nutrition.)

4-26. Soldiers can experience physiological acclimatization with abrupt ascents to high altitudes. The circulatory system works to provide the needed oxygen to the body. Large meals require the digestive system to work harder than usual to digest food. Large meals may be accompanied by indigestion, shortness of breath, cramps, and illness. Therefore, relatively light meals that are high in carbohydrates are best while acclimatizing at higher elevations. Personnel should eat moderately and rest before strenuous physical activity. Since fats and protein are harder to digest, less digestive disturbances may occur if meals are eaten before resting. A diet high in carbohydrates is not as dense in energy and may require eating more often.

Consuming carbohydrates, beginning in the morning and continuing through mid-afternoon, are important in maintaining energy levels.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: ACCLIMATIZATION
4-27. The following elements are characteristic of nutritional acclimatization in mountain operations:

Loss of appetite with symptoms of AMS.

Loss of weight usually stops with acclimatization.

- Weight loss during the first 2 to 3 days at high elevation.

- At progressively higher elevations (greater than 14,000 feet), the tolerance of fatty or high-protein foods rapidly decreases. A high-carbohydrate diet may lessen the symptoms of AMS and may digest better than fat at high altitudes. (See USARIEM TN-01/4.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: FATIGUE
4-28. Increased fatigue can cause Soldiers to become disinterested in eating properly. Decreased consumption can result in malnutrition because of the unpleasant taste of cold rations. Leaders should ensure that fuel tablets and squad stoves are available or that natural flammable materials are used, if possible.

Although there is no physiological need for food to be hot, hot food does increase morale and a sense of well-being. Losing weight in the first few days can occur because of dehydration, metabolic changes, and loss of appetite. Carbohydrate-rich beverages, such as fruit juices and sports drinks, increase carbohydrates, energy, and liquid intake when the normal appetite response is blunted at altitude.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SECTION II – COLD-WEATHER SUSTAINMENT
4-29. The ability to operate for extended periods in cold weather is an essential skill if a unit wishes to be successful in mountainous terrain. Individual skills and collective unit skills required for cold weather must be practiced before deploying to a cold region of the world. The skills required for survival in the cold must be known so they do not become the sole focus once the unit is faced with harsh conditions. For a unit to

accomplish its mission in cold, unforgiving weather, it must have the equipment necessary to live and operate in extreme conditions. Cold weather can affect many facets of daily life, from nutrition to clothing.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: CLOTHING
4-30. The clothing system refers to every piece of clothing placed against the skin (base layer), the insulating layers, and the outermost garments (shell), which protect the Soldier from the elements. When clothing is worn properly, the Soldier can function better; when clothing is worn improperly, Soldiers can develop hypothermia or frostbite. Wearing cold-weather clothing provides a barrier against weather and heat retention, moves moisture away from skin, dries quickly, and allows for adequate dexterity.

4-31. Clothing for the cold-weather environment must manage Soldiers’ temperatures and control moisture.

Clothing must be able to vent and breathe so a Soldier does not become overheated and perspire excessively.

The clothing must also create insulation to shelter the Soldier from extreme temperatures. Clothing must manage moisture that comes from external sources, such as rain, or from internal sources, such as perspiration.

4-32. One of the most effective ways to manage moisture from perspiration is to set a pace that limits exertion.

In a cold-weather environment, patrols should move at a pace that will not saturate Soldiers’ insulating layers with sweat. Rest halts allow Soldiers an opportunity to adjust layers and control their own individual temperatures.

4-33. Fitness, load, body composition, and metabolism all affect individual clothing needs. There should be room for individual variation in the wear of cold-weather clothing, but clothing decisions should be based on the principles of moisture management, trapped air spaces, and protection from the elements. The components of any clothing system can be broken down into the following general categories to match those principles.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: BASE LAYER
4-34. The base layer is a thin, lightweight moisture-wicking layer worn against skin and moves moisture away from the skin to prevent heat loss from conduction. Base layers should be constructed of wool, synthetic, or silk fabrics.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: INSULATING LAYERS
4-35. Insulating layers are those layers worn over the base layer and under the shell. Insulating layers provide additional warmth by resisting the transfer of heat. Modern insulating layers allow moisture to move easily, and they trap air to increase the insulating factor. Several thin layers of insulation provide more warmth than one thick layer. Insulating layers should be constructed from wool, synthetic, or down fabrics. Insulating layers help Soldiers retain heat and not lose heat through convection.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SHELL
4-36. The shell is the outermost layer worn. The shell is the primary barrier between the elements and the body and prevents outside moisture from getting in, while allowing inside moisture out. Shells must have some ventilation capacity to prevent overheating. There are two types of shells.

4-37. A hard shell is generally more moisture and wind repellant and provides better protection against the elements at the cost of breathability. Soldiers cannot ventilate their bodies as well with a hard shell. The hard shell is a good outer layer to be worn in extreme conditions or static environments.

4-38. A soft shell is a more breathable option. The soft shell is not as moisture and wind repellent as hard shells but is more breathable. The soft shell is a good outer layer to be worn when active as it provides better ventilation and breathability.

Note. Cotton layers should NEVER BE WORN as any layer during operations in a cold environment.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: GAITERS
4-39. Gaiters protect the lower leg from snow, ice, mud, twigs, and stones. Using waterproof fabrics or other breathable materials laminated to nylon makes the gaiter an integral component of the cold-weather clothing

system. Gaiters are not presently fielded in the standard extended cold-weather clothing system and, in most cases, will need to be locally purchased. Gaiters are available in four styles:

Trail gaiters are short, typically extending just above the boot. They keep snow and debris out of boots in the spring and summer.

- Alpine gaiters extend up to the knee and keep deep snow out of boots in the winter.

Expedition gaiters are thicker, designed to fit around a larger plastic mountaineering boot, and sometimes have added built-in insulation.

The integrated gaiter is a boot with a built-in gaiter, eliminating the need for a second piece of equipment.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: HAND WEAR
4-40. Even during the summer months, temperatures in the mountains can dip below the freezing point. While mittens are warmer than gloves, the finger dexterity needed to do most tasks makes gloves the primary cold-weather hand wear.

4-41. The principles that apply to clothing also apply to gloves and mittens. A modular approach should be taken when wearing hand wear. (See figure 4-2, page 48.) They should provide moisture transfer from the skin to the outer layers―the insulating layer must insulate the hand from the cold and move moisture to the shell. The outer layer must be weather resistant and breathable. Both gloves and mittens should be required for all Soldiers during mountain operations, as well as replacement liners for both. This will provide enough flexibility to accomplish all tasks and keep the users’ hands warm and dry.

4-42. Just as the clothing system is worn in layers, gloves and mittens work best using the same principle.

Soldiers should have multiple sets of hand wear. They should have multiple thin liner gloves, intermediate climbing gloves, thicker gloves for working with carabiners in the extreme cold, trigger-finger mittens for using their personal weapon in the extreme cold, and thick mittens to protect their hands in the extreme cold.

When outer layer gloves are removed, they can be placed in the jacket to keep warm.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: HEADWEAR
4-43. Headwear in mountainous environments should take a modular approach as well. A single hat does not perform well in every condition; several hats should be available to mountaineers: a thin moisture-wicking hat to wear under the helmet or during active movements; an intermediate fleece cap for protection in cold, dry environments; and a heavy thick cap for extreme cold. For colder climates, a neck gaiter can be added.

The neck gaiter is a tube of synthetic material that fits around the neck and can reach up over the ears and nose. For extreme cold, a balaclava can be added. This covers the head, neck, and face, leaving only a slot for the eyes. Worn together, the combination is warm and provides for moisture movement, keeping the wearer drier and warmer.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: FOOTWEAR
4-44. Soldiers may encounter various environmental conditions within relatively short distances in mountainous, plateau, and other highland areas. (See CTA 50-900.) In such places, clothing allowances that are adequate for adjacent lower areas may not provide sufficient protection for diverse highland areas; therefore, in these higher or more rugged areas, commanders may authorize, at their discretion, additional clothing items and special mountaineering equipment, as required.

4-45. Mountain climates are colder than surrounding areas. Even in summer months, it may be necessary to equip troops with cold-weather boots. The intermediate cold-weather boot provides adequate protection for summer conditions in most mountain ranges with high elevation (2,500 to 4,000 meters) located in temperate

regions. It can be appropriate footwear for Soldiers whose mission only requires mobility in easy terrain. (See figure 4-3, page 50.)

4-46. Leather mountaineering boots are designed to provide more capability in mountainous terrain. They are typically warmer and more water resistant than combat boots are. Rigid leather mountaineering boots provide better edging while climbing alpine rock. They also perform better when kicking steps in firm snow or wearing crampons. In general, a leather mountaineering boot with moderately stiff soles and upper section works well for most military mountaineering tasks. Although they are not available through the supply system, they can be a commercial, off-the-shelf purchase. (See figure 4-3, page 50.)

4-47. During the winter months, most mountain ranges experience periods of extreme cold (–26°F to – 40°F).

The extreme cold-weather boot (with vapor barrier [VB]) provides adequate protection from the extreme cold, but it is not an adequate platform for mountaineering tasks in these conditions. Plastic mountaineering boots provide a more versatile platform for any condition encountered in the mountains, while keeping the foot dryer and warmer. Plastic mountaineering boots are a double boot, consisting of a hard, plastic shell, and an inner insulating boot. The stiffness of the shell makes it compatible with crampons, snowshoes, and skis. The hard, plastic shell provides waterproofing, while the inner liner remains free of snowmelt, making it an ideal boot in wet conditions. Plastic mountaineering boots are the optimal footwear for extended operations in cold mountainous environments. (See figure 4-3, page 50.)

Note. Maintenance of all types of footwear must closely follow the manufacturers’ recommendations.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SOCKS
4-48. Socks cushion the foot, remove excess moisture, and insulate from cold temperatures. Improper wear and excess moisture can cause hot spots and blisters. Socks can be worn as a layered system:

The first layer should be a moisture-wicking material that moves moisture from the foot surface to the outer sock.

The outer sock should also be made of moisture-wicking materials but should be complemented with materials that provide cushioning and abrasion resistance.

- A third layer can be added depending upon the climatic conditions:

In severe wet conditions, a waterproof-type sock can be added to reduce the amount of water that would saturate the foot. This layer would be worn over the first two layers if conditions were extremely wet.

In extremely cold conditions, a VB sock can be worn either over both original pairs of socks or between the moisture-wicking layer and the insulating layer. If the user is wearing VB boots, the VB sock is not recommended.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: EXTENDED COLD-WEATHER CLOTHING SYSTEM
4-49. The extended cold-weather clothing system is the currently issued cold-weather clothing system. (See figure 4-4.)

Level 1: Base layer—a crewneck shirt constructed with a lightweight material that is highly breathable and has moisture-wicking properties. Kit includes two sets.

Level 2: Grid fleece (waffle)—a midweight long-sleeve shirt and elastic waistband drawers constructed with a heavyweight material, which offers stretch for increased comfort during movement, is highly breathable, and has moisture-wicking properties. Kit includes one set.

Level 3: Fleece jacket—a zipper jacket with raglan sleeves constructed with a material that creates air pockets that trap air and retain body heat, providing outstanding warmth without weight. This material also offers excellent breathability and the ability to dry quickly. However, this layer is not recommended as an outer garment for use during inclement or windy weather. Kit includes one piece.

Level 4: Wind jacket—a nylon or spandex jacket. It offers wind protection and environmental comfort with low weight and bulk and is quick drying. Kit includes one piece.

Level 5: Soft-shell jacket and trousers—constructed with a waterproof fabric, is quick drying, highly durable, and breathable. Kit includes one piece.

Level 6: Wet weather jacket and trousers—constructed with a two-layer waterproof and windproof fabric with sealed seams and is extremely breathable. Kit includes one piece.

Level 7: Extended cold-weather parka and trousers—three-layer construction that is fabricated with an outer layer, an insulation layer, and an inside layer of lightweight rip-stop material. They are water resistant, highly durable, and breathable. Kit includes one piece.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SECTION III – SMALL GROUP MOUNTAIN SUSTAINMENT AND SUBSISTENCE
4-50. There is no one standardized way to plan for sustainment in mountain operations. Each mission has its own set of complications. Layers of contingencies should be built into the plan for sustainment. If one method fails due to any number of unforeseen circumstances, another method of sustainment must be ready to provide Soldiers with what they need. Gear that Soldiers carry must not be so heavy it makes them vulnerable.

Methods to procure water must be available. Detailed coordination must be made with air assets to provide aerial resupply.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SOLDIER LOAD MANAGEMENT
4-51. Operations in mountainous terrain require Soldiers to carry only mission-essential items as they move through challenging terrain. Individual Soldiers, small teams, and large units must be able to adjust their equipment. They must understand the fundamentals of cross-leveling essential equipment to avoid excess gear. Understanding and practicing these simple, yet often overlooked, skills can make a unit lighter, faster, and more lethal in mountainous terrain. No amount of training can change the body’s reaction to carrying excessive loads. Analyzing the situation and the level of risk are the keys to determining what is mission essential. All leaders should familiarize themselves with ATP 3-21.18, where they can access table 3-4, which is a helpful guide for leaders when planning loads for dismounted movements. (See chapter 6 for detailed information on load management to maximize mobility.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: CHOICE OF EQUIPMENT
4-52. Mission requirements and unit standard operating procedures (SOPs) will influence the choice of gear carried, but the following lists provide a sample of what should be considered during mission planning.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Personal Gear
4-53. Personal gear includes an emergency survival kit with signaling material, fire-starting material, and water procurement material. Pocket items should include a knife, whistle, pressure bandage, notebook with pen or pencil, sunglasses, sunblock and lip protection, map, compass, and altimeter.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Standard Gear
4-54. Standard gear that can be individually worn or carried includes cushion-soled socks; combat or mountain boots, combat uniform and cap; load-carrying equipment with canteens, magazine pouches, and first aid kit; individual weapon; a large rucksack containing hard-shell top and bottom; insulating layers; helmet; poncho; and sleeping bag.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Mountaineering Equipment and Specialized Gear
4-55. The element’s leadership should scrutinize mountaineering equipment. The equipment should allow Soldiers to move safely through any known technical terrain. Identifying hazards and obstacles during the planning phase is a crucial step to help determine the appropriate gear for the given mission. Required mountaineering equipment is never set in stone, and each patrol or mission may require different equipment.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Daypack
4-56. When Soldiers plan to be away from the bivouac site for the day on a patrol or mountaineering mission, they carry a light daypack. This pack should contain—

Extra insulating layer.

- Hard-shell layer, such as a waterproof jacket and pants or poncho.

- Necessary mountaineering equipment for technical terrain.

- Gloves.

First aid kit.

Flashlight or headlamp.

Canteen or water bottle.

Cold-weather hat or scarf.

Rations for the time away from the base camp.

Survival kit.

Improvised harness materials or climbing harness.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: TEN ESSENTIALS
4-57. Regardless of what equipment is carried, the individual military mountaineer should always carry 10 essentials when moving through the mountains.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Navigational Tools
4-58. Knowledge of the terrain and a well-developed route plan are the two most important tools for avoiding issues in the mountains. A map should be carried along with appropriate navigational aids like a compass, altimeter, and Global Positioning System (GPS) device.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Headlamp and Night Vision Devices
4-59. Night vision devices are powerful tools for target identification and discrimination and are critical tools in the mountains. However, their narrow focal plane at close ranges is minimally helpful when scrambling in rough terrain. Light enables faster and safer movement and scrambling. Preventing enemy observation should always be a primary concern, but for maneuver in terrain that is masked from observation where risk of a fall is a greater threat, a light is valuable. Headlamps are also important for camp tasks, planning, and map work.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Sunglasses and Sunscreen
4-60. In alpine or snow-covered terrain, sunglasses are vital for preventing snow blindness. Side shields, which minimize light entering from the side, permit ventilation to help prevent lenses from fogging. Each independent climbing team should carry at least one extra pair of sunglasses.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Extra Clothing
4-61. Clothing is the primary protection from the environment and needs to match the conditions and the pace of a movement. Extra clothing includes the additional layers needed for static operations or enduring the long, inactive hours of an unplanned bivouac, such as the following:

- Waterproof, breathable shells to protect from unexpected wind or precipitation.

Extra underwear to replace sweat-soaked underwear.

Extra hats or balaclavas.

Extra pair of heavy socks.

Extra pair of insulated mittens or gloves.

Extra insulation for the upper body and the legs (winter or severe mountain conditions).

4-62. To back up foul weather gear, bring a poncho or extra-large plastic trash bag. A reflective emergency space blanket can be used for hypothermia first aid and emergency shelter. Insulated foam pads prevent heat loss while sitting or lying on snow. Finally, a bivouac sack can help by protecting insulating layers from the weather, cutting the wind, and trapping essential body heat.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Water and Water Containers
4-63. These include wide-mouthed water bottles for water collection, hands-free backpack hydration systems, and a small length of plastic tubing for water procurement at snowmelt seeps and rainwater puddles on bare rock. Wide-mouth containers are easy to fill, easy to clean, and easy to clear out if ice forms.

Hydration bladders are convenient, but they can break and have freezing issues in the tube.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: First Aid Kit
4-64. Small teams in the mountains often operate independently. In addition to the improved first aid kit, each Soldier should carry first-aid supplies to address common mountaineering injuries, such as punctures and abrasions with severe bleeding, broken bones, serious sprains, and blisters. Splinting material for stabilizing orthopedic injuries is an important addition.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Lighter and Fire Starter
4-65. Lighters are handy for starting fires, but they should be backed up by matches stored in a waterproof container with a strip of sandpaper. Fire-starting material is key to igniting wet wood for emergency campfires. Candles, heat tabs, and canned heat all work. These can also be used to quickly warm water or soup in a canteen cup. In alpine zones above the tree line with no available firewood, a stove works as an emergency heat source.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Knife
4-66. A small folding knife is a helpful tool for its weight. It can be useful for cutting rope or cordage or for food preparation and shelter construction. A knife is important for first aid when improvising interventions with limited medical supplies. A multipurpose pocket tool is also helpful.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Repair Kit
4-67. A repair kit should include— Stove tools and spare parts.

- Duct tape. Patches. Safety pins.

- Heavy-duty thread or dental floss.

Sewing kit.

Cord or wire.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Extra Food
4-68. Soldiers should carry one day’s extra worth of food in case of delay caused by bad weather, injury, or navigational error.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SMALL UNIT CROSS-LEVELING
4-69. A small team of Soldiers must analytically look at the packing and choice of team gear. Everyone must be aware of essential-item locations. They must also ensure there is no unnecessarily redundant equipment.

In challenging terrain, unnecessary weight slows Soldiers and reduces situational awareness.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SQUAD OR TEAM EMERGENCY PACK
4-70. When a squad-sized element leaves the bivouac site, squad safety gear should be carried in addition to individual daypacks. This can be either loaded into one rucksack or cross-leveling among squad members.

In the event of an injury, CASEVAC, or unplanned bivouac, these items may make the difference between succeeding and failing the mission. Squad safety gear comprises the following items:

Sleeping bag.

Sleeping mat.

Squad stove.

Fuel bottle.

Litter or packaging material.

4-71. The following mnemonic, DROP, can help leaders ensure an appropriate load plan is achieved:

R—Reduce redundant or nonessential equipment.

- D—Determine level of mobility.

- O—Organize resupply. P—Police Soldiers.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: WATER PROCUREMENT
4-72. Supplying adequate water to maintain hydration is a sustainment challenge. Fluids lost through respiration, perspiration, and urination must be replaced. However, water is heavy, and even a few days’

supply will overburden Soldiers. If the water supply is insufficient, then physical activity must be reduced.

However, Soldiers cannot adjust permanently to a decreased water intake.

4-73. Individual loads and support requirements can be reduced if small units can procure water from the environment. Water found in mountain streams and pools is often clean, but it cannot be assumed potable.

Soldiers should be educated to drink water only from safe sources. Emphasis must be placed on the three rules of water discipline:

- Only drink treated water.

- Do not contaminate or pollute water sources.

Conserve water for drinking. Potable water in the mountains may be in short supply.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: WATER SOURCES
4-74. Water is scarce above the timberline. After setting up a perimeter, such as a patrol base, assembly area, or a defense; a watering party should be employed. Mountain streams, springs, rain, and lakes provide good sources of water. In areas where water trickles off rocks, a small piece of tubing can be used to fill canteens, or a shallow reservoir may be dug from which to collect water (after the sediment settles).

4-75. Snow may be melted to procure water, which is a time-consuming process that requires significant fuel.

Water should be collected at midday when the sun thaws the snow. In glaciated environments, fine particulate can be found in the runoff and should be filtered. After sundown, high mountain areas freeze. Storing water next to a Soldier or placing it in a sleeping bag at night can protect water from freezing.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: BIOLOGICAL CONTAMINANTS
4-76. Virtually all surface waters are microbiologically contaminated. There are three types of disease-causing microorganisms: protozoa, bacteria, and viruses.

4-77. Protozoa are the largest of waterborne bugs measuring in the 1-micron range. Giardia and cryptosporidium are the most infamous of the group. The infective cysts and oocysts of protozoa are extremely robust and can survive for months in a water source. They thrive in domestic and wild animals, as well as humans. Cryptosporidium oocysts are known for their resistance to chemical treatment. Most disinfectants are ineffective, and the few that require long dwell times to do the job.

4-78. Bacteria are an order of magnitude smaller than protozoa measuring in the 0.2-micron range. The most well-known bacteria are Escherichia coli (commonly referred to as E. coli); others include campylobacter, salmonella, and shigella. Bacteria are not as hearty, surviving in water for weeks instead of months. However, unlike protozoa and viruses, which require a host to multiply, some bacteria grow in water and are found in higher numbers than protozoa and viruses. Both animals and humans often carry bacteria, such as protozoa; as a result, many water sources are contaminated with bacteria. Beware of water sources near agricultural operations.

4-79. Viruses—some notorious waterborne viruses are poliovirus, hepatitis, and norovirus. Waterborne viruses are very resilient and can survive for months in water. Viruses are generally species-specific; in other words, those that infect animals do not infect humans and vice versa. Because of this and the modern sanitation measures taken in developed countries, not as many water sources are contaminated with viruses.

In developing countries with little or no sewage control, the opposite is true. However, as more people are becoming active outdoors, more rivers, lakes, and streams are at risk of viral contamination.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: CHEMICAL CONTAMINANTS
4-80. Filters and purifiers provide microbiologically safe drinking water. The carbon in the filters and disinfectant of the purifiers will adsorb or react with chemical contaminates, such as herbicides, pesticides, and volatile organic compounds. However, filters or purifiers will not protect from high chemical concentrations or heavy metal contamination. It is best to avoid water sources such as mining tailing ponds or those near agricultural operations.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: WATER PURIFICATION
4-81. Water should always be treated. No matter how clean the snow or water may appear, it should always be considered contaminated. There are many techniques to purify water, each with inherent advantages and disadvantages.

4-82. Boiling kills biological contaminants but does not eliminate chemical contaminants. Filtration should also be used if chemical contaminants are suspected. To ensure all biological contaminants have been killed, let the water boil for 1 minute. This method requires time and fuel while allowing water to be treated away from the source.

4-83. Chemical treatment can be accomplished with iodine or chlorine dioxide. These treatments are available commercially and come with instructions for proper use. Chemical treatment will kill almost all biological contaminants but are ineffective against cryptosporidium. It requires time for these chemical treatments to be effective, and, in cold temperatures, it takes longer. This method allows water to be treated away from the source and on the go.

4-84. UV lights are an effective way to kill all biological contaminants but do not treat for chemical contaminants. They require batteries and in cloudy debris-filled water, it may not kill all contaminants.

Ensure water is filtered if it is cloudy before use. This method can be used away from the source.

4-85. Filters are compact and lightweight. They work by filtering out particulate, chemicals, and biological contaminants. Filters are not capable of removing any chemical contaminants that are dissolved in the water.

Certain filters may not be able to filter out smaller biological contaminants as the pores of the filter are too large to remove them. These types of filters are designed to remove waterborne protozoa and bacteria but not viruses. The benefit of these types of filters is that water moves more rapidly through them. Filters that can filter smaller contaminants are often referred to as water purifiers. Water purifiers are designed to remove protozoa, bacteria, and viruses, offering a higher level of defense by combating all three. The drawback to these types of filters is that they filter water at a slower rate and may clog when filtering very cloudy water.

Be sure to read the capabilities of the filter and treat the water with multiple methods, if necessary. Filters can freeze in cold temperatures if they are not properly cleared after use. In extreme temperatures, they may not operate at all. Filters require treating water at the source.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: AERIAL RESUPPLY
4-86. Movement of supplies by air is often the preferred method for remote, hard to reach locations. This is only possible when assets are available and the weather, terrain, and situation allow it. Air distribution in the mountains requires detailed planning, effective liaison, and a habitual relationship between the aviation crew and the ground unit. Direct communication between the unit and the air delivery asset is coordinated early in the planning process to help ensure proper delivery.

4-87. Aerial resupply, though effective, is limited by weather, which must be permissive from the point of origin to the point of delivery, and all stops in-between. Fog, cloud cover, sudden storms, ice, and unpredictable air currents can shut down air support. Higher elevations will decrease aircraft lift capabilities.

Temperature and air density at altitude restrict an aircrew’s ability to carry loads into the high mountains.

Erratic weather conditions in the mountains will frequently prevent rotary- and fixed-wing aircraft from flying; this may last for multiple days.

4-88. Suitable drop zones in the mountains are difficult to find. A common resupply delivery is accomplished by helicopters flying at a low altitude above the mountain terrain delivering unit-configured loads. Supplies should be packaged for immediate Soldier transport upon delivery. (For more information on options, see ATP 3-21.50, ATP 4-48, and supporting aviation assets.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SECTION IV – BIVOUAC CONSIDERATIONS
4-89. Mountainous regions often have strong turbulent winds, cold temperatures, and a general lack of concealment above the timberline. The wind overhead creates an extensive lee near the mountain. An inland wind blowing 50 miles per hour (mph) may not strike the ground for several kilometers after passing the edge of a cliff or a very steep slope. Such a lee is an attractive bivouac site from the standpoint of wind protection.

Note that such a lee area is often an area of maximum snow deposit. Frequent requirements to dig out vehicles, walkways, and weapons positions may offset the wind-free advantages of a lee site during snowfall or snow-blowing weather. Avalanche hazard areas must be avoided. Cold air is heavier than warm air and frequently settles in valleys. If tactically feasible, it is better to establish a bivouac up the hillside above the valley floor and below the timberline.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: BIVOUAC SITE LOCATION
4-90. The following are considerations for selecting a bivouac site in the mountains:

Cover and concealment—cover and concealment from air and ground observation is essential for the bivouac area. Forested areas pose few problems in comparison to areas above the tree line.

Operations above tree line or at altitude may offer less cover and concealment. Bivouac may need to be established below tree line to support operations conducted above tree line.

- Wind protection—protection from the wind is a prime consideration. Depending on the area of operations, bivouac site selections may vary due to the unpredictability of winds at elevation.

- Ground condition—the condition of the ground is important. In warm conditions, bivouac should be located on hard ground. In snow-covered terrain, avoid digging to the ground, as this may collect water due to the thawing of the snow, or rain. A layer of snow should be left for ground insulation, comfort, and water absorption. The depth that is dug in will vary depending on the site selection and snow depth. Construction materials—construction materials play an important part in selecting a bivouac. When reconnoitering an area, consider such things as the availability of firewood, water procurement, and snow for snow shelters.

- Hazard awareness—bivouac sites must be protected from mountain-specific hazards that are not commonly encountered, such as rockfall, icefall, flooding, and avalanche-runout zones. These hazards are not always obvious so good awareness and vigilance must be used when selecting these sites in mountainous terrain.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: GLACIER BIVOUAC PROCEDURES
4-91. When locating a bivouac site or a gathering area where the team might need or want to unrope, at least one person will need to probe the area for hidden crevasses. (See chapter 14, section II, for detailed information on probing a potential bivouac site.)

4-92. Once an area is determined to be safe, it must be marked to keep personnel within that area. Typically, wands are carried as flagging material specifically for this purpose. Water procurement sites (uphill) and latrines (downhill) must be determined for safety to avoid contamination.

4-93. Multiple tent sites can be connected, which keeps tents closer together. Probe all areas between the tents if planning to move in those areas. Closer tents will make communicating between tent groups and rope teams easier.

4-94. If there is a chance for severe storms with high winds, snow walls may be constructed to protect the tent site from wind. The walls can be constructed from loose snow piled on the perimeter, or blocks can be cut from consolidated snow layers. In deep, soft snow, digging 3 or 4 feet to find a consolidated layer will result in enough snow moved to build up decent walls around the tent site. For block construction, move the soft snow from the surface into the wall foundation areas (down to a consolidated layer of snow).

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: SHELTERS
4-95. Operations in mountainous terrain require Soldiers to be familiar with a variety of bivouac shelter construction techniques. Ideally, purpose-designed shelters should be used. However, given the erratic nature of weather in mountainous terrain, Soldiers should also be prepared to improvise survival shelters for emergency bivouac situations that occur when carrying manufactured shelters is not feasible.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: TENTS
4-96. When selecting a tent, the mission must be defined to determine the number of people the tent must accommodate. The climate should also be taken into consideration. A tent used for warmer temperatures will

greatly differ from tents used in colder, harsher environments. Tent manufacturers offer many designs of different sizes, weights, and materials. Mountaineering tents are made of breathable or weatherproof material.

A single-wall tent allows for moisture inside the tent to escape through the tent’s material. A double-wall tent has a second layer of material (referred to as a fly) that covers the tent. The fly protects against rain and snow, and the space between the fly and tent helps moisture to escape from inside. Before using a new tent, the seams should be treated with seam sealer to prevent moisture from entering through the stitching. The frame of a tent is usually made of an aluminum or carbon fiber poles. The poles are connected with an elastic cord that allows them to extend, connect, and become long and rigid. When the tent poles are secured into the tent body, they create the shape of the tent. Tents are rated by a relative strength factor; the speed of wind a tent can withstand before the frame deforms. Temperature and expected weather for the mission should be determined before choosing the tent.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: FLOORLESS SHELTERS
4-97. Floorless shelters are lighter and more packable than conventional tents. Soldiers can enter and exit the tent with boots on without bringing dirt into the tent or ripping the floor of a conventional tent. They can be pitched in many different types of terrain for many different uses. They can provide overhead cover while staying in a static position for a long period. They offer much more ventilation, allowing the use of a stove inside and no worry of spilling fuel onto the tent floor. Because floorless shelters are lightweight and easier to pack than conventional tents, it is possible to carry a much larger shelter. A tarp, poncho, or piece of house wrap makes a clean and dry floor area near the sleeping bags if the ground is damp. Floorless shelters do have some drawbacks. They are not suitable above the tree line due to the lack of anchoring and potential high winds. They need soil into which to drive the stakes. If extreme cold temperatures are encountered, they have much more ventilation and are inherently colder than a conventional tent. (See figure 4-5.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: EMERGENCY BIVOUAC SHELTERS
4-98. The equipment that a Soldier carries, and the environment determine the type of shelter that can be built. Shelters can be built in wooded areas, open country, and barren areas. Wooded areas usually provide the best location, while barren areas have only snow as building material. Wooded areas provide timber for shelter construction, wood for fire, concealment from observation, and protection from the wind.

Note. In extreme cold, do not use metal, such as an aircraft fuselage, for shelter. The metal conducts away from the shelter what little body heat the Soldier can generate.

4-99. Shelters made from ice or snow usually require tools, such as ice axes or saws. Significant time and energy must be expended to build these shelters. Be sure to ventilate an enclosed shelter, especially if the

intent is to build a fire for warmth. Always block a shelter’s entrance, if possible, to keep the heat in and keep out the wind. Use a rucksack or snow block. Construct a shelter no larger than needed. This will reduce the amount of space to heat. A common error in cold-weather shelter construction is making the shelter so large that it steals body heat rather than helping conserve it.

4-100. If possible, avoid sleeping directly on the ground. Lay down some pine boughs, grass, or other insulating material to keep the ground from absorbing body heat.

4-101. Never fall asleep without turning off the stove or lamp. Carbon monoxide poisoning can result from a fire burning in an unventilated shelter. Carbon monoxide is a great danger. It is colorless and odorless. Any time there is an open flame, it may generate carbon monoxide. Always check for proper ventilation. Even in a ventilated shelter, incomplete combustion can cause carbon monoxide poisoning. Usually, there are no symptoms. Unconsciousness and death can occur without warning. Sometimes, however, pressure at the temples, burning of the eyes, headache, pounding pulse, drowsiness, or nausea may occur. The one visible sign of carbon monoxide poisoning is a cherry red coloring in the tissues of the lips, mouth, and inside of the eyelids. Soldiers must immediately be moved to fresh air if they begin to exhibit any of these symptoms.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Poncho Lean-To
4-102. A poncho lean-to takes only a short time and minimal equipment to build. (See figure 4-6.) Material requirements are a poncho, 6 to 9 feet of rope or cord, 3 stakes about 12 inches long, and 2 trees or 2 poles that are 6 to 9 feet apart. Before selecting the trees or the location of the poles, check the wind direction to ensure that the back of the lean-to is facing into the wind.

4-103. When using the lean-to for more than one night or if rain is expected, make a center support for the lean-to. Make this support with a line. Attach one end of the line to the poncho hood and the other end to an overhanging branch. Ensure there is no slack in the line.

4-104. Another method for creating a center support is to place a stick upright under the center of the lean-to.

However, this method restricts space and movement in the shelter.

4-105. For additional protection from wind and rain, place some brush, a rucksack, or other equipment at the sides of the lean-to. To reduce heat loss to the ground, place some type of insulating material, such as leaves or pine needles, inside the lean-to. (See ATP 3-50.21 for more information.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Field Expedient Lean-To
4-106. In a wooded area that has enough natural materials, a field-expedient lean-to can be constructed with only a knife. It takes longer to make this type of shelter than it does to make other types, but it protects against the elements. It requires two trees (or upright poles) about 6 feet apart, one pole about 6 feet long and 1 inch in diameter, 5 to 8 poles about 9-feet long and 1 inch in diameter for beams; cord or vines for securing the horizontal support to the trees; and other poles, saplings, or vines to crisscross the beams. (See ATP 3-50.21.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: NATURAL SHELTERS
4-107. Do not overlook natural formations that provide shelter. Examples are caves, rocky crevices, clumps of bushes, small depressions, large rocks on leeward sides of hills, large trees with low-hanging limbs, and fallen trees with thick branches. When selecting a natural formation, stay away from low ground, such as ravines, narrow valleys, or creek beds. Low areas collect the heavy cold air at night and are therefore colder than the surrounding high ground. Thick, brushy, low ground also harbors more insects. Check for ticks, mites, scorpions, stinging ants, and venomous snakes. Look for loose rocks, dead limbs, or other natural growth that could fall on the shelter. (See ATP 3-50.21.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Snow-Cave Shelter
4-108. The snow-cave shelter is an effective shelter because of the insulating properties of snow (See figure 4-7.) A snow-cave shelter requires significant time and energy to build, and during construction, clothing can become quite wet. Find a drift about 3-meters (the length of an avalanche probe) deep that can easily be dug into. While building this shelter, keep the roof arched for strength and allow melted snow to drain down the sides. Build the sleeping platform higher than the entrance. Separate the sleeping platform from the snow-cave’s walls or dig a small trench between the platform and the wall. This platform and a smooth ceiling will prevent the melting snow from dripping on clothing and equipment. The construction is especially important to ensure an efficient means of retaining heat. Ensure the roof is high enough to sit up on the sleeping platform. Block the entrance with a snow block or rucksack. The walls and ceiling should be at least 12-inches thick. Install a ventilation shaft. If there is no drift large enough to build a snow cave, a variation can be built by piling snow into a mound large enough to dig out. (See ATP 3-50.21.)

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: Snow-Trench Shelter
4-109. The snow trench is a hasty shelter designed to allow Soldiers to sleep below the snow and wind level while using the snow’s insulating qualities. To build a snow-trench shelter, follow the following steps:

Cut snow blocks about 1 foot by 1 foot by 2 feet and construct the walls by interlocking the snow blocks with overlapping placements. The walls should be slightly higher than the tent. At a minimum, build walls on the windward side of the tent site. Snow walls can also provide shelter from wind for food preparation and when using latrines.

In areas of compacted snow, cut snow blocks and use them as overhead cover. If not, use a poncho or other material. Build only one entrance and use a snow block or rucksack as a door.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: LIGHTWEIGHT STOVES
4-110. Lightweight stoves should be used during mountain operations to reduce Soldier load and as a cold-weather emergency risk mitigation measure. They should be issued at the team level and provide 3 to 4

Soldiers with the ability to rehydrate rations, melt snow into water, heat water to warm Soldiers with cold weather or other injuries, and generate heat in small shelters during emergencies.

4-111. Soldier load can be greatly reduced by carrying lightweight stoves and fuel in winter environments.

This is because they can create far more water by volume for the same carried weight than just carrying water. The actual amount varies dependent on altitude, temperature, and fuel type.

4-112. Factors that should be considered when choosing lightweight stoves for mountain operations are weight, altitude, operational temperature, fuel availability, and reliability.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: STOVE TYPES
4-113. Most mountaineering stoves are either canister-fuel or liquid-fuel:

Canister-fuel stoves are simpler to operate than liquid fueled stoves. The gas, already pressurized, flows from the fuel canister into the burner, where it ignites, in the same manner as a domestic kitchen stove. Canister-fuel stoves are generally accepted as being more efficient for weight carried than liquid fuel. However, canister-fuel stoves are unreliable in cold temperatures. They are not recommended for use in freezing temperatures or at high altitudes.

Liquid-fuel stoves are more complex because the liquid fuel must be vaporized prior to burning. In many stoves, the priming pressure is generated by a small hand pump that forces air into the fuel container. Liquid-fuel stoves are typically capable of burning multiple types of fuel. They generally operate well in cold weather and at high altitude. Liquid fuel costs less than canister fuel. Some forms of fuel, such as kerosene or diesel, are readily available worldwide. Liquid-fuel stoves are the most widely used for mountaineering due to their wide versatility.

## Mountain Subsistence and Sustainment: STOVE USE
4-114. Stoves should be tested and maintained prior to operational use. Soldiers should be familiar with stove disassembly, cleaning, and troubleshooting prior to operational use. A repair kit and the tools needed to fix these stoves should always be packed together. The reliability of the stove has a huge impact on the success of the mission and the morale of personnel.

4-115. When in use, hot stoves must be kept away from equipment and ammunition. At higher elevations, the heating times may be doubled. To conserve fuel, stoves, fires, and fuel tablets should be protected from the wind.
