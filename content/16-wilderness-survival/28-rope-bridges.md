## Rope Bridges
**Chapter 16**

Operations in mountainous terrain often encounter obstacles, such as fast flowing water, ravines, or rock fields. Well-trained mountaineers can exploit this terrain with the use of rope bridges and high lines. These allow units to take the terrain out of the equation by using a rope suspended in the air to cross the obstacle. High lines are a type of rope bridge. The two terms are often used synonymously, and the techniques are relatively interchangeable; however, for the purposes of this training circular, we will refer to rope bridges when used for assisting personnel for river crossings and the term “high lines” to denote rope installations used to span longer or higher gaps in terrain.

## Rope Bridges: SECTION I – TECHNIQUES
16-1. A rope bridge is a rope tensioned between two anchors and suspended off the ground to assist units with crossing rivers. These are sometimes necessary in situations with swift and dangerous currents or very cold water, where keeping the majority of Soldiers dry is important.

16-2. Rope bridges can pose distinct disadvantages. Though linear in nature, rope bridge crossings in a tactical environment should be considered VDAs. This is because units establishing and using them are fixed and potentially split by the river obstacle for prolonged periods. This can put them at a significant tactical disadvantage. (See ATP 3-21.50 for more VDA-crossing considerations.)

## Rope Bridges: CONSTRUCTION
16-3. The rope bridge is constructed using a static rope. The rope is anchored on the far side of the obstacle in the same manner as described for using handlines in paragraphs 13-26 through 13-29, page 327. It is then tensioned and anchored on the near side of the obstacle. A rope bridge may be built many ways depending upon the tactical situation and area to be crossed. However, all rope bridges share common safety principles for construction and use. Rope bridges must be tensioned as low as practical on the anchor while still facilitating suspending loads and off-loading them. The creation of artificial height (covered later) can be used for rope bridges. The type and location of the tensioning system depend on terrain and situation.

## Rope Bridges: METHODS OF TENSIONING
16-4. There are multiple ways to apply tension to a rope. The method selected depends on the length of the bridge, equipment available, personnel available, and time. Regardless of the method used, the principle remains the same; apply just enough tension to facilitate ease of crossing through the terrain.

**WARNING:** Ropes must not be overtensioned. An overtensioned rope is in its weakest state, and the system can apply exponential forces during use. Critical failures can occur with overtensioned ropes.

## Rope Bridges: HAND TIGHT
16-5. The simplest and least equipment-intensive method is hand tightening the rope. Once Soldiers anchor one side of the rope, they pull the rope as tight as they can. Next, they tie off the rope using one of three methods: mule knot secured with an overhand knot (see figure 16-1), round turn around anchor and two half hitches on a bight, or a high strength tie off (tensionless hitch). (See figure 16-4, page 374.)

Note. Figure 16-2, and figures 16-3 and 16-4, page 374, illustrate the transport tightening system.

16-6. The benefit of hand tensioning the rope is that it is the fastest way to get the rope tight. The limitation is that if the rope bridge span is long, it may not be possible to apply adequate tension to the rope using this method. Adequate tension may require more personnel than units have available.

## Rope Bridges: TRANSPORT TIGHTENING SYSTEM
16-7. The transport tightening system can be used to tighten a rope bridge when additional tension is necessary. One Soldier on the near side ties a knot that is easily untied after being placed under tension (for example, an alpine butterfly or figure-eight slip knot with a half hitch to stabilize the adjustable bight [see figure 16-1]) approximately 1 to 2 meters from the near side anchor. They place a locking carabiner into the loop of the knot. At that time, Soldiers route the remainder of the rope around the near side anchor point and clip the rope into the carabiner.

![Figure 16-1. Transport tightening system](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-1.webp)

16-8. The pull team on the near side pulls the slack out of the installation rope. The knot should be close enough to the near side anchor to allow personnel to easily load the installation.

16-9. The rope then can be secured using one of the same three methods described above.

16-10. The benefit of using the transport tightening system is that the MA allows more tension to be applied to the rope with limited personnel and minimal equipment. The limitations are the increased system platform size required, reduced MA due to friction generated by rope redirects, and time of construction.

## Rope Bridges: SIMPLE 3:1 TIGHTENING SYSTEM
16-11. The simple 3:1 tightening system is another method for gaining MA (see chapter 12).

16-12. The benefits of the simple 3:1 system are increased efficiency in tensioning, the capability to tension on a smaller platform, and the ability to re-tension due to rope stretch. The drawbacks are increased complexity, time, equipment, and the possibility of overtensioning the rope. The limitations are that the system requires more equipment, is more complex to construct, is easier to overtension and the increased time required to construct.

**WARNING:** No more than four personnel should be used to tighten the rope when a simple 3:1 or a transport tightening system is used to apply tension. Using more personnel can overtighten the rope and bring the rope critically close to failure.

## Rope Bridges: REDUNDANCY
16-13. A rope bridge constructed with a single rope has the risk of critical failure due to cutting and lacks redundancy. A redundancy (in rigging) is the condition in which a rope system will not fail as the result of any single component of the system failing. If the risk or critical nature of the load requires an additional level of redundancy, a second rope bridge may be constructed parallel to the first one. Examples of critical loads are a casualty, Soldiers crossing a rope bridge that is high off the ground, and a dry river crossing where a separate belay rope is not used. If two ropes are used, they should be as close as possible so that a single carabiner can be clipped to both lines. An individual would attach to both ropes when crossing, thus having a backup in case of failure. Redundancy is not always necessary or practical but is typically used in training as a risk management tool.

## Rope Bridges: METHODS OF CROSSING
16-14. All wet crossing techniques using handlines are covered in chapter 13. Methods of dry crossings using rope bridges is determined by several factors:

The load being transported on the rope bridge (such as personnel, equipment, and litter).

The risk of the terrain (such as fast-moving water and catastrophic falls).

The angle of the rope bridge (such as level, ascending, and descending).

Equipment available (such as extra ropes, harnesses, and rigging equipment).

16-15. Leaders must evaluate the situation and determine the most efficient means of crossing. For example, the method chosen for moving a light element with no sustainment gear across a 10-foot stream is different from the method chosen for moving crew-served weapons across a 50-foot-wide, fast-moving river.

16-16. If Soldiers can transport themselves across, they can choose either the commando crawl or the Tyrolean traverse.

## Rope Bridges: COMMANDO CRAWL
16-17. Soldiers lie on top of the rope with a foot hooked on the rope and a knee bent, o positioned nearly in line with the buttocks; the other leg hangs straight to maintain balance. They progress by pulling with their arms. To recover if they fall over, Soldiers hook one leg and the opposite arm over the rope, and then push down with the other hand to regain position.

## Rope Bridges: TYROLEAN TRAVERSE
16-18. Soldiers don a harness or tie a field expedient harness and attach a locking carabiner into it, with the nose of the carabiner facing up and away from their body. They then face the rope and clip into the rope bridge. Soldiers rotate under the rope and pull with their hands and arms to make progress. The Tyrolean traverse is the preferred method. If crossing with rucksacks, a carabiner is inserted into the frame and attached to the rope bridge. Soldiers then place one or both legs through the shoulder carrying straps and pull the rucksack across.

## Rope Bridges: TAGLINES
16-19. Heavy equipment will require the use of a tagline. A tagline is used to pull objects from the near side to the far side. An additional rope is brought across the rope bridge and anchored to the far side. The other end is anchored on the near side. All the slack is pulled to the near side, and a figure eight on a bight knot is tied at the loading platform. A carabiner is inserted into the loop and clipped onto the rope bridge.

16-20. If the rope bridge is longer than half a rope length, two separate ropes are required to create a tagline.

One end of the far-side tagline is brought across the obstacle and anchored to the far side. The other end is secured to a carabiner. One end of the near side tagline is secured at the near side, and the other end is secured into the same carabiner as the far-side tagline. That carabiner is then placed onto the rope bridge.

16-21. If the rope bridge is descending (the far-side anchor is significantly lower than the near side), a belay may need to be added to the tagline system. The tagline attached to the near side will be converted into a belay (see chapter 10) to allow for a controlled movement across and down the rope bridge.

## Rope Bridges: EQUIPMENT RIGGING
16-22. Any special equipment, such as crew-served weapons, ammunition, or supplies, must be rigged for movement across the rope bridge. A unit SOP may dictate the rigging of these items, but many expedient methods exist. The rigging should use various items that would be readily available to a deployed unit. Some of these items include tubular nylon webbing, cordage (various sizes), and carabiners.

16-23. Equipment that can be bundled together, such as rucksacks, should be Bundles can be created with a cordelette. If there are insufficient personnel on the far side to lift the bundle for unloading, a releasable Munter mule can be utilized at the point of attachment to the tagline.

16-24. Litters can be transported across a rope bridge if there is a secure means of attaching the litter to the rope, such as the rescue litter with its horizontal lift straps. The carabiner of the tagline will remain on the rope bridge. On each side of this carabiner, using the hauling line tie a middle-of-the-rope clove hitch around the carabiners of both horizontal lift straps of the litter. Remove the slack between the carabiners. Then place the carabiners in each of the lift straps onto the rope bridge. (See figure 16-5.) The same technique used for the rucksacks is used to pull the litter across.

## Rope Bridges: ARTIFICIAL HEIGHT
![Figure 16-5. Litter rigging](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-5.webp)

16-25. Artificial height is often useful or even necessary to raise the height of rope bridges. (See figure 16-6, page 378.) This may be needed when desiring dry river crossings or to better facilitate the loading and unloading of personnel or equipment. Artificial height may be needed on one or both sides of a rope bridge.

16-26. Occasionally, bridge sites may be selected where a tree with a high sturdy limb may be slung with rope or cord. On rock faces, artificial anchors may be constructed in convenient locations. Otherwise, A-frames may need to be constructed.

16-27. A-frames are structures constructed with poles to create artificial height for use with rope bridges.

Two sturdy poles are needed. The exact size of the poles depends on the type of load and location of the installation. The average size A-frame pole should be at least 3 inches in diameter and 9 to 12 feet long.

Three to five 13-foot cordelettes are needed depending on the size of the poles used for the A-frame.

![Figure 16-6. Artificial height (A-frame) used with high line](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-6.webp)

## Rope Bridges: A-FRAME CONSTRUCTION
16-28. There are many acceptable techniques and variations for the construction of A-frames. The general process is described as follows: (See figure 16-7.)

Place two suitable poles with the butt ends flush and mark the desired apex on both poles.

- With a section of rope or cordelette, tie a clove hitch around one of the poles a few inches above the apex marking, leaving about 12 inches of tail to the outside of the poles. (See figure 16-7[A].)

- With the poles side by side, wrap the remaining length of rope horizontally around both poles six to eight times, wrapping downward from the clove hitch. (See figure 16-7[B].) It may be necessary to join another section of rope to the first with an appropriate joining knot. Position this knot on the outside of one of the poles so as not to interfere with the vertical wraps. (See figure 16-7[C].)

- On the last horizontal wrap, continue around the pole that does not contain the clove hitch, pass the rope between the poles below the wraps, and make four to six tight vertical wraps around the horizontal wraps. (See figure 16-7[D].) Make the wraps as tight as possible. The vertical wraps must be as flat as possible next to each other. Insert a carabiner into the last two vertical wraps. (See figure 16-7[E].)

- On the last vertical wrap, pass the rope between the poles above the horizontal wraps. Tie it off with a square knot in the section of rope coming from the clove hitch. Secure with overhand knots tied in the tails. (See figure 16-7[F].)

![Figure 16-7. A-frame horizontal and vertical wraps](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-7.webp)

16-29. Use a spreader rope to prevent the A-frame from collapsing from pressure applied at the apex. (See figure 16-8, page 380.) Secure the legs of the A-frame together with a cordelette to fix the maximum spread.

If the ground is soft, dig the legs in about 6 inches. If the ground is a hard surface, anchor the bases of the poles at a 45°-angle to secondary anchor points. The spreader rope should be no more than 6 inches above ground level.

## Rope Bridges: ANCHORING THE A-FRAME TO THE INSTALLATION ROPE
![Figure 16-8. A-frame spreader](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-8.webp)

16-30. If used, an A-frame must be anchored to the installation rope. Tie a clove hitch at the center of a cordelette. Place it over one of the poles above the apex and move down to the apex so that the locking bar of the clove hitch is to the inside of the A-frame. Secure each end of the cordelette to one of the tightened static lines with two prusik knots—one forward and one to the rear of the A-frame on the same static line rope. (See figure 16-9.)

![Figure 16-9. Anchoring the A-frame to the installation rope](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-9.webp)

16-31. Position the A-frame so that the angles created by the A-frame bisecting the installation rope are approximately equal on both sides. This creates downward pressure holding the A-frame in position, not forcing it in a lateral direction. It must also be placed in a straight line between the upper and lower anchor points.

## Rope Bridges: RETRIEVAL
16-32. If the element is crossing the obstacle as part of a longer movement, and there are no follow-on troops, the system must be installed to be retrievable:

- Nearside personnel untie the high-line system maintaining the near side ends of both the installation

rope and belay or tagline rope.

Far-side personnel pull all slack across the obstacle.

- Nearside personnel secure the end of the installation line to the anchor.

Tie a fixed loop in the near side end of the installation rope.

Secure the fixed loop to the installation rope around the nearside anchor with a locked carabiner.

Far-side personnel pull the installation rope tight with several personnel, and then wrap it around an anchor tree with a minimum of two full wraps. Those personnel maintain control of the end of the rope keeping it tight with the tree by hand or can close out the rope by means of two half hitches like the close out on the transport tightening system. Another method is for far-side personnel to build a new tightening system on their side to tighten the rope.

- Nearside personnel attach the tagline to installation line figure eight on a bight using a locked

carabiner and a fixed loop. The tagline remains slack.

- Nearside personnel attach themselves to the installation line in the same way as the main body and

cross the high line.

- Once all personnel are on the far side, the installation rope is unwrapped from the far-side anchor

and allowed to go slack. If a new tightening system was used, it is broken down.

The tagline is then pulled across the obstacle dragging the installation line with it until both ropes are on the far side. Ensure all knots are removed from both ropes before pulling.

## Rope Bridges: HIGH LINES
16-33. A high line is a type of suspended rope bridge used to transport equipment or casualties high above difficult terrain that might otherwise be impassable. These can be used for personnel movement as well, although this is less common. The high line is used over large rivers, ravines, chasms, or diagonally from the top of a cliff to the ground below. The system may be established on different planes, varying from horizontal to near vertical. High lines keep the load suspended above the terrain as it travels directly between the near-and far-side anchors.

16-34. High lines come with a high level of risk due to the height of the load above the ground. High lines used for personnel movement require careful calculation to ensure system failure will not occur, resulting in catastrophic fall injury. They should only be constructed by advanced or master military mountaineers who have been formally trained in high-line construction for this purpose.

## Rope Bridges: SITE SELECTION
16-35. The crossing site must have appropriate anchors at the near side and the far side and suitable loading and off-loading platforms. If the anchors do not provide enough height to allow clearance, additional height, such as an A-frame, may be used.

## Rope Bridges: INSTALLATION
16-36. The same options and tightening techniques used for rope bridges can also be used for high lines; however, due to the higher consequences of rope or equipment failure, a second rope as described earlier must always be used for redundancy. Construction of high lines can be time-consuming and equipment intensive. Installation teams are likely to require additional skills and techniques to get the far end of the installation ropes to the far side of the obstacle.

16-37. Artificial height is used the same way as described for rope bridges.

## Rope Bridges: EQUIPMENT RIGGING, BELAYS, AND TAGLINES
16-38. Slings and cordelettes can be used to attach loads to the installation ropes and can be modified as necessary. (See figure 16-10.) Two examples are using two ends of a piece of webbing with a water knot or using a sewn sling. Place a locking carabiner on the high line and place the webbing or sling into it. Bring the bight at the bottom end of the sling up (double it) and clip it into the locking carabiner. A second locking carabiner can now be placed into the two bights. Attach the load by running the long loop of the carrying sling through the load and attaching the bottom loop to the traverse rope carabiner.

![Figure 16-10. Carrying sling](img:28-rope-bridges/fig-16-10.webp)

16-39. Attach a lowering rope to the second locking carabiner in the carrying sling when speed of the load must be controlled. (See figure 16-10.) Standard lowering techniques discussed in chapter 12 can be used.

This rope can be attached to the A-frame using an additional carabiner attached to the carabiner that is in the apex. If Soldiers are crossing, they should hold onto the carrying sling and keep their hands off the installation rope.

16-40. Taglines can also be used as described for rope bridges and are nearly always necessary when the high line does not contain a relatively constant downgrade.

## Rope Bridges: RETRIEVAL
16-41. The high line can be retrieved in a manner like the one rope bridge. However, as it is often used to move equipment and casualties, it can be dismantled after it is no longer needed without being retrievable.

## Rope Bridges: SECTION II – GUIDING LINES
16-42. A guiding line (see figure 16-11) is an effective means of transporting a litter over uneven, broken terrain (talus) commonly found at the base of cliffs. A guiding line is established and tightened in the same manner as a descending high line using MA but is not tied off once tensioned. The tensioning team retains control of the haul strand and adjusts tension in the installation line as the litter is lowered down it. The litter’s

descent is controlled by a separate team operating the lowering system using a second rope. (See chapter 12 for hauling and lowering systems.) These two teams work together to ensure the litter travels just high enough above the broken terrain to keep movement smooth. Additionally, attendants may move on foot through the terrain to help guide the litter around obstacles.

16-43. The main benefit gained from using a guiding line over a high line is the reduction in risk. Because the guiding line is only tensioned enough to lift the litter in the air, the rope is at a very low risk of being over tensioned and the litter is never high above the ground. Using a high line in the same piece of terrain would usually involve suspending the litter high above the ground on a highly tensioned rope. Additionally, movement down a guiding line is much easier on the casualty than being hand carried through large talus.

16-44. While guiding lines can be a lower risk solution, they can be more complex and are usually more time-consuming. Leaders must ensure that their element is formally trained on guiding lines and has the equipment necessary to accomplish the task safely.
