## Mountain Stream Crossing
**Chapter 13**

In 480 BC, Persian King Xerxes constructed a pontoon bridge that spanned a mile and a quarter across the Hellespont. After a previous failure to take Greece, the king weighed the options of either finding alternate routes to the north that added months to his expedition or pinpoint potential water crossing locations for his army. Xerxes insisted on the fastest and most direct route across the Aegean inlet for his 300,000 men. While negotiating the linear obstacle involved massive resources and engineering, it would save precious time for his army to reach their objective.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: SECTION I – MOUNTAIN STREAM FUNDAMENTALS
13-1. A mountain stream crossing is essentially an approach to negotiating a potentially swift and cold waterway that Soldiers may encounter in the mountains. Learning to cross small streams forms a foundation of skills for personnel to manage larger streams and rivers, where consequences could be far more hazardous.

13-2. In an operation involving the crossing of a stream or river, the actual crossing itself is a means, not an end sought. The purpose is for designated units to conduct a crossing to establish security on the far bank to protect remaining personnel. This operation should be conducted economically and with minimal loss of momentum.

13-3. There are limits on the safe use of the techniques discussed in this chapter. Not all mountain rivers or streams will be fordable with these techniques. If a water obstacle is too wide, swift, or deep, an alternate route should be used. It may require using aircraft transport if engineer support is unavailable.

Reconnaissance of questionable crossing sites is essential. This chapter covers the techniques for crossing mountain streams that have a depth generally not exceeding waist level.

13-4. Stream crossings should not be taken lightly. The force of flowing water may be extremely great and is most often underestimated. All rivers and streams are obstacles to movement. They should be treated as danger areas and avoided whenever possible. When rivers or streams must be crossed, there are many techniques that the small-unit leader may choose from, depending upon the type of stream, its width, speed of the current, and depth of the water. This activity should be rehearsed prior to execution in the operational environment.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: RECONNAISSANCE
13-5. Performing a site survey of potential crossing locations (map, photo, or aerial) may not always reveal that a water obstacle exists. In a swamp, for example, unfordable sloughs may not show on the map, and they may be concealed from aerial observation by a canopy of vegetation.

13-6. Site selection is extremely important once Soldiers determine that they must make a crossing. Elevated positions offer an optimal vantage point from which to analyze a large area of possibilities. This is sometimes better than a hundred close views from a riverbank. Select the site selection before the main body arrives and examine the site around a similar time of day that the crossing will be conducted.

13-7. A suitable crossing site should have specific characteristics. A river crossing site should limit exposure to observation, facilitate the installation team crossing (if necessary) or swimming with ropes, and minimize drowning hazards. Care must be taken to avoid an installation team crossing upstream of rapids, waterfalls, or fallen trees in the river. For dry features, such as gorges or ravines, the site should facilitate the installation team’s descent and ascent crossing while minimizing any falling hazards that exist. While dry crossings are preferred to wet crossings for speed and safety factors, they can become locations for an enemy to place landmines and improvised explosive devices.

13-8. The time of day is an important factor. Recent precipitation or increased temperatures could cause water levels to rise as glaciers or snow-covered peaks begin to melt. Early morning crossings are preferred, as the cooler night temperatures usually reduce the melt from snow and ice. A morning crossing also allows clothing items to dry throughout the day. Whether a crossing is made in daylight or in darkness depends on

the need for concealment, proficiency of troops, nature of the terrain, characteristics of the water obstacle, enemy disposition and capabilities (such as minefields on far banks), and the need for speed.

13-9. A crossing point should be chosen where the river widens. The sudden width of the channel normally yields a shallow depth. Avoid sharp turns in the river or a narrowing choke point in the river or stream since the water is likely to be deep and have a strong current on the outside of the bend for curves.

13-10. Many mountain streams, especially those fed by glacier runoff, contain sections with numerous braided channels. While often shallower than a main river, this changing landscape offers little in the way of cover and concealment of exposed personnel. Shoals, or partially submerged sandbars, can act like quicksand depending on the riverbed composition.

13-11. Riverbanks should be low enough to accommodate for easy entry and exit from the water. Banks that are tall and steep may require employing installations such as hasty rappels (see figure 11-11, page 293) or simple fixed ropes (see figure 11-2, page 278).

13-12. Logjams and other large obstructions present their own hazards. Floating debris, while often hung up in shallow spots, can potentially hit personnel in the water. Water flowing around logjams creates riverbed erosion and swift waters. If crossing upstream of logjams, ensure that Soldiers are far enough away to react and do not become entangled in branches beneath the water surface.

13-13. When possible, cross with ample overwatch in place for support and with an approach that offers minimal detection by enemy forces.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: PREPARATION OF TROOPS AND EQUIPMENT
13-14. Special considerations should be taken for personnel and the items they carry for the mission. Water can weigh personnel down, as can the uniform posture when dealing with submerged equipment. Most of the preparation should happen long before entering the water, with only special considerations executed in a short halt prior. Preparation should include—

- Water-sensitive items. Wrap radios, binoculars, documents, maps, and any extra clothing in waterproof bags (trash bags also work well), if available. These bags also provide additional flotation, should Soldiers find themselves in deeper water than expected. Pack these dry clothing items on top, along with other necessary items (such as munitions, sensitive items, and machine gun tripods) quickly before getting into the water.

- Uniform. If the tactical situation permits, a crossing can be made with minimal clothing. Once on the far side, Soldiers can change into dry clothing. Normally, a complete uniform is worn. Unblouse boots and untuck shirts. Ensure all pockets are buttoned or closed. If water has a point of entry, ensure that it has an easy way to exit. Trapped water can weigh down personnel who may need to swim at a moment’s notice. Socks and boot insoles can be removed and stowed. Once on the far bank, boots can be drained, and wet socks replaced. Load-carrying equipment and body armor. Unbuckle fighting load carrier or other modular equipment and be prepared to remove, if necessary. It is difficult to remove a buckled harness in the water during an emergency.

- Helmets can be removed and stowed, but if Soldiers fall in water with debris, the helmet protects

their head from impacts.

The rucksack should be worn well up on the shoulders and snug, so it does not flop around and cause imbalance. The waist and chest straps must be unbuckled so Soldiers can rid themselves of the rucksack if they are swept off their feet and must resort to swimming. Securing everything inside the pack will help if it needs to be discarded. It is easier to find one large pack than several smaller items.

- Weapons should be secured to the pack or slung over the shoulder.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: SECTION II – CROSSING TECHNIQUES
13-15. Several techniques and methods exist to successfully negotiate water obstacles. Variables that can influence the most optimal crossing style are water depth and speed, confidence around water and swimming

proficiency, riverbed surface, daylight or night crossing, and resources available. When available, a dry crossing is always preferred.

13-16. In this section, we will discuss three different variations that work well for mountain stream crossings: the individual crossing, team crossing, and belayed crossing.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: INDIVIDUAL CROSSINGS
13-17. Whenever possible, and when the degree of experience permits, streams should be forded individually for a speedier crossing. (See figure 13-1.) The average Soldier should be able to cross most streams with mild to moderate currents and water depths of not much more than knee-deep using proper techniques. An advantage to the individual crossing is that there is nothing to recover once Soldiers have performed the crossing. A disadvantage is the time of exposure for the individual and time invested for the element to negotiate the water obstacle.

13-18. The water depth at a suitable crossing site should be about knee height or below, and the water’s speed should be mild to moderate. The velocity of the water and the presence of debris are used to determine the effect, if any, on the ford’s condition. Currents are categorized as—ν Swift (more than 1.5 meters [5 feet] per second). ν Moderate (1 to 1.5 meters [3 to 5 feet] per second). ν Slow (less than 1 meter [3 feet] per second).

13-19. Individuals should look upstream for obstacles, and pick up a good athletic stance, angling their body slightly perpendicular to the current. Adjust to a more parallel stance and lean upstream if the current is stronger.

13-20. Pick a location slightly downstream to the Soldier’s position. Traveling at 90° causes Soldiers to fight the current rather than work with the flow of water as much as possible. Always lead off with the downstream foot and take small steps while keeping the feet close to the ground. Avoid moving one foot before securing the other foot, and do not cross legs. This will decrease the likelihood of moving water throwing the Soldier off balance. Additionally, it will allow the Soldier to explore the riverbed for obstacles such as holes, rocks, shoals, or debris for optimal management. If a sizeable rock is found, Soldiers should always step on the upstream side of it. Water cresting the top and sides of rocks picks up speed, digging around and on the downstream side. The integrity of these rocks is unknown and could roll downstream at any time.

13-21. For added stability, using a third contact point is encouraged. A long stick or staff, ice axe, or tree limb could be used. By placing the end of the third point as upstream as possible, Soldiers can create a tripod, while exercising a body position described above to create a streamlined signature. The third point can be useful as a probe, searching for obstacles and depth.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: TEAM CROSSINGS
13-22. When the water level begins to reach thigh deep, or anytime the current is too swift for personnel to safely perform an individual crossing, a team crossing may be used. With each of these methods, have a leader call each step. One advantage to the team crossing is reduced exposure time for higher concentration of personnel, and there is no equipment to recover once Soldiers have performed the crossing. Disadvantages for all team crossings include a lack of interval for free movement and negotiation of riverbed makeup.

Additionally, the close concentration of personnel makes them an easy target for direct or indirect enemy fires. The speed at which Soldiers can move fire teams and squads is that of a singular unit.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: CHAIN CROSSING
13-23. Two or more individuals cross arms with each other and lock their hands in front of themselves. (See figure 13-2.) The line formed faces the far bank. The largest individual should be on the upstream side of the line to break the current for the group, leaning upstream, into the current. The line formed will then move across the stream using the same principles as for individual crossings but with the added support of each other. The team still moves at a slight downstream angle, stepping off with the downstream foot in the lead.

If the formation begins to degrade, avoid trying to excessively hang onto personnel who have lost control. In that scenario, the alternative of swimming is the safest.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: CHAIN CROSSING (CONGA VARIATION)
13-24. All personnel in the team face into the current, with their hands grasping clothing or gear just behind the shoulders of the person on their upstream side. The largest individual should be on the upstream side of the line to break the current for the group, leaning upstream, into the current. The line formed will then move across the stream using the same principles as for individual crossings but with the added support of each other. The team still moves at a slight downstream angle, stepping off with the downstream foot in the lead.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: WEDGE OR DIAMOND TEAM CROSSING
13-25. Three or more individuals form a wedge, with the largest individual upstream to break the current for the group. Cross arms behind the back or rucksack and grasp the upstream individual, developing structured support. Subsequent personnel widen the wedge with smaller personnel or casualties filling the middle portion. Maintain an interval that supports free movement without tripping on other personnel. The wedge formed has the added support of each other. Move across the stream using most of the same principles as for individual crossings but all facing upstream. The team still moves at a slight downstream angle, stepping off with the downstream foot in the lead stepping together as one unit. If the formation begins to degrade, avoid trying to excessively hang onto personnel who have lost control. The alternative of swimming is the safest in that scenario.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: BELAYED CROSSINGS
13-26. Certain conditions may require using a rope system to aid in crossing or even keep Soldiers from being exposed to water. When the depth begins to approach knee to waist level and the current is strong enough to potentially knock a Soldier off balance, then using a handline (one rope bridge with belay line) could be appropriate. This is the safest form of crossing simply because Soldiers have direct influence from either riverbank.

13-27. Crossing on a handline will still require everyone to enter the water. In a case where water and air temperature significantly increase the risk of hypothermia, artificial height or other considerations may need to be implemented. Joining two or more ropes will only be possible for a handline. The maximum distance a one-rope bridge is capable of spanning is about one-half to two-thirds the length of the rope in use.

13-28. An advantage of using a belayed crossing is the safety aspect. If individual control is lost, personnel can be pulled to the unloading bank by those belaying. Disadvantages of this technique are the time required for preparation, the number of available anchors, and the time dedicated to recovering the crossing site.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: ESTABLISHING THE FAR ANCHOR
13-29. Whether a handline or rope bridge (see chapter 16) is to be installed, someone must cross the stream with one end of the rope and anchor it on the far side. The most capable and strongest swimmer in the party should perform this duty. Swimmers should be belayed across for their own safety. The belay position should be placed as far above the crossing as possible. If the current is too strong for individuals, they will pendulum back to the near bank. Rescuers should be poised on the near bank at points where individuals will pendulum back, should they fail to reach the far bank. The initial crossing site should be free of obstacles that would snag the rope and prevent the pendulum from going back to the bank for an easy recovery.

13-30. Individuals may attach the belay rope to their seat harness or a swami belt with a carabiner. They should never tie directly into the rope when being belayed for a stream crossing. If swimmers are swept away and become tangled, they must be able to release themselves quickly from the rope and swim to shore as best they can. Individuals may also choose to tie a fixed loop into the end of the belay rope and hang on to it, where they can immediately release it in an emergency.

13-31. Anytime a crossing site must be used where swimmers may encounter problems getting to the far bank, they should wear a life vest or other personal flotation device (PFD). If swimmers must release the rope at any time, they will have to rely on their own water survival skills and swimming ability to get to shore. A PFD greatly increases personal safety. A PFD may also be used by the last individuals across, as they will release the rope from the anchor and be belayed across as the first individual.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: INSTALLATION OF A HANDLINE
13-32. If it is possible to use a rope high enough above the water to enable Soldiers to perform a dry crossing, then a rope bridge should be installed as such. If this is impossible, and the rope must be installed to assist in a wet crossing, then it should be installed as a handline. (See figure 13-4.)

13-33. The far anchor should be downstream from the near anchor so that the rope will run at an angle downstream from the near anchor, about 30° to 45°, rather than straight across the stream. Here again, it is easier to move with the current as opposed to directly across or against it.

13-34. The rope may be anchored immediately on the far bank, pulled tight, and anchored on the near bank.

If a tighter rope is required, the handline may be installed with a transport tightening system. (See chapter 16.)

13-35. A second climbing rope is used as a belay. (See figure 13-5, page 330.) One end of the belay rope will be on the near bank and the other end on the far bank. It should be sent across with the strong swimmer. An appropriate knot is tied into the middle of the belay rope to form two fixed loops. Each loop is approximately 6-inches long. One loop is connected to the handline with a carabiner and individuals crossing connect one loop to themselves. The loops are short enough so individuals are always within arm’s reach of the handline should they slip and let go. Individuals are belayed from both the near and far banks. If a mishap should occur, the individual can be retrieved from either shore, whichever appears easiest. The belay on the opposite shore can be released, allowing the individual to pendulum to the bank. It is important not to anchor or tie the belay rope to the belayer so that it may be released quickly if necessary.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: MOVEMENT
13-36. Crossing will always be performed on the downstream side of the handline, shuffling the feet with the downstream foot in the lead. Under most circumstances, the handline should be crossed by one person at a time. This keeps rope stretch and load on the anchors to a minimum. Hands should remain on the handline, on either side of the belay line tie-in point. Individuals should look upstream for obstacles, and pick up a good athletic stance, keeping their body slightly perpendicular to the current. Take small steps while keeping the feet close to the ground to avoid any unwanted influence forced onto the body by rushing water. Either rucksacks can be carried on the back the same way as for individual crossings, or they can be attached to the handline and pulled along behind the individual. If a large amount of equipment must be moved across the stream, especially heavier weapons, such as mortars, recoilless rifles, and so on, then a site should be selected to install a rope bridge. (See chapter 16.)

13-37. Disadvantages to the belayed crossing include the large amount of time dedicated to site preparation, individual crossings, positioning of security teams, and lack of natural anchors on the riverbanks.

Additionally, only one person is belayed at a single time, with personnel on each bank dedicated to the belay, leaving fewer personnel for security at any given time.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: SWIMMING
13-38. Soldiers may find themselves alone and have no choice but to swim across, or they may find themselves suddenly plunged into a swift river or rapids. Soldiers should consider the following actions if Soldiers are in a situation that forces them to swim during a crossing:

Immediately strip away gear or clothing that restricts free movement and increases their signature.

Instead of fighting the current, work with the flow of water to get to a dry location downstream. It is often easier to negotiate hazards and see where Soldiers are going if they face downstream and fan the hands alongside the body to add buoyancy. They should use their hands to also fend off submerged rocks and position their feet as a shock absorber to shove off unseen or hidden obstacles.

- Keep the head above water to identify obstacles or debris that Soldiers may impact. Take the

opportunity to breathe between breaks if waves begin to rise over Soldiers’ heads.

- Avoid backwater eddies, bubbly water, water churning upwards, and converging currents. All these

hazards have the potential to create unpredictable buoyancy and possibly undertow.

If the shore is too difficult to reach, seek out the closest and safest spot, such as a sandbar, to get out of the water as quickly as possible. Hypothermia will set in quickly in colder waters.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: SECTION III – ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS AND HAZARDS
13-39. River and stream crossings present one of the most hazardous situations faced by military mountaineers. Substantial experience is necessary to properly evaluate and mitigate swift water hazards.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: SAFETY GUIDELINES FOR RIVER AND STREAM CROSSINGS
13-40. The following safety procedures are minimum guidelines that should be followed when conducting a river or stream crossing:

- All weak swimmers should be identified before a crossing so that stronger swimmers may assist in

crossing.

- Not every river or stream can be crossed safely. If it is possible, cross at a different time or place,

use a different technique, or choose another route.

The technique used is directly dependent upon water depth, speed of the current, stream bottom configuration, width of the stream, and individual experience.

The safest methods of crossing in the water are always with the use of a handline or rope bridge.

If the installation of a handline or rope bridge becomes too difficult at a given crossing site, then that site should be considered too hazardous and another site selected.

- A lookout should be posted 50 to 100 meters upstream to watch for any obstacles that may be carried

downstream and interfere with the crossing.

- When conducting individual crossings (those without a handline or rope bridge), spotters or security should be posted downstream with poles or ropes prepared to throw, for assistance or rescue. Medical personnel should be prepared for cases of hypothermia.

- When the unit knows a rope installation is required for crossing, at least two life vests or other PFDs should be on hand. These PFDs provide additional safety for the strong swimmer who must establish the far anchor and the last individual across who retrieves the system.

## Mountain Stream Crossing: WATER SURFACE CLUES
13-41. Observing and analyzing the top of the water can give military mountaineers an indication of what to expect beneath the surface. Water is not often clear enough to see all the hazards that could make the crossing difficult or have adverse effects on personnel attempting to cross. The following are common clues that can provide ideas of what lies beneath the surface.

Riffles or ripples—if they are lined up, there are likely one or several rocks that form a line. In shallower water, it could mean a stable, rocky riverbed.

- Holes, souse holes, or keepers—depressions that can form from water moving around an obstacle. The rise and drop on the surface can be visibly seen. Avoid stepping here as these rises and drops could suction Soldiers’ feet and debris can fall. Convergences and their depth—can increase the force the water has on someone attempting to cross. Eddy or back eddy—swirling of water and the reverse current created when water flows over or around an object or terrain. The consistency of a current is very unpredictable.
