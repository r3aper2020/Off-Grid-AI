# Navigating Mountainous Terrain (TC 3-97.61 Ch. 7)
Source: https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN45366-TC_3-97.61-000-WEB-1.pdf

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain
**Chapter 7**

The goals, strategies, and tools available to Soldiers for navigation in mountainous terrain are no different from other types of terrain. However, mountainous terrain makes using those tools to stay on course much more difficult. Additionally, straying off course while navigating in this terrain can prove extremely hazardous and detrimental to mission success. The difficulty of planning routes and calculating movement timelines also increases significantly with the steepness and complexity of terrain. Soldiers and leaders who learn how to mitigate these challenges effectively can not only successfully navigate in mountainous terrain but also better use the terrain for cover and concealment.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: MOUNTAINOUS TERRAIN
**Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: SECTION I – MILITARY LAND NAVIGATION IN NONTECHNICAL**

7-1. Navigation is the process of determining one’s present position, locating a target objective, and selecting and following a route between these two points. Navigation consists of three distinct stages: orientation, navigation, and route finding.

7-2. Orientation is simply figuring out exactly where one is. Using a map, compass, and identifiable terrain features, assisted by an altimeter and GPS, is the foundation of good orientation.

7-3. Navigation determines the objective’s location and the direction from the Soldier’s starting point to the objective. The same skills and equipment used in orientation are essential for good navigation.

7-4. Route finding is picking the best line of travel IAW METT-TC (I); mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available, time available, civil considerations, and informational considerations, and the commander’s intent. Good route finding in mountainous terrain requires solid terrain analysis, clear priorities for route selection, an understanding of group members’ levels of fitness and mobility skill, unit capabilities for technical movement, and mission-specific requirements. Examples of priorities for route selection are navigability of terrain, tactical advantage and disadvantage throughout the movement, concealment throughout, fatigue reduction, and required speed of movement.

7-5. Perhaps the single, most important factor in navigation through mountainous terrain is the ability to identify and analyze terrain. Navigators must be able to create an accurate mental model of the terrain to be moved through from a map or satellite imagery. The more skilled they are at this, the more effective they will be at avoiding hazards, selecting tactically advantageous or concealed routes, and planning paths that minimize fatigue. This ability can only be developed effectively through route planning and movement in such terrain. Skill at land navigation in level terrain does not necessarily translate to skill in mountainous terrain. (See TC 3-25.26 for more information on general navigation techniques.)

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: NAVIGATIONAL EQUIPMENT
7-6. The following equipment should be considered for use when planning for or navigating in mountainous terrain:

- Maps and protractors. Imagery (satellite, aerial, or surface taken). Compasses.

- Altimeters.

- GPS devices.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: MAPS AND PROTRACTORS
7-7. Ideal topographical maps for mountain navigation are those of sufficient scale and detail to depict terrain features accurately. This may require using larger-scale maps usually requested for military operations and

may require carrying more map sheets to cover all terrain in any operation. For larger-scale maps, a smaller contour interval can make terrain analysis easier.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: SATELLITE AND AERIAL IMAGERY
7-8. Additional imagery of complex terrain can be very useful to a navigator for route planning. However, top-down satellite imagery often is not as useful as topographical maps. Aerial or surface photographs of specific terrain features can be incredibly useful for finding and planning routes through complex terrain.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: COMPASSES
7-9. The lensatic compass is most used and can be employed in a variety of ways for either day or night navigation. It can be used as an effective tool for navigating the mountains. The primary benefits of a lensatic over adjustable declination compasses are durability, inclusion of a mil-radian scale, and availability in the military supply system.

7-10. Ideal compasses for navigation in mountainous terrain are ones that have an adjustable declination. The user can adjust the compass to compensate for the difference between magnetic and grid north. This eliminates the conversion step usually required when obtaining navigational azimuths, thereby reducing time and probability of math errors. These types of compasses also usually come with illuminated north-seeking arrows, built-in protractor, and even magnifying glasses incorporated into the design. They are typically lighter than traditional military compasses and are the most widely used compasses by mountaineers. These compasses typically do not include scales in mil-radians and are usually plastic in construction, making them less durable than metal compasses.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: ALTIMETERS
7-11. Altimeters are valuable pieces of navigational equipment used by experienced navigators in mountainous terrain. They are rarely used in flatter terrain types. Most Soldiers have not been exposed to them or trained in their use. Altimeters provide navigators with elevation information for their location without requiring GPS satellite signal. This information turns contour lines on a map into reliable linear features that can be followed, used as progress markers during movement, or as known points when they intersect with other clearly identifiable linear terrain features.

7-12. Modern altimeters are most commonly digital and typically come in wristwatch form, though they are available as stand-alone digital devices. Battery life must be considered when using these devices.

Nonelectronic analog altimeters are also available, typically as stand-alone devices. Digital altimeter devices also have a digital compass feature. Most mountaineers use digital watch altimeters.

7-13. Altimeters use a barometer incorporated into their design to measure the weight of air above the device.

Since air pressure drops uniformly as elevation is gained, it can be used to read altitude by means of the altimeter’s scale or digital display, marked in feet or meters of elevation above sea level.

7-14. Changes in the weather are usually accompanied by air pressure changes, which are reflected in the altimeter. As the air pressure drops due to the approach of inclement weather, for instance, the displayed elevation rises by a corresponding amount. This means that a barometric pressure change of 1 inch of mercury equals roughly 1,000 feet of elevation. If the altimeter displays an elevation gain of 300 feet, a loss of barometric pressure of 0.3 inch has occurred, and bad weather should be expected.

7-15. Because the altimeter is sensitive to changes in air pressure, it must be recalibrated whenever a point of known elevation (summits, saddles, stream-trail intersections, survey monuments, and so forth) is reached.

This is especially important when weather fronts are moving rapidly through the area.

7-16. Altimeters are currently available in the military supply system. (See appendix C for national stock numbers.)

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: GLOBAL POSITIONING SYSTEMS
7-17. The GPS is a highly accurate tool that can calculate user location by using a satellite constellation system. It can determine the latitude, longitude, and elevation of the user. Location information is also displayed in military grid coordinates.

7-18. The GPS provides precise steering information, as well as position locations. The receiver can accept many checkpoints entered in any coordinate system by the user and convert them to the desired coordinate system. The user then calls up the desired checkpoint, and the receiver displays direction and distance to the checkpoint. The receiver can also compute the approximate travel time to the next checkpoint.

7-19. Because the GPS does not need visible landmarks to operate, it can provide position (accurate to within 5 meters) in darkness or on featureless terrain. GPS also does not compound navigational errors as compass use can.

7-20. GPS devices and satellites communicate via radio waves, so anything that blocks light reduces or blocks the effectiveness of the signals. The more unobstructed the view of the sky, the better the system performs.

Although the GPS can be used in any terrain, it performs best in more open areas, such as the desert. Heavy cloud cover and precipitation, such as snow or rain, can interfere with or block GPS signals.

7-21. Because the GPS requires horizon-to-horizon views for good satellite reception, its utility can be limited in the mountains. Canyons, deep valleys, saddles, and steep mountainsides are all problematic. Good locations to get satellite reception are high points or wide-open areas, such as ridgelines, spurs, summits, plateaus, and wide valleys.

7-22. When using GPS in regions with questionable surveying and mapping products, the operational datum of the local maps must be reconciled with the datum used in navigational and targeting systems. Identify the spheroid and datum information on the pertinent map sheets and then check that the GPS receiver has the compatible datum loaded. If not, then contact the intelligence staff office for updated datum or maps.

Otherwise, the GPS will show different locations than those on the map.

7-23. Cold temperatures (50°F and below) and high elevations will adversely affect the GPS’s operation due to the batteries and the liquid crystal diode screen freezing. Soldiers can improve battery life and overall performance by placing the GPS inside their parka or coat.

7-24. The best use of the GPS is to supplement, back up, or verify the accuracy of other navigational techniques. GPS should never solely be relied on to navigate in mountainous terrain.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: NAVIGATIONAL TECHNIQUES
7-25. When planning movement through mountainous terrain, all possible techniques should be considered for use. Typically, the best approach is a combined use of all the above tools to identify and navigate efficiently IAW the mission and commander’s intent. No single technique is a universal solution to every situation. Navigating requires putting varied emphasis on tools at different times along the route. The one skill used in mountain navigation that remains critical is terrain analysis and association.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: DEAD RECKONING
7-26. Because of the complex nature of mountainous terrain, dead reckoning is usually of limited value on most movements. Broken or sheer terrain can make it impossible to follow a precise azimuth for any significant distance. Furthermore, doing so may put significantly more strain on personnel as it may require traveling up and down or boxing around numerous large terrain features. The compass is generally employed more to support terrain association and to orient the map than as a primary navigational aid. Dead reckoning can be a useful and necessary tool under certain conditions, such as periods of limited visibility. Heavy fog, snowy, or whiteout conditions may prevent the use of other more effective techniques. Using dead reckoning under these conditions requires the development of a detailed limited visibility navigational plan. This plan must be followed precisely to prevent wandering off course into potentially hazardous terrain.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: TERRAIN ASSOCIATION
7-27. Terrain association is the process of creating a mental model of what terrain features look like from what is depicted on a map so that they can be identified and used to guide navigation during movement. This skill is critical in mountainous terrain. Once proficiency is developed, navigation in terrain of significant relief can be easier than in flatter terrain, because every feature stands out as a landmark.

7-28. The most efficient way to increase skill and proficiency with terrain association is through practice navigating in areas of significant relief. The mnemonic SOSES (shape, orientation, size, elevation, slope)

approach to considering terrain features is especially valuable in mountainous terrain association and should be practiced extensively (see TC 3-25.26). Terrain identification and association training should be incorporated into all land navigation training for Soldiers who are likely to operate in mountainous terrain.

7-29. Terrain association allows Soldiers to identify navigational aids while planning to ensure the navigator does not stray off course. The following are some examples of navigational aids:

Checkpoints are easily identifiable terrain features to either side of a route, or linear features crossing a route, which are used to determine distance traveled. These can be hilltops, saddles, streams, or ridgelines, to name a few. Manmade features are less reliable due to lack of permanence.

Checkpoints are typically used to mark the end of a leg of a route.

- Handrails are easily identifiable linear features that run parallel with the navigator’s route, or leg of a route. They can be roads, streams, valleys, draws, or even distant ridgelines. During movement, they can be paralleled to a checkpoint or the objective. Navigational corridors are very large handrail features, such as valleys.

- Attack points are easily identifiable features within 200 meters of an objective where area navigation ends and point navigation starts. Attack points should be located on a feature that is easy and quick to navigate to, such as a hilltop or prominent feature. From the attack point, the navigator can slow down movement speed to increase navigational accuracy for the final short movement into the objective. Often, it is best to use dead reckoning from the attack point to ensure navigational accuracy is maintained. Expanded objectives are large features that contain the actual objective, such as a large hill. The navigator can easily navigate to the hill from far off and then reorient to move to the precise objective. Catching features and elevation backstops are used to provide a limit of advance to prevent navigators from overshooting their objective. Some examples of catching features include stream, draw, elevation, or even movement time.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: ALTIMETER NAVIGATION
7-30. Altimeters help the navigator in several ways. They aid in orientation, in computing rates of ascent or descent, during resection, and in weather prediction.

7-31. Altimeters allow any elevation line on a map to be used as a linear feature. Navigators can plan the legs of their route that run perpendicular to elevation lines. Navigators can then easily track progress as they ascend or descend by referencing their current elevation during movement. In the same way, elevation lines provide checkpoints and catching features to ensure the navigator does not ascend or descend too far.

Navigators can also plan the legs of their route to run parallel to elevation lines on large terrain features. The navigator only needs to ascend or descend to the desired elevation, then turn, and follow it toward the objective or next checkpoint. Again, this is done by checking the altimeter occasionally to ensure they stay at the desired elevation while moving.

7-32. Altimeters can confirm terrain association assumptions during movement. Anytime navigators are standing on an easily identifiable terrain feature, they can cross-reference data displayed on the altimeter with the terrain feature they think they are on using a map. If both numbers match, then the navigator is at the correct location.

7-33. Altimeters are also useful in reestablishing navigators’ locations if they are unsure of it via modified resection. This is done by obtaining a back azimuth to their location from an easily identifiable terrain feature, then drawing it on a map. Wherever that line crosses the contour line corresponding to the elevation, their altimeter reads their location.

7-34. Most altimeters allow the user to track and record elevation related data, such as ascent and descent rates, as well as overall elevation gain and loss. This data can help leaders more accurately plan route movement times in the future.

7-35. Altimeters can be used as barometers to assist in weather prediction. Although GPS provides triangulated altitude, it does not use a barometer to do so and, therefore, cannot monitor weather. Altimeter weather prediction only works if the altimeter remains in a static position for a significant period (for example, several hours or more). Most digital devices have a barometer function, allowing the user to track

the increase and decrease in pressure. These indicate changing weather conditions, whereas no or little increase or decrease indicates stable weather trends.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: ROUTE PLANNING
7-36. Careful map reconnaissance, knowledge of the enemy situation, terrain analysis of the operational area, and an accurate assessment of the unit’s capabilities are all key parts of the planning process. Route planning in the mountains proves to be difficult, and often the original route selection changes during movement due to the restrictive nature of this terrain. Always use METT-TC (I) to maximize advantages of routes used in military operations and carefully weigh costs and benefits of route decisions. Additionally, weather and surface conditions impact route decisions greatly.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: MISSION FACTORS
7-37. The mission and commander’s intent must be considered when planning routes in the mountains.

Establish priorities for route characteristics IAW their impact on the mission. Some common examples of route selection priorities are navigability of terrain, tactical advantage or disadvantage throughout the movement, concealment throughout, fatigue reduction, and required speed of movement. For example, if a strong security posture is required, speed may need to be sacrificed in favor of moving through tactically advantageous terrain that is more difficult. In other circumstances, the opposite may be true. Ultimately, route planners must possess a clear understanding of how their route selection decisions may affect the mission.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: ENEMY SITUATION
7-38. There are several factors to consider when assessing the enemy’s capabilities. Some of which include range of weapons, effectiveness of indirect fires, level of mobility and capabilities in mountainous terrain. If the enemy force is better prepared to maneuver in the mountains, they have a marked advantage, and route selection must be scrutinized.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: TERRAIN FACTORS
7-39. Topographic map reconnaissance provides the primary source of information concerning the area of operations. A 1:25,000 map depicts greater detail than a 1:50,000 map and should be used whenever possible.

Because examining the micro terrain is important for mountain operations, even larger-scale maps are desirable. Higher detailed nonmilitary maps may be useful, if available. Aerial, oblique angle, and photographs give details not always shown on maps (crags and overhangs). Sketch maps supplement other sources of information, but they should not be relied on for accuracy since they are seldom drawn to scale.

Along with sketch maps, verbal descriptions, documented information gathered from units previously in the area, or published sources, such as alpine journals or civilian climbing guides, may help. Forest service, logging, and mining company maps provide additional information, often showing the most recent changes to logging trails and mining access roads. Standard military topographic maps are generally accurate graphic depictions of the operational area.

7-40. Ensure map marginal information is reviewed for each new map used. The legend may contain mountain-specific symbols. Ensure map datum matches GPS datum, if used.

7-41. Enemy capabilities and recent activity in the area provide important information for determining concealed routes.

7-42. OAKOC should be used to analyze mountainous terrain during route planning. Typically, considering OAKOC from the enemy’s perspective is important. Additionally, mountain-specific hazards, such as falling rock or ice, loose, slick or ice-covered footing, avalanche threat, crevasse presence, and fall hazard, should all be considered when selecting routes.

- Observation and fields of fire—in mountainous terrain, the easiest lines of movement are often exposed to observation from great distances. Good analysis of potential enemy positions and activity must be considered prior to using open or silhouetting terrain for movement.

- Avenues of approach—when route planning, it is important to consider avenues of approach to positions that dominate terrain a route may run through. There is usually high ground around Soldiers in the mountains. Easily accessible dominating terrain is the most threatening to units on the move.

- Key terrain—when selecting routes, key operational terrain must be identified. Often key terrain is easier and allows nontechnical movement through and around very difficult terrain. These features are more likely to be observed by the enemy from positions that dominate them.

- Obstacles—broken rocky terrain, ice-covered slopes, cliff bands, rivers, ravines, and avalanche terrain can easily restrict or halt movement. These obstacles often require tactical movement techniques to maintain effective security (for example, traveling and bounding overwatch). Technical mobility skills, equipment, and systems for safe movement are also a result. If the terrain is severe or restrictive enough, VDA-crossing techniques may need to be employed to ensure unit security during certain legs of the route, which are a substantial operational undertaking. Cover and concealment—often folded and broken terrain provides good cover and concealment, even though it is more difficult to move in and maintain command and control. Draws, gullies, couloirs, and ravines often provide great concealment from a distance but also may contain water, loose rock, thick vegetation, and may be exposed to the hazard of falling objects or observation from high ground above.

7-43. Other resources may greatly assist in selecting appropriate routes in mountainous terrain. Open-source mapping programs developed for mountain navigation provide useful tools for route finding and planning.

Some provide detailed slope angle imagery that can help determine difficulty of movement, and valuable avalanche risk information that is not easily identified on a topographical map. In cases of extreme terrain complexity, advanced military mountaineers may need to be tasked with route finding or reconnaissance missions just to identify the possibility of a route existing through a given area.

7-44. Terrain also has a huge impact on the effectiveness of communications systems. Many terrain features block or reduce communications ranges in the mountains. The capabilities of the group’s radios must be considered. Routes may need to be adjusted to ensure line of sight with higher or adjacent units is maintained, or to periodically gain high points to hit communications windows.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: TROOPS FACTORS
7-45. Route planners must have a solid understanding of the fitness, mobility skills under load, tactical movement proficiency, technical proficiency, and morale of their group members to adequately plan routes through the mountains. These factors determine what terrain is passable, passable with protective systems, or impassable, and how much time is required to do so.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: CONSIDERATIONS FOR WINTER CONDITIONS
7-46. Winter route planning can be far more difficult than in other seasons. This is primarily due to the presence of more difficult snow-laden and ice-covered surfaces. Cold temperatures and the additional hazards that come with accumulated snow and ice may also present some challenges. Even just a few inches of snow or occasional icy patches significantly slow movement, especially if the correct equipment is not used.

Soldiers operating in these conditions should be issued special equipment necessary for moving over ice and snow IAW surface conditions. Movement times and risk of injury increase significantly in steeper terrain if the correct mobility equipment is not used. See chapter 6 for a detailed description of this equipment and its uses. Some of these techniques require significant practice to achieve proficiency, and the level of proficiency increases as the severity of the terrain and conditions increases.

7-47. The following must be considered when planning routes in winter conditions:

- Weather conditions affect the chosen route. During calm weather, a Soldier’s rate of movement is significantly faster than during periods of inclement weather. In windy conditions, routes on the leeward side of terrain features are usually preferred, so long as they do not pose an avalanche risk.

- Avalanche danger zones must be identified by map review and data gathered during route planning. Routes should not be planned through avalanche danger areas unless necessary for the mission. In that case, experienced personnel should conduct in-depth analysis of avalanche risk prior to movement.

- Vegetation can aid or hinder movement. Thickly forested areas usually have a deep snowpack. Trees provide shelter from the wind, but footing can be less reliable due to brush and small trees holding up a layer of snow that collapses when weighted, making movement arduous. Avalanches are still possible on forested slopes with an angle of 30° to 45°.

Lower angled snow-covered water features can provide valuable navigation aids and easy movement routes, especially if personnel are proficient skiers. Runoffs sometimes provide the only feasible routes through steep, thickly vegetated terrain.

Individual movement abilities affect rate of movement, limit choice of terrain, and route. All personnel must be proficient in the mobility skills (for example, crampon use, snowshoeing, or skiing) required by the surface conditions. Greater fitness is required to move in winter conditions than other seasons.

- Moisture output during movement should be considered when planning routes in cold temperatures. The rate of movement and the strenuousness of ascending slopes directly increase sweating, which can have a significant impact on the ability for personnel to stay warm when they stop movement. More frequent, shorter breaks should be planned, as opposed to fewer longer breaks, to reduce the need to up-dress and down-dress, and help personnel shed heat periodically.

7-48. The following conditions and characteristics of cold weather and mountainous regions make accurate navigation difficult:

In winter, short hours of daylight, fog, snowfall, blizzards, whiteouts, and drifting snow, especially above tree line, drastically limit visibility. At times, an overcast sky and snow-covered terrain create a phenomenon called flat light, which makes recognizing irregularities in the terrain extremely difficult.

- Heavy snow may completely cover existing tracks, trails, outlines of small lakes and similar landmarks. Because the terrain looks quite different in winter from that in summer, Soldiers must pay careful attention when identifying landmarks, both on the ground and from aerial photographs.

- Avoid handling maps, compasses, and other navigation instruments in low temperatures with bare hands. Exposing bare skin to these conditions increases the risk of frostbite. (See chapter 3 for information on cold-weather injuries.)

- Maintaining pace count is extremely difficult in winter and mountain environments. Thick

vegetation and rough, steep slopes hamper attempts at accurate pace counts.

7-49. Whenever there is a possibility of extremely limited visibility during movement, a whiteout navigation plan should be developed ahead of time and disseminated to every Soldier in the patrol. They should include numerous checkpoints and record the distance, azimuth, and end elevation for each leg. Guidance should be disseminated regarding rendezvous and rally points procedures. The easier the terrain each leg covers, the easier the plan is to follow blind. Waypoints can be entered manually into most GPS devices as well for those low-visibility circumstances where they can still connect to satellites. 7-50. Movement in mountainous terrain requires a lot more time than in many other terrain types. Route planners must understand how all the factors above affect the rate of their group’s movement so that they can

make route selection decisions that meet mission timelines. Accurately predicating movement times can only be achieved through building experience by moving in a wide range of mountainous terrain under a wide variety of conditions. Ideally, experience is developed through realistic unit training in similar terrain.

7-51. Movements in mountainous terrain are often measured in time rather than distance units because elevation gain, elevation loss, and surface conditions can be bigger factors. To do this, first measure the map distance. This distance plus one-third is a good estimate of actual ground distance. Add 1 hour for each 1,000 feet of ascent or 2,000 feet of descent to the time required for marching a map distance.

7-53. Combined Soldier loads that exceed 50 pounds per Soldier can be expected to slow movement significantly in mountainous terrain. Given the increased weight of extra ammunition for crew-served weapons, basic military mountaineering gear, and clothing for mountain travel, it becomes obvious that Soldiers will carry weights well over that 50-pound limit. Units should conduct cross-country movements in the mountains with the expected rucksack and tactical load out to develop more accurate movement time data.

7-54. Deep snow significantly reduces movement speeds and requires either snowshoes or skis to be practical for anything but the shortest movements.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: SECTION II – NAVIGATION IN TECHNICAL MOUNTAINOUS TERRAIN
7-55. Technical terrain is defined as terrain that requires ropes and specialized mountaineering equipment to increase security and reduce the consequences of falling. Navigation on this type of terrain can be very difficult. Safe travel requires careful analysis and a greater level of experience. Given the capability, however, units can own and operate in areas that may be off limits to the enemy.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: RECONNAISSANCE
7-56. For small teams with advanced skills, the variable nature of technical terrain makes detailed reconnaissance an invaluable tool. Although Soldiers must be particularly skilled at decision making and on-the-fly problem solving, information obtained during reconnaissance can greatly reduce the challenges of navigation.

7-57. Collecting information about the terrain before initiating movement is helpful to determine special equipment needs as well as movement techniques that may be required. Turnaround points, egress routes, and contingency plans should be identified to account for changing timelines, weather conditions, or unexpected enemy presence. Visually observing the terrain from as many angles as possible with the use of optics aids the mountaineer to identify specific features, such as cracks, ledges, cliff bands, and other landmarks, for travel. It may be used to determine rock quality and identify the presence of objective hazards.

7-58. When a physical recon is not practical, several tools assist the mountaineer with interpreting terrain and identifying potential routes. A combination of sources is best.

7-59. Maps can be used to assess terrain, establish distances, elevation, and general timelines. They are helpful for identifying key landmarks and potential weaknesses in the terrain that may allow for travel. Maps may not be up to date and may not always accurately reflect the ever-changing mountain landscape. Usually, micro terrain is not well represented on a military map, especially in mountainous terrain.

7-60. Satellite imagery is useful for identifying specific objective hazards, degree of cover and concealment, and for visualizing possible routes through steep terrain. If the imagery is recent, it may provide supplemental information if using an older map to verify the terrain and hazards. Imagery may also identify terrain that is not always identified on a map, for example, a scree or talus field.

7-61. Photos taken before or during movement are helpful for detailed technical navigation and can be used for future reference. They are particularly useful for remembering locations of small landmarks, intersections, and exposed or hazardous sections of terrain. Keep in mind that ridges and faces in photos often appear steeper than they are.

7-62. Using time estimating tools, such as the Munter method (see appendix B, section III), can assist in making accurate timelines in nontechnical terrain. However, timelines through technical terrain are difficult to accurately calculate, and extra time must be allotted to these sections. The timeline is determined based on the accuracy of the information collected, the conditions, and the skill and ability of the unit. The more experience an individual has moving in technical terrain, the more accurate their time estimations.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: ROPED MOVEMENT TECHNIQUES
7-63. Using roped movement techniques is an important decision that greatly affects the speed and efficiency of an element in technical terrain. Techniques described in chapter 10, such as fixed ropes, lead climbing, simultaneous climbing (known as simul-climbing), and rappelling may be necessary. Techniques are dictated by the terrain, weather, conditions, skill level, equipment available, and the tactical situation. Terrain that varies in difficulty may require the group to change movement techniques often to maintain efficiency. The best options create a balance of speed and security.

## Navigation in Mountainous Terrain: ROUTE FINDING
7-64. Individual mobility skills play a huge factor in determining the feasibility of movement in technical terrain. Good route finding requires continually assessing terrain in relation to the climbing skill of team members. Information gathered during reconnaissance can often be inaccurate or incomplete once in the terrain and requires quick decision making and problem-solving ability.

7-65. Keep established landmarks in sight and move through terrain that allows for protection when and where fall hazards exist. Prioritize ridges and buttresses rather than faces and gullies when possible. This reduces exposure to rockfall and keeps Soldiers in position to observe the surrounding terrain. Gullies often look easier but are more difficult to assess and have looser terrain and generally more objective hazard.

7-66. A common route-finding mistake is climbing rock that appears easy but is loose or does not offer solid anchors or good options for protection, when other options are available. Another is misinterpreting the difficulty level of the terrain and climbing to the point where reversing direction becomes too difficult.

Recognizing when one is “off route” early in the process is the key to avoiding serious injuries and major time delays. Any of these mistakes, when combined, can quickly lead to mission failure.

7-67. Soldiers must have experience with both success and failure when navigating in technical terrain to build the foundation of experience required to be effective on complex missions in the mountains. Time spent focused on planning, analyzing terrain, and executing technical movements while managing risk is invaluable to both the individual Soldiers and the unit.

7-68. Soldiers should be familiar with using collective data on established climbing routes found in guidebooks and online resources (see appendix B). Soldiers can train stateside and develop many of these critical skills, while intentionally selecting objectives within their ability level and with reasonable margins of safety. These resources contain route descriptions that include approach and decent information, rock quality, recommended rope strategies, topos, photographs, and more.

7-69. Commanders must understand that these skills take time to develop and that a degree of investment is necessary to create mountain Soldiers capable of such tasks.
