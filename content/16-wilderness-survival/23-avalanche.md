## Avalanche
**Chapter 5**

World War I is remembered for the brutal fighting in the trenches in France and Belgium.

However, in the fighting between Italy and the Austro-Hungarian Empire, Soldiers saw some of the most intense mountain fighting in human history. After the Italians checked the Austrian offensive through the passes of the Alps, both sides fought higher and higher into the mountains. Small, light units of mountaineers clashed over new ground high in the peaks, while the armies dug in on the peaks they had secured. Positions and paths were blasted out of the rock and carved through the glaciers to move personnel and materiel to the fighting in the heights.

By the winter of 1916, the line had mostly stabilized, and both armies were hunkering down for winter. Both sides had established extensive networks of trails and camps to support their forward positions; however, much of the infrastructure had been built in avalanche paths. In the second week of December, a major snowstorm blew in, loading the slopes and causing massive avalanches across the range.

Both nations suppressed the number of casualties they received, and no official Italian records exist, but what evidence did survive paints a grim picture. The lowest estimates place casualties at around 4,000 during the storm. Some records support numbers as high as 10,000. Today, their bodies are still melting out of the glaciers below.

## Avalanche: SECTION I – AVALANCHE HAZARDS
5-1. During winter months and throughout the year, snow covers the slopes. Steep, snow-covered terrain, in addition to mobility challenges and fall hazards, present the risk of avalanches. This hazard is easy to underestimate. It is not clearly visible, and, due to the complexity of the factors involved, there is a high degree of uncertainty, but the consequences of ignoring the hazard are high. Avalanches have taken the lives of more troops engaged in mountain warfare than all other terrain hazards combined.

5-2. An avalanche is an unstable snowpack, located on particularly angled terrain, where the bonds between layers or several layers of snow have broken, causing a mass of snow to slide downhill. When operating in the mountains during the winter, these instabilities in the snowpack may make large areas too dangerous for travel. These snow slopes may appear unthreatening but may have weak layers buried in the snowpack that can be easily triggered. Military elements may gravitate into avalanche terrain to avoid steeper technical terrain or enemy observation. Avalanches can generate incredible destructive force, destroying equipment, killing or burying Soldiers, and blocking key roads. Whether operating dismounted in high alpine terrain or as a mounted force in valley bottoms, units must be aware of avalanche hazards. They must incorporate the evaluation of avalanche danger into the planning process and learn to recognize and avoid hazardous terrain. (See figure 5-1, page 64.)

## Avalanche: AVALANCHE ELEMENTS
5-3. Avalanches occur when the weight of accumulated snow on a slope exceeds the cohesive forces that hold the snow in place. There are three elements needed to create an avalanche: an unstable snowpack, terrain steep enough to slide, and a trigger that initiates the failure of bonds holding the snow in place.

## Avalanche: SNOWPACK
5-4. Layers of snow accumulate with each new snow or wind event. Variation in temperature and atmospheric conditions form layers with different types of snow crystals. These layers are subject to changes in texture and strength throughout the winter, as they are exposed to sun, wind, and temperature changes. Each layer in the snowpack will have distinct characteristics. Strong layers are made up of well-bonded, small, rounded grains. Weak layers are made up of larger, angular, poorly bonded grains.

## Avalanche: Snowpack Concerns
5-5. Instabilities are weaknesses within the snowpack as described below (see figure 5-2):

- Weak layers—weak layers may collapse under load, initiating an avalanche. Poorly bonded crystals can act like ball bearings between stronger layers. Weak layers can change over time, becoming stronger, denser layers. This happens more quickly in temperatures above freezing. However, many avalanche cycles are due to weak layers that resist this change and retain their large grain size and

angular shape for long periods. These types of layers are known as “persistent” weak layers. These are common in prolonged colder temperatures.

- Weak bonds between layers—differences in the size and compositions of crystals between layers

inhibit bonding. Large differences between layers are the primary source of instability.

Bed surface—the bed surface is the surface over which a fracture and subsequent avalanche release occurs. Smooth, uniform sliding surfaces provide less friction to hold the snow in place. Examples are firm old snow layers, rain crusts, and sun crusts.

## Avalanche: Loading
![Figure 5-2. Snowpack layers](img:23-avalanche/fig-5-2.webp)

5-6. When instability exists in the snowpack, another important factor is the load placed on top of the weak layer. With the added weight of accumulated snow, it increases the shear forces within the snowpack:

- New snow—during snowstorms, snowfall will rapidly increase the forces placed on the snowpack. Snowfall of more than 2.5 centimeters (cm) (1 inch) of snow an hour or more than 30 cm (1 foot) of snow in 24 hours is considered critical loading. Avoid traveling in avalanche terrain during critical loading.

- Wind loading—sustained winds will transport snow from windward slopes and ridge tops, depositing it in sheltered features on leeward aspects. With high winds, even small deposits of new snow can produce significant loading. Top loading occurs when wind deposits snow over the top of a ridge. Side loading (or cross loading) occurs when snow is blown across the slope and deposited in gullies or on the leeward sides of ridges. Rain—rain rapidly increases the load on the snowpack and erodes bonds between snow crystals and layers. After a period of sustained cold where weak layers have been preserved in the snowpack, rain can produce dense, wet avalanches that are very destructive.

## Avalanche: TERRAIN FACTORS
5-7. Terrain is the second factor that determines if an avalanche can occur. The character of a snowpack and opportunities for whether instabilities develop are determined by its elevation and aspect. A slope’s characteristics—slope angle, shape, and physical characteristics—determine the likelihood of a slide.

Recognizing and avoiding avalanche terrain are the keys to managing avalanche risk.

## Avalanche: Elevation
5-8. Precipitation, temperature, and wind often vary significantly at different elevations. This results in differences in the snowpack, all of which influence the type, size, distribution, and sensitivity of avalanches.

5-9. Increased precipitation—as weather systems are lifted over mountains (orthographic lift), the air cools and can hold less moisture, causing additional precipitation at elevation. Increased precipitation causes increased loads on the snowpack.

5-10. Temperature—warming in the snowpack temporarily increases instability as bonds melt but improves stability when it re-freezes. Cooler temperatures at higher elevations inhibit stabilization. Instabilities can persist for much longer.

## Avalanche: Aspect to Wind
5-11. Sustained winds of 15 mph or higher transport snow from the windward aspect of the slope and deposit it in the form of wind slabs on the leeward slopes. Leeward slopes can accumulate three to five times the amount of snow that falls in sheltered locations. Slopes on the windward side have much of their new snow “scoured” off by the wind and are generally more stable than leeward slopes. Leeward slopes tend to form dangerous cornices and large, unstable slabs of snow.

## Avalanche: Aspect to Sun
5-12. Solar radiation causes drastic temperature swings in the snowpack. South-facing slopes can be dangerous on warm, sunny days immediately following storms. However, over time, these slopes stabilize as the added heat promotes bonding between layers. Snow on north-facing slopes does not stabilize as quickly and is more hazardous through most of the winter.

Note. This effect is reversed in the Southern Hemisphere.

## Avalanche: Start Zone Characteristics
5-13. The steepness of a slope, or its slope angle, is the primary factor that determines if an avalanche will occur. Most large avalanches occur on slopes between 30° and 45°. Slopes above 60° often do not build up significant quantities of snow because they are too steep. Snow will slide off as frequent, small sloughs.

Under most conditions, slopes below 25° do not slide. (See figure 5-3.)

![Figure 5-3. Slope angle](img:23-avalanche/fig-5-3.webp)

5-14. The shape of the slope will also affect the forces within the snowpack, concentrating tension in specific areas where it requires less force to trigger an avalanche. (See figure 5-4, page 68.)

Profile or shape—

Convex slopes. Slabs are most likely to fracture just below the bulge where stress is greatest.

Convex rollovers have little support and are often steeper than the surrounding slope, making them a likely trigger point.

Planar slopes. On these broad, smooth slopes, avalanches can happen anywhere. Slabs often fracture below cliff bands.

Concave slopes. These slopes provide a certain amount of support through compression at the base of the hollow, but they are still capable of avalanching, especially on large slopes.

- Anchoring—

Boulders, trees, and ledges act as anchors and help hold the snow in place until they are buried.

 Anchors can be areas of stress concentration because the snow upslope is held in place while the snow below or to the sides are pulled downhill by gravity. For this reason, anchors can be starting points for initial failure to occur, and fractures often run from tree to tree or rock to rock.

## Avalanche: Terrain Traps
![Figure 5-4. Slope shape](img:23-avalanche/fig-5-4.webp)

5-15. Terrain traps are features that increase the consequences of being caught in an avalanche. Examples include cliffs, gullies, creeks, lakes, trees, benches, or roads where debris will pile up, crevasses, or bergschrunds. Some terrain traps increase trauma during an avalanche. Others increase burial depth, making survival more unlikely.

## Avalanche: TRIGGERS
5-16. All avalanche triggers, whether caused by natural forces or human actions, are due to overloading the cohesive strength of the snowpack:

- Natural—avalanches may be triggered by new snow, falling rock, snow, ice, or smaller avalanches. Solar warming erodes bonds and can trigger avalanches. Naturally occurring avalanches are a critical warning sign that the conditions for an avalanche exist on similar slopes. If natural avalanches are possible, human triggered avalanches are likely.

- Human—most victims trigger the avalanches that kill them. People apply significant force to the snowpack. The impact of that force depends on snow density and distribution of force. A Soldier traveling on skis will not stress the snowpack as deep as a Soldier on foot will. Vehicles apply even more force. When a collapse occurs in a weak layer, it can propagate long distances up or across the slope.

## Avalanche: TYPES OF AVALANCHES
5-17. Avalanches can be categorized into two broad categories: loose snow and slab avalanches. (See figure 5-5, page 70.) Loose snow slides start with less snow and pick up more snow as they descend. Slab avalanches occur when one or more layers of cohesive snow break away as a unit and are generally more destructive.

5-18. There are many variations of these types, each with distinct characteristics. These characteristics are defined by the structures within the snowpack, how an avalanche is released, and where it is found in the terrain. These avalanche problems guide group discussions, route planning, terrain choices, and the observations made during snowpack evaluation.

## Avalanche: LOOSE-DRY AVALANCHES
5-19. A loose-dry avalanche is a release of dry, unconsolidated snow. These avalanches occur within layers of soft snow near the surface of the snowpack. Loose-dry avalanches start at a point and entrain snow as they move downhill, forming a fan-shaped avalanche. Other names for loose-dry avalanches include point-release avalanches or sluffs. Loose-dry avalanches can trigger slab avalanches that break into deeper snow layers.

Loose-dry avalanches are usually less dangerous than other avalanche problems but can be hazardous when combined with terrain traps, such as gullies, rocks, dense timber, cliffs, or crevasses. (See figure 5-5, page 70.)

## Avalanche: STORM SLAB AVALANCHES
5-20. A storm slab avalanche is a release of a soft cohesive layer (a slab) of new snow that breaks within the storm snow or on the old snow surface. Storm-slab problems last between a few hours and a few days but often stabilize quickly. (See figure 5-5, page 70.)

## Avalanche: WIND SLAB AVALANCHES
5-21. A wind slab avalanche is the release of a cohesive layer of snow (a slab) formed by the wind. Wind transports snow from the upwind sides of terrain features and deposits snow on the downwind side. Wind slabs are often smooth and rounded, sound hollow sometimes, and can range from soft to hard. Wind slabs form in specific areas and are confined to lee and cross-loaded terrain features. They can be avoided by sticking to sheltered or wind-scoured areas. (See figure 5-5, page 70.)

## Avalanche: PERSISTENT SLAB AVALANCHES
5-22. A persistent slab avalanche is a release of a cohesive layer of soft to hard snow (a slab) in the middle to upper snowpack, when the bond to an underlying persistent weak layer breaks. Persistent weak layers can continue to produce avalanches for days, weeks, or even months, making them challenging to manage. As additional snow and wind events build a thicker slab on top of the persistent weak layer, this avalanche problem may develop into a deep, persistent slab. They can be triggered by light loads and last weeks after the last storm. The slabs often propagate in surprising and unpredictable ways, making this problem difficult to predict and manage, and require a wide safety buffer to handle the uncertainty. The best ways to manage the risk from persistent slabs are to make conservative terrain choices. (See figure 5-5, page 70.)

Note. Storm slabs and wind slabs formed over persistent weak layers are also considered persistent slabs.

## Avalanche: DEEP, PERSISTENT SLAB AVALANCHES
5-23. A deep, persistent slab avalanche is a release of a thick, cohesive layer of hard snow (a slab), when the bond breaks between the slab and an underlying “persistent” weak layer, deep in the snowpack or near the ground. Deep, persistent slabs are difficult to trigger but are very destructive and dangerous because of the large mass of snow involved. Once developed, they can persist for months or an entire winter season. They are often triggered from areas where the snow is shallow and weak and are difficult to forecast and manage. (See figure 5-5, page 70.)

## Avalanche: WET-LOOSE AVALANCHES
5-24. A wet-loose avalanche is a release of wet, unconsolidated snow or slush. These avalanches occur within layers of wet snow near the surface of the snowpack but may quickly gouge into lower snowpack layers. Like dry-loose avalanches, they start at a point and entrain snow as they move downhill, forming a fan-shaped avalanche. They often carve channels or paths through the snowpack to which subsequent avalanches are constrained. They move slowly but can contain enough mass to cause significant damage to trees, cars, or buildings. To avoid wet-loose avalanches, travel when the snow surface is colder and stronger. Plan movements to avoid crossing on or under very steep slopes in the afternoon. Move to colder, shadier slopes

once the snow surface turns slushy. Avoid steep, sunlit slopes above terrain traps, cliffs, and long steep slopes. (See figure 5-5.)

## Avalanche: WET SLAB AVALANCHES
5-25. A wet slab avalanche is a release of a cohesive layer of wet snow (a slab) when the flow of liquid water weakens the bond between the slab and the surface below (snow or ground). They often occur during prolonged warming events or rain-on-snow events. Wet slabs can be very destructive. Avoid terrain where and when wet slab avalanche activity is expected. Give suspect slopes a wide safety buffer to handle the uncertainty. (See figure 5-5.)

## Avalanche: CORNICE FALL
5-26. Cornice fall is the release of an overhanging mass of snow that forms as the wind moves snow over a sharp terrain feature, such as a ridge, and deposits snow on the downwind (leeward) side. Cornices range in size from small wind lips of soft snow to large overhangs of hard snow that are 10 meters (30 feet) or taller.

They can break off suddenly. Even small cornices can have enough mass to be destructive and deadly.

Cornice fall can entrain loose surface snow or trigger slab avalanches. Cornices can never be trusted and avoiding them is necessary for safe travel. Stay well back from ridgeline areas with cornices. They often overhang the ridge edge; they can be triggered remotely. Avoid areas underneath cornices, especially during periods of significant warming. (See figure 5-5.)

## Avalanche: GLIDE (FULL DEPTH) AVALANCHES
5-27. A glide avalanche is a release of the entire snow cover at the ground surface. Glide avalanches can be composed of wet, moist, or almost entirely dry snow. They occur in very specific paths, where the slope is steep enough and the ground surface is smooth. They often occur on south-facing slopes during periods of strong sun and warm temperatures. They may be proceeded by full depth cracks (glide cracks), though the time between the appearance of a crack and an avalanche can vary between seconds and months. Predicting the release of glide avalanches is very challenging. Because they only occur on very specific slopes, avoidance is key to safe travel. Glide cracks and other glide avalanches are indicators. (See figure 5-5.)

## Avalanche: FORECASTS
5-28. In areas with extensive avalanche terrain and conditions, there are regional offices with staff members that monitor weather and snowpack conditions and produce forecasts to warn the public of avalanche hazards.

Forecasts are created using a combination of weather models, remote weather stations, snowpack data, and field observations. Forecasts use the North American Avalanche Danger Scale and add additional information regarding specific avalanche problems, location in the terrain, likelihood of triggering, and expected size.

## Avalanche: NORTH AMERICAN DANGER SCALE
5-29. The North American Avalanche Danger Scale is a tool used by avalanche forecasters to communicate the potential for avalanches to cause harm or injury to backcountry travelers. It converts the forecasters’ projections of the size and distribution of avalanches and likelihood of triggering into travel advice. (See figure 5-6.)

## Avalanche: AVALANCHE PROBLEM
5-30. In addition to the danger rating, forecasters publish a more detailed discussion of the avalanche problem. The avalanche problem describes the types of potential avalanche, the expected location based on aspect and elevation, the likelihood of triggering, and the expected size. Specific avalanche problems with similar ratings on the danger scale may present differently and require different terrain choices. (See figure 5-7, page 72.)

## Avalanche: USING THE FORECAST
5-31. The forecast is a starting point to guide planning for patrols or missions. In addition, planners should consult a record of the season’s conditions and current weather. Group discussion, identifying the hazard, communicating recent observations, and planning routes with clear decision points are all part of the decision-making framework used to manage avalanche risk.

## Avalanche: SECTION II – AVALANCHE RISK MANAGEMENT
5-32. More than any other hazard faced in the mountains, avalanches pose the most significant threat of a catastrophic accident for an entire element. There is inherent uncertainty in any forecast, even those produced by avalanche professionals, and the consequence of being caught in an avalanche are high. The best control measure is avoiding all avalanche terrain. However, this is not always possible. In some mountain ranges, even valleys, roads, and railways are threatened by avalanches when dangerous weather conditions are present. Key terrain and approaches to it may be in avalanche terrain. Much of the time, the snowpack may be stable and safe for travel, but without an understanding of specific hazards and their locations in the terrain, units can be dangerously exposed.

5-33. Education is the greatest tool for managing avalanche hazard. Unit commanders are responsible for implementing avalanche training. They must understand the hazards that Soldiers may be exposed to and ensure that subordinate leaders and Soldiers are prepared for those threats. Units should have designated unit members with formal avalanche training and education.

## Avalanche: UNIT-LEVEL RESPONSIBILITIES
5-34. Avalanche hazard shuts down large areas to maneuver. As part of their intelligence preparation of the battlefield, units need to analyze terrain, snowpack, and weather factors to develop a forecast for subordinate elements. Route planning, terrain selection, and risk decisions are all driven by a forecast.

5-35. When training in the United States, units may have access to avalanche forecasts being generated at avalanche centers in our geographic regions. These forecasts identify the avalanche problems and locations in the terrain and assign them a danger rating based off the likelihood of triggering them and their size. These forecasts guide route planning and help to evaluate the overall risk. To develop an operational capability, units must identify and develop key personnel that can evaluate the hazards using weather data and snowpack observations within their area of operations.

## Avalanche: MILITARY MOUNTAINEER RESPONSIBILITIES
5-36. Route planning and decision making are the primary tools to reduce exposure and avoid avalanche prone terrain and being caught in an avalanche. The following skills are critical for travel in avalanche terrain:

Terrain analysis—military mountaineers must be able to use information from an avalanche forecast to identify and inform commanders of hazardous slopes and safe corridors within their area of operations. They must also recognize terrain traps. To travel safely below avalanche terrain, they must estimate run-out zones (how far an avalanche may travel before it stops). This can vary and is determined by snow cover and snow depth.

Route selection—routes with terrain angles that are less than 30° and greater than 45° should be identified, and care must be taken to ensure routes are not threatened by overhead avalanche hazard.

The safest routes are on ridge tops, slightly on the windward side. Valley bottoms can provide safe

Critical weather analysis—changes in the following weather factors that increase loads on the snowpack or erode bonds will directly contribute to increasing avalanche danger:  New or recent wind loading.

Rapid rise in temperature.

Stability analysis—direct signs of instability, such as those noted below, should be viewed as clear evidence of immediate danger to Soldiers traveling in similar terrain:

Evidence of avalanching and recent avalanches on similar slopes.

Shooting cracks—a shooting crack is evidence of a slab or weak layer combination.  Whoomphing sounds—hollow sounds when moving through the snow. These are evidence of the collapse of a weak layer. On a steeper slope, a whoomph could trigger an avalanche.

- Deliberate triggering:

Types—avalanches can be triggered using explosives. Soldiers can use recoilless rifles, artillery, and mortars to trigger avalanches. Small charges of C4 explosive (1 to 2 kilograms) with a time fuse are effective if Soldiers can drop the charge to a point in the slope that triggers an avalanche.

 Uses—deliberate triggering can be used for force protection. Soldiers can trigger small avalanches so that larger avalanches do not threaten key positions and infrastructures. Soldiers can use avalanches to deny key terrain to the enemy, and to block roads and narrow passes.

Mountaineer Soldiers can use deliberate triggering of avalanches offensively to isolate elements that they desire to engage with direct fire and indirect fire assets.

Principles—understanding what cause avalanches and where they are likely to occur are useful for selecting the best triggering locations. Choose a point of maximum tension in the snowpack to detonate the charge.

Planning and supervising avalanche training—leaders must ensure that all Soldiers operating in avalanche terrain practice and rehearse individual avalanche protective measures. They must be capable of coordinating and leading the rescue if someone is caught in an avalanche.

## Avalanche: INDIVIDUAL RESPONSIBILITIES
5-37. All Soldiers operating in avalanche terrain must be proficient in basic avalanche tasks. They must be able to travel in the terrain, be proficient with all equipment, and well drilled in tasks for companion rescue.

Travel techniques—just as battle drills are rehearsed for tactical movement, troops moving in avalanche terrain must practice and rehearse movement techniques for limiting exposure to avalanche hazard by—  Having a planned and rehearsed communication strategy. Be deliberate when passing through

pockets of increased hazard.

Zipping up clothing and other layers.

Posting a spotter to observe the entire element as it passes through. Send one person across at a time. Stop on islands of safety. All Soldiers should travel in the same track. Set the track high, above potential start zones.

- Avalanche safety equipment—every Soldier operating in avalanche terrain should be equipped with the three basic items of avalanche rescue equipment. They should be trained and rehearsed in their use.

Shovel—to dig out the victim. It should be constructed of aluminum and collapsed or disassembled in a pack where it is protected and will not be lost but easily accessed. E-tools are insufficient for this purpose.

Transceiver—an active radio-beacon transceiver designed for finding people buried under snow. When turned on, it broadcasts a signal that other transceivers can locate with a range of about 30 meters.

Probe—a long pole used to confirm the location of an avalanche victim and to measure how deeply they are buried.

Survival—try to escape quickly out of the side of the avalanche. An alternative for if or when Soldiers are caught in the start zone and before debris gains speed is to arrest in the bed surface and let the snow flow past. If Soldiers are caught, they should discard equipment and attempt to swim to stay closer to the surface. If their head goes under the snow, they should shut their mouth and use their elbow to form an air pocket in front of their face by reaching across the body and grabbing the opposite shoulder strap of the rucksack. Many avalanche victims suffocate because their mouth and nose are plugged with snow. The opposite arm can be used to attempt to reach for the surface.

Although often difficult to tell surface direction when tumbling, many victims have been quickly found when a rescuer found a hand or body part sticking out of the snow. When the snow stops, try to relax. If Soldiers feel themselves about to pass out, they should not fight it. Once unconscious, respirations are shallower, pulse rates slower, and body temperature lower, all of which help to conserve oxygen.

Companion rescue—a fast, efficient transceiver search and shoveling are key to the survival of an avalanche victim. Avalanche rescue is covered in-depth in section III.

## Avalanche: SNOWPACK EVALUATIONS
5-38. Snowpack stability evaluation can be difficult in the absence of direct evidence, such as recent avalanches, whoomphing, or shooting cracks. It is also especially difficult when it has been several days since new loading has occurred (rain, new snow, wind-transported snow), several days without significant temperature changes, or in situations where a “persistent” weak layer exists in the snowpack. During these times, experienced Soldiers may use a multitude of tests to help determine stability and identify particularly dangerous terrain. These tests are limited, because their accuracy is localized to the vicinity and should only be used to determine where not to go. Typically conducted by master mountaineers, these tests require a high degree of skill and training both to perform and interpret; therefore, specific procedures are not covered in detail in this manual. Most of these tests require the digging of snow pits in safe locations that are specifically representative of terrain that is in question (same aspect and elevation). These snow pits can have a range of dimensions that can be modified depending upon the types and quantities of tests to be conducted; however, they are typically 2-meters across the slope by 1.5-meters up slope and dug to below the depth of any weak layers in question.

Layer identification—used to identify specific areas of concern within the snowpack, for example, layers with major differences in characteristics. This could be major differences in hardness, softness, gain size, and shape.

Shovel tilt—used to evaluate bonding between the most recent storm snow and the old snow surface.

Also used to identify thin weak layers not found during layer identification. Limited to the top 45 cm of the snowpack.

Compression test—used to locate weak layers in the top 1 meter of snow and to provide an indication of triggering potential on nearby slopes with similar conditions.

Extended column test—used to test for the potential of crack propagation of a pre-identified slab or weak layer combination in the upper 1 meter of the snowpack.

Propagation saw test—used to test for the potential of crack propagation of a pre-identified slab or weak layer combination that is greater than 1-meter deep.

Rutschblock—used to test stability of pre-identified weak layers up to 1-meter deep with skis.

Explosives—used to test slopes and trigger slides in the top layers of the snowpack. Placement in the sensitive regions of the start zone is critical for getting a reaction.

- Hasty tests (hand shear, pole plants, test slopes)—nonstandardized tests that can be used quickly while traveling in the terrain to determine signs of instability or to recognize when conditions are changing.

5-39. Avalanches pose a significant threat to Soldiers operating in mountainous terrain. Unlike fall hazards, or weather conditions, avalanche hazard is not easily observed. If units wish to maintain the ability to conduct operations during winter months or in regions of the world with the possibility of snow throughout the year, they must develop the capability to assess avalanche hazard. This means identifying individuals for further training and education, incorporating avalanche forecasts into the planning process, and ensuring that all Soldiers operating in the terrain are equipped and trained for the avalanche threat.

## Avalanche: SECTION III – AVALANCHE SEARCH AND RESCUE
5-40. The primary risk mitigation tool in avalanche terrain is avoidance. In the planning phase, Soldiers should make every effort to patrol routes that avoid avalanche hazards. The planning phase includes having clear decision points from which to evaluate conditions and viable alternate routes. However, when evaluating an avalanche hazard, there is often a high degree of uncertainty. When mistakes are made, the consequences are high. Avalanches produce incredible energy. Soldiers caught in an avalanche may be injured or killed by blunt force trauma from collisions with rock, ice, and trees. If they come to rest with an intact air pocket, they may succumb to hypothermia or shock. However, most avalanche victims die from asphyxiation. Without an air pocket, unconsciousness may come in minutes, followed rapidly by permeant brain injury and death. Evidence has shown that after 15 minutes, the chances of surviving complete burial decreases from 90 percent to 30 percent. It is critical that all Soldiers operating in avalanche terrain are equipped with avalanche safety equipment and are drilled in companion rescue.

## Avalanche: AVALANCHE SEARCH
5-41. All individuals traveling into terrain in which avalanches could possibly occur must be familiar with and carry the following equipment: avalanche transceiver, lightweight shovel, and collapsible probe. Soldiers must be adequately trained in their use during avalanche rescue.

## Avalanche: TRANSCEIVER
5-42. Avalanche transceivers (beacons) are radio transceivers that emit a pulsed signal. (See figure 5-8, page 76.) When turned on, they broadcast a signal that other transceivers can locate within a range of approximately 30 meters. Other transceivers can be switched into a search mode to receive that signal and find transceivers buried under the snow. Soldiers should wear their transceiver over the top of their base layers but underneath insulating layers that may be removed or added throughout the course of the day.

Soldiers should always wear their transceiver below a layer to protect it in case of an avalanche.

5-43. Electronic interference has been shown to interfere with the range and accuracy of avalanche transceivers, especially in search mode. Manufacturers recommend that transceivers are worn 30 cm (12 inches) away from any radios, cell phones, and GPS devices to ensure that there is no metal or reflective material (such as meals, ready to eat wrappers) in the area around where the device is worn. This may not be possible when wearing combat equipment, but in a training environment, every effort should be made to adhere to manufacturer recommendations. In search mode, manufacturers recommend that Soldiers turn off all other devices and keep the transceiver at least 50 cm from other devices. If possible, Soldiers with radios and active electronic equipment should delegate transceiver searches to those without electronic equipment and radios.

## Avalanche: SHOVEL
5-44. Shovels suitable for avalanche rescue are lightweight and made of aluminum. They are durable enough to cut through dense avalanche debris but light enough to for Soldiers to carry while skiing or mountaineering.

Soldiers can collapse or disassemble their shovel to carry it inside their pack. Soldiers should pack their transceiver, so it is quickly accessible. (See figure 5-8, page 76.)

## Avalanche: PROBE
5-45. Probes are used to confirm the location of a burial before digging. Probes are approximately 3-meters long. They collapse to fit disassembled inside a pack but deploy quickly with a pull of the connecting cable.

They should lock into place automatically. Soldiers should carry their probes inside their pack in a location where it is protected and immediately accessible.

## Avalanche: GROUP CHECKS
5-46. All Soldiers must be familiar with the group avalanche equipment functions check process before movement into avalanche terrain. All leaders must be familiar with conducting these functions checks to ensure that their unit is prepared to respond to an avalanche burial event. All Soldiers must be familiar with avalanche response actions. SOPs and rehearsals should be conducted in the same manner as battle drills.

## Avalanche: PRECOMBAT CHECKS
5-47. Soldiers must be trained and familiar with each equipment item and how it functions. Individual and team rehearsals, transceiver checks, and rescue drills are necessary:

Check that individual rescue equipment is functional and stowed appropriately.

Conduct verbal rehearsals of all avalanche tasks, movement through danger areas, and avalanche rescue.

## Avalanche: TRANSCEIVER CHECK
5-48. A group check confirms that each member of the element knows how to turn on the device and switch between modes, ensures that every device can receive and search for a signal, and that every device transmits a signal. Manufacturers recommended procedures for group checks might vary.

Locate an area away from other groups that may cause conflicting signals. Stand in a half circle an arm’s length or more apart, with the leader a few meters away in the middle of the group.

Turn transceivers on. As the transceivers power on, each individual confirms that the transceiver runs a self-check, and the display is ok. Note battery strength and report it aloud to the group. For

patrols or missions lasting less than 24 hours, battery life should be a minimum of 50 percent. For multi-day missions, each Soldier should begin with fresh batteries.

Switch all transceivers to search mode. Silence indicates that everyone is ready and ensures that no transmitting units are in packs or sleds.

The leader then switches to transmit/send and checks that each group member can receive a signal by walking toward each group member one by one while group members read their display aloud.

Turn all units to transmit. The leader ensures that group members stow their unit for the day, secured under an outside layer, and 30 cm from a cell phone or radio.

The leader returns to Search mode and confirms all group members are transmitting. The check-leader then switches to transmit mode and confirms to at least one group member, who also verifies that the leader’s device is transmitting and properly stowed.

## Avalanche: AVALANCHE RESCUE
5-49. Units must be prepared to conduct avalanche rescue when operating in avalanche terrain. Avalanches, especially those involving Soldier burial, will significantly affect tactical operations. Commanders must provide clear guidance regarding subordinate unit response to avalanche burial events during tactical operations. This guidance should cover avalanche victim rescue priority in relation to mission conduct, affected unit response SOPs, higher unit response capabilities and SOPs, and reporting expectations.

Maneuver units must be capable of finding and digging out victims quickly. Unit commanders must plan for and be prepared to organize rescue operations for avalanche mass casualty events, as well as deliberate body location and recovery.

## Avalanche: ORGANIZING THE RESCUE
5-50. The normal chain of command may be broken if a leader is buried or separated. Everyone should be prepared to organize and take charge of a rescue. Every Soldier must be prepared to make critical decisions and tasks for an efficient and successful rescue.

- Assess safety—determine if there is further avalanche hazard. If there is risk of secondary avalanches (hangfire), limit the number of personnel exposed to further hazard. Post a watch and a relay to maintain communications.

- Headcount—get an accurate headcount to find out how many Soldiers are buried.

Report or call for help—do not waste time; report the accident to get resources moving if a Soldier needs them. If possible, delegate and leave a relay up high to maintain communications. Do not let radio operators in the rear or at a dispatch delay or interfere with the rescue.

Switch all transceivers to Search mode—physically ensure that all unit members have switched their transceivers to Search mode to avoid disrupting the searchers. Just one rescuer still transmitting will confuse the searchers and waste precious time.

- Assign roles—in small teams, everyone may need to help with the transceiver search. In a larger

group, assign some to search and others to prepare probes and shovels.

- Determine where to search—the spotter should have been able to observe a point last seen. Search the fall line below the point last seen. Search the run-out zone and other areas of deposition, such as terrain traps. Search in line with equipment clues if they exist.

## Avalanche: TRANSCEIVER SEARCH
5-51. Transceiver searches involve three distinct phases: signal search, course search, and fine search.

Signal search—begin searching for a signal. Get all the searchers online with 40-meter spacing to ensure that the search ranges overlap across the entire search area. If alone, or covering a very wide search area, use a search pattern that covers the entire path. Movement should be rapid but deliberate. Keep eyes up, searching for physical clues. (See figure 5-9, page 78.) Course search—once a signal is found, the transceivers change tones and light up with a distance and direction. Slow down and follow the arrow. Communicate with the other searchers, calling out the distance reading. At 10 meters, slow down even further. Ensure the transceiver aligns with the direction arrow. Continue moving slowly and bring the transceiver lower and closer to the surface of the snow. Avoid sudden movements or changes in orientation.

Fine search—inside 3 meters, transition to a fine search. The transceiver should be moving in a plane just above the surface of the snow. Maintain orientation throughout the rest of the search. Do not twist or turn it. Continue the azimuth of approach until the numbers start to rise. Return to the low point, and search side to side, far enough to see the numbers rise, returning to the point with the lowest number.

## Avalanche: PROBING
![Figure 5-9. Signal search](img:23-avalanche/fig-5-9.webp)

5-52. Mark the lowest point with a foot or hand. Probe at the low point, perpendicular to the snow surface.

Probing must be conducted with both hands. (See figure 5-10.) If no strike is felt, probe again 2 cm uphill and continue to probe in a spiral search pattern. Once a probe strike is achieved, do not remove the probe.

This probe is a valuable reference point that must be visible to ensure Soldiers do not waste effort and energy during shoveling.

## Avalanche: SHOVELING
![Figure 5-10. Probing](img:23-avalanche/fig-5-10.webp)

5-53. Upon probe strike, take 1 large step downhill, 2 steps for a deep burial (greater than 2 meters). Rescuers should excavate snow from the side so that they are not lifting snow out of a hole. They should dig towards the probe and throw the snow far away while shoveling as fast and hard as possible. If there is a large group, rotate personnel often. Those who are not shoveling in the hole should continue to move snow out of the way and widen and improve the platform the others are creating. Everyone must prepare to switch out often so that those shoveling are doing so at maximum capacity. (See figure 5-11, page 80.)

## Avalanche: PATIENT CARE
![Figure 5-11. Shoveling](img:23-avalanche/fig-5-11.webp)

5-54. Work to clear the victim’s airway as soon as possible. Provide first aid and CPR as required. With long burials where there is a risk of hypothermia, move the patient gently. Move the patient to safe terrain and begin planning for evacuation.

## Avalanche: PROBE SEARCHES
5-55. Avalanche burials longer than 15 minutes can result in permanent brain damage and death. If victims are going to be saved, it is critical that it is done quickly. If the Soldier is not wearing a transceiver, the likelihood of finding them in time for survival is small.

5-56. If transceivers are unavailable, it is likely there are no probes available either. Soldiers can use their ski poles with the basket removed to begin initial probing attempts. If there is any hope of success, searchers must focus their efforts on the most probable locations.

Start the search from the point last seen.

Look for equipment clues. Gloves, hats, ski poles, packs—all may give clues to the trajectory of the victim or may still be attached. Pull each out of the snow to confirm.

- Most buried victims are carried to the place of greatest deposition, usually the toe of the slide. Any terrain features that catch and hold avalanche debris, such as outside curves, are also likely locations.

- Vegetation, rocks, and other obstacles act as strainers and may catch or slow the victim.

## Avalanche: PROBE LINES
5-57. For body recovery of individuals without transceivers, it may be necessary to locate them using a probe line. A probe line is a systematic search, online, through the entire debris field. It is a slow process and requires many rescuers. A probe line is not appropriate when there is still an overhead avalanche hazard.

5-58. There are two distinct probing methods: coarse probe and fine probe. Coarse probing is intended to be fast, with wide spacing of probe insertions. Fine probing involves close-spaced probing with an emphasis on thoroughness. The coarse-probe technique has about a 70-percent chance of locating the victim on a given pass, while the fine probe has closer to a 100-percent chance of locating the victim.
