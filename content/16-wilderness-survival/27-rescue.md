## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation
**Chapter 15**

A unit’s capability to conduct mountain rescue and evacuation is a critical part of mountain operations. Swift and efficient rescue can mean the difference between success and failure of a mission or even the survival of a casualty. In mountainous terrain, even relatively minor injuries may require evacuation. The type of injury, distance to be moved, terrain, training level of the team, and litter capabilities determine the evacuation technique chosen. Air evacuation is preferred. However, the weather, tactical situation, or operational ceiling of the aircraft may make this impossible. Therefore, it is imperative that all personnel are trained in mountain evacuation techniques and are self-sufficient. Casualties should be triaged before evacuation.

The most experienced medical personnel available perform triage (physician, physician’s assistant, or medic).

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: SECTION I – RESCUE OPERATIONS
15-1. Every commander should have a MEDEVAC plan before undertaking an operation. This plan should have dedicated assets (primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency) in place so as not to rely on a single evacuation platform or method. Rotary-wing aircraft may be available to remove casualties from cliffs or inaccessible sites and to quickly transport casualties to a medical treatment facility. However, high altitudes and gusting winds reduce the ability of fixed-wing or rotary-wing aircraft to assist in operations. Therefore, relying solely on aircraft or specialized equipment is a poor substitute for careful planning.

15-2. The two types of rescue operations encountered by units operating in mountainous environments are deliberate and improvised.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: DELIBERATE
15-3. Deliberate rescue is when the mission objective itself is a rescue operation, thus allowing time for focused planning and the use of specialized rescue specific equipment.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: IMPROVISED
15-4. Improvised rescue situations are encountered when a casualty is sustained during normal operations and conducting a rescue was not the intended objective. Even improvised rescue operations should be practiced and rehearsed to minimize the negative effects on mission capability. All operations should have a CASEVAC plan developed before an emergency arises (plan for the worst and hope for the best). Ensure that the MEDEVAC plan is well thought out and comprehensive. These plans must be understood by all who may be involved in a potential rescue.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: PLANNING
15-5. The rescue tasks must be identified by obtaining the following information:

Terrain features and with respect to the location of the casualties.

Tactical situation.

- Number of casualties by precedence (urgent, priority, routine, convenience).

- Number of casualties by type (litter or ambulatory) and the nature of their injuries.

- Any assets available to aid in security, rescue, treatment, and evacuation.

- Additional personnel or equipment required for the rescue operation.

- Any circumstances that may require specialized skills.

- Weather conditions.

- Difficulty of the terrain.

Time available.

If treatment can be provided at the scene or if the casualty requires movement to a safer location.

15-6. Route selection when planning mountain CASEVAC often depends upon technical capabilities. In some cases, trained personnel and equipment available for low- or high-angle rescue may allow for safer, less physically demanding operations. A lack of these resources and capabilities may require traveling longer distances around steeper terrain, greatly increasing the burden on rescuers.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: CONSIDERATIONS
15-7. Time considerations can cause a leader to compromise planning stages or treatment (beyond first aid).

Make a realistic estimate of time available as quickly as possible to determine the action time remaining. The key elements are the casualty’s condition and ability to endure the environment. Time available is a balance between these conditions and the resources and personnel available.

15-8. There are many additional considerations for the rescue team during deliberate rescue:

Typically, team members must distribute and carry all equipment, including hot food and drinks, stoves, sleeping bags, bivouac equipment, warm clothes, ropes, and stretchers.

Evacuation routes often require extensive preparation and can involve establishing anchor points, belays, fixed ropes, lowers, and raises.

- Altitude and visibility can greatly affect the team as well as the casualty. Maximum use of secure,

reliable trails or roads is essential.

Protection from the elements, such as blankets, tarps, warming layers and rain gear, can be vital.

Even a mild rain can complicate an otherwise simple rescue. In higher altitudes, extreme cold or strong winds can greatly increase the rescue’s time and complexity.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: GENERAL STEPS
15-9. Regardless of whether a rescue is deliberate or improvised, the following steps must be taken upon arrival at the rescue site to accomplish a safe and efficient rescue operation:

- Assume command—one person and one person only is in charge.

Scene safety—prevent further injuries to the casualty and to others. Explore all options for casualty access prior to committing to one. Use reasonable care in reaching the casualty. Rescuers must not become casualties.

Immediately ensure the casualty has an open airway, control serious bleeding, and protect the casualty from further harm and from environmental hazards. If the casualty is unconscious, continually monitor for a pulse.

- Do not move the casualty until determining the extent of injuries unless necessary to prevent further

injury or if the casualty is in a dangerous or exposed location.

- Do nothing more until the situation has been thoroughly considered. Resist the urge for action. Speed is less important than correct actions and decision making. The decisions made on scene drive the rest of the rescue (for example, casualty rigging, packaging, route selection, anchors, and rope systems).

- Decide whether to evacuate with available resources or to send or call for help. Speed in getting to the next level of care must be balanced against the probability of further injury. This is especially crucial if working with inexperienced personnel, insufficient equipment, or terrain or weather hazards.

- When the evacuation route is long and arduous, a series of litter relay points or stations should be established, if possible. These help to manage the welfare of both the casualty and the rescue team. These stations should be staffed with the medical personnel and equipment necessary to provide proper treatment.

- Helicopters or heated vehicles, if available, should be used for evacuation. While the use of aircraft or vehicles is preferred and can expedite a rescue operation, evacuation of a seriously wounded Soldier should never be delayed awaiting aircraft, vehicle, or a change in weather.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: SPECIALIZED TRAINING
15-10. Rescue team members must receive basic instruction in military mountaineering and first aid, prior to any training and rehearsals for mountain rescue and evacuation. Individual mobility skills, knowledge of knots and basic rope systems, and the ability to sustain and protect themselves from the environment are

essential. Once formal evacuation training has been received, proficiency can be maintained by periodic integration with other concurrent training exercises. Scenarios that require simple casualty movement techniques, with and without litters, as well as low-angle slope evacuations (see section II) can easily be created. Scenarios are added when time becomes available during or following other training exercises. These opportunities allow commanders to quickly evaluate a unit’s preparedness to carry out basic rescues if needed, refresh level 1 skills, and help to reinforce unit teamwork and problem solving. Basic military mountaineers, at a minimum, should oversee this training. Medical personnel should be present. Rescues in complex 4th- and 5th-class terrain, whether real or simulated, require advanced-level training and skills, and they must be led by level 2 and level 3 mountaineers.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: PROLONGED FIELD CARE
15-11. The mountain environment is defined by austere and remote conditions. This environment, combined with the difficulty of patient movement, creates situations that can extend patient management over many hours or days. Prolonged field care is the continued delivery of medical care prior to patient movement beyond the holding capability of that role of care (JP 4-02).

15-12. Medical personnel with standard training may be required to perform beyond their skillset. Specific training should be conducted to prepare medical and nonmedical personnel for prolonged field care situations.

Tactical combat casualty care uses protocols aimed primarily at preventing mortality in the first few hours of treatment, prior to arrival at surgical resuscitative care. However, patients in the mountains lacking fast and immediate evacuation are often at risk for complications, such as sepsis, multi-organ system failure, respiratory compromise, and other serious conditions. Prolonged field care begins with the sound principles of tactical combat casualty care, but when care is extended over hours or days, treatment must continuously be re-evaluated. Diligence is necessary to minimize the effect of potentially harmful interventions and to prevent, recognize, and treat conditions that may develop over time.

15-13. Critical patients ultimately require care in a hospital with surgery, blood products, an intensive care unit, and advanced imaging. A combination of rescue skills training and prolonged field care skills are necessary to make this possible.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: SECTION II – CASUALTY MOVEMENT TECHNIQUES
15-14. Casualties in the mountains can be moved and transported in a variety of ways, from simple manual field carries to high-angle evacuations using litters and complex rope systems. The type of injury, distance to be moved, time available, and resources will determine the best technique for a given situation.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: MOVING CASUALTIES WITHOUT LITTERS
15-15. There are varieties of circumstances where casualties may move, be moved, or be carried without the use of litters. This could be a result of sustaining relatively minor injuries, not having a litter available, or not having the available personnel for litter transport. Alternatively, these could involve moving a casualty in technical terrain that is not suited for transport in a litter. If casualties are able, they can walk, with one or more rescuers walking alongside, to provide physical support. A rescuer close behind can help in difficult terrain. Have team members ahead to select the easiest route and remove loose branches and other obstacles.

Ski poles may be helpful if they are available. In situations where the casualty cannot walk, simple techniques described in the Soldier’s Manual of Common Tasks (fireman’s carry, two-hand, four-hand, saddleback, piggyback, pistol belt, and poncho litter) may be useful. However, rugged mountainous terrain often demands other techniques. The most basic one-man carries include the webbing or rope carry, the rope coil carry, and the rucksack carry.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: NYLON WEBBING OR ROPE CARRY
15-16. A 4.5-meter piece of webbing or rope can be used to improvise a buddy carry system. (See figure 15-1, page 356.) Place the middle of the webbing around the casualty’s back and under the shoulders, crossing in front of the chest. Place the ends over the carrier’s shoulders (from back to front). Bring the ends alongside the carrier’s ribs, through the casualty’s crotch, and around the outside of the casualty’s thighs. The carrier then ties the ends of the webbing together around the waste with a square knot.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: ROPE COIL CARRY
15-17. A dynamic or static rope can also be used as an improvised buddy carry system. (See figure 15-2.) Coil the length of rope using the mountain coil technique, sizing the loops to fit from the casualty’s armpits to crotch, and then separate the coil in half to form a pair of loops. Place the casualty’s legs through the loops and slip the upper part of the loops over the carrier’s shoulders. A short piece of webbing can be used to tie these loops together.

Then the carrier wears the backpack as usual. This requires the contents of the carrier’s pack to be carried by another Soldier.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: TANDEM RAPPEL
15-19. Rappelling with a casualty is not often the first choice in rescue. It is typically preferable to lower a casualty with a rescuer during descents because it allows the rescuer to focus on controlling the casualty rather than having to control both the casualty and a rappel. However, in the case of a sturdy, conscious patient with nonlife-threatening injuries, it is possible to rappel together with a rescuer. To do this, a rappel extension can be created using a 48-inch sling or cordelette as shown below to allow both the casualty and rescuer to rappel from the same device but slightly offset from one another. ## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: ROPED MOVEMENTS
15-20. Casualties can also be placed in a seat or improvised harness and lowered or hauled through steep terrain using the techniques described in chapter 12. This may be done with ambulatory patients or may be necessary in situations where an injured Soldier must be moved from a cliff to an alternate location where packaging in a litter is more easily accomplished. This may require considerable assistance from rescuers or other Soldiers. It should only be attempted when necessary and after any immediate life-threatening injuries have been treated. Remember the more complex the technical rope systems become, the more time consuming they tend to be, increasing exposure and vulnerability to enemy attack.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: MOVING CASUALTIES IN LITTERS
15-21. Usually, casualties with significant injuries in the mountains need to be evacuated with the use of a litter. Several types of litters are available for transporting casualties in rough terrain.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: MANUFACTURED LITTERS
15-22. The following litters are readily available to mountaineering units:

The poleless, nonrigid litter is lightweight and readily available but is the least favored litter. It does little to protect the patient’s injuries and is not appropriate for attachment to any rope system.

The standard collapsible litter is readily available in all units and may be rigged for movement over rough mountainous terrain. However, it is not suitable for carry as a contingency litter due to its bulk and weight.

- A rigid, metal-framed, basket-style litter that is rated for hoist operations is very well-suited for moving casualties through mountainous environments. These litters provide excellent patient protection and spinal immobilization, and when rigged properly, can readily attach to a variety of technical rope systems. The drawbacks to this type of litter are that it is heavy and is not designed to slide over surfaces. A single-wheel attachment is available, which vastly increases the efficiency of movement over moderate terrain but comes at the cost of an even greater weight to be carried. The patient rescue and recovery collapsible litter system is a lightweight, semirigid, rollout plastic litter that can also be rigged to meet rating requirements for hoist operations. The most commonly issued and used version in mountaineering is the rescue litter, which provides excellent patient support and protection. This is the preferred litter for mountain operations due to its versatility and relatively low weight. However, it is not a spinal immobilization device. A backboard must be used with this system for patients who have injuries to the shoulder area. This system will accommodate long and short backboards, scoop stretchers, and most other immobilization equipment.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: IMPROVISED LITTERS
15-23. Litters can be improvised in the field using several techniques, but all of them require construction materials and time, generally offer less patient protection, and are more prone to breakage.

15-24. Litters can be made by securing poles inside blankets, tarps, ponchos, jackets, shirts, sacks, or bags.

Poles can be improvised from strong branches, tent supports, skis, and other similar items.

15-25. If poles cannot be found, a large item, such as a blanket, can be rolled from both sides toward the center. Then the rolls can be used to obtain a firm grip to carry the casualty. If a poncho is used, the hood must be up and under the victim, not dragging on the ground.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: PATIENT PACKAGING
15-26. Casualties must be packaged and secured to litters before movement in rough terrain. Proper packaging should achieve stabilization, immobilization, protection from hypothermia, and preparation for transport. Physically strapping a person into a litter is relatively easy but making it comfortable and effective can be a challenge. The needs of litter bound casualties are great and should not be overlooked or underestimated. The decisions made during initial packaging will affect the physical and mental outcomes for the casualty in the end.

15-27. The rescuer’s goals are as follows:

Package the casualty to avoid causing additional injury.

Ensure the casualty’s comfort and warmth with focus on preventing hypothermia.

Immobilize the casualty’s entire body in such a way as to allow continued assessment during transport.

Package the casualty neatly so that the litter can be moved easily and safely.

Ensure that casualties are safe during transport by securing them within the litter and belaying the litter as necessary.

15-28. Rescuers follow the same basic principles of the cold-weather clothing system to create a hypothermia wrap (hypo-wrap). The fundamental goal is to reduce heat loss. The outermost layer should be a VB to protect the casualty and the insulating layers from the environment. Use a ground pad to protect against conductive heat loss using a closed cell or inflatable foam pad. A closed-cell foam sleep pad is preferable because it will not rupture, and it can be cut into pieces for additional medical treatments (such as splints and neck braces).

Begin layering with available insulating materials. The casualty’s equipment should be used first before sacrificing clothing or sleep systems from non-injured Soldiers. Rescuers put the casualty into the wrap and place additional layers of insulation on top of the casualty.

15-29. Consider protecting the insulating layers from casualties themselves if there is a possibility they could become incontinent. A trash bag or waterproof bag with leg holes cut in it can be filled with socks or T-shirts to form a diaper. Wrap the casualty as completely as possible, leaving only necessary areas exposed. When available, hot water bottles should be added and placed by the casualty’s axillary areas (armpits, groin) and the neck to provide active rewarming.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: MOVEMENT TECHNIQUES
15-30. ATP 4-02.13 covers general principles of handling and moving with litters—principles that apply in all terrain. However, in rugged mountainous terrain, teams must often move considerably slower. Extensive personnel may be required to ensure adequate rest and safety. Advanced teams may be useful for route selection and clearing.

15-31. For longer movements, it can be useful to add slings or cordelettes to the litter rim or handles for each litter bearer. These slings can be passed over the shoulders to the opposite hand, allowing each bearer to push down with the hand opposite the litter. This can help to reduce strain on the arm and hand carrying the litter, as well as providing some height adjustment.

15-32. In severely uneven terrain, such as talus or boulder fields, the pass-through technique can make movement safer for both the casualty and rescuers, provided extra personnel are available. To conduct the pass-through technique, simply have extra litter bearers take up static positions in the terrain ahead of the litter and as close to one another as possible. The litter is then passed forward into the hands of the extra litter bearers. As soon as litter bearers in the rear become obsolete, they move around and take up a new position further ahead in the direction of travel. They then wait until the litter reaches them and repeat the technique.

This allows rescuers to remain still as the litter is transported, taking advantage of secure footing and stable positions. In this way, the litter is less likely to be dropped and litter bearers are less likely to sustain an injury attempting to move and carry the heavy load at the same time.

15-33. If a litter can be dragged, attaching ropes may make doing so easier and more controllable. Ropes can be attached to the front of the litter for dragging purposes as well as to the rear of the litter for use with a brakeman to slow the speed of the litter on gentle descents. This technique can speed up evacuation in conditions that allow for it, such as snow-covered terrain. (See figure 15-4.)

15-34. On flat or low-angle snow-covered terrain, two ropes can be attached to the corners of the litter with girth hitches to allow up to four Soldiers to pull, brake, and steer the litter. This enables moving up, down, and traversing slopes with better control to prevent the litter from tipping over.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: LOW-ANGLE RESCUE
15-35. Low-angle rescue can generally be categorized by roped litter evacuations on low to moderately steep slopes where much of the weight of the casualty and rescuer is still supported by the ground. In this terrain, the litter slides along the slope, either up or down, depending on the direction of travel. The rope pathway should be cleared prior to casualty movement. All precautions must be taken to keep the litter within the fall line of gravity to eliminate pendulum swings. In all situations, the rescue team should strive to solve the terrain problem safely with the least technical solution.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: ROPE ATTACHMENT
15-36. Rescue litters are generally oriented vertically during low-angle slope evacuations. (See figure 15-5.) The rescue rope is attached to a vertical attachment point at the head of the litter with a locking carabiner or carabiners. The rope is connected at the head of the litter for comfort and for ensuring that blood does not pool at the casualty’s head during transport.

15-37. Many techniques can be used to create a vertical attachment point at the head of the litter. (See figure 15-6, page 362.) Techniques may be improvised, based on the type of litter and available equipment.

Ensure that this fixed loop attachment point connects multiple components of the litter for redundancy. In the case of the patient rescue and recovery system, use the short length of rope that comes with the system and follow the manufacturer’s instructions for creating this.

15-38. The end of the rescue rope can then be attached to this point with a figure eight on a bight and connected with a locking carabiner. Ropes used for rescues should be static (low stretch) and should have a minimum breaking strength of 30 kilonewtons (kNs).

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: BELAYS
15-39. In some situations, the rescue rope can be used to simply provide a tight belay for the casualty while litter teams carry a litter up or down slopes. Many of the various belay techniques described in chapter 10 are appropriate for this; however, the terrain must be gentle enough that there is no falling hazard to members of the litter team.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: ATTENDANTS
15-40. In steeper terrain where lowering or raising the litter from above is necessary, there is often a falling hazard for the litter team. If rescuers cannot safely walk beside the litter, it is typically advisable to use a single rescuer attached to the system as an attendant. This attendant’s primary responsibility is to ensure the litter and casualty do not roll over, become hung up on terrain obstacles, or create slack in the system.

15-41. One technique is to attach the rescue rope to the litter head using a bowline with a tail that is long enough to reach the bottom of the litter when stretched out. The long tail is then be used by a litter attendant as an attachment point to the system in situations where it is necessary. (See figure 15-7.) This can also be achieved using a figure eight on a bight with enough rope behind the knot to extend this distance.

15-42. The attendant can attach directly into the end of this rope with another figure eight on a bight attached to a locking carabiner in the harness or by tying directly in with a rerouted figure eight. An autoblock or prusik can also be attached to this strand of rope to allow the attendant to adjust position in relation to the litter and hang completely from the system. Occasionally but less often, two attendants may be required to manage the litter.

15-43. These methods use some of the rope that is available for the rescue. In cases where the length of available rope is limited, it may be prudent to improvise the attendant’s attachment using other materials, such as a cordelette.

![Figure 15-7. Litter rigged for lowering with long-tailed bowline and attendant attached](img:27-rescue/fig-15-7.webp)

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: LOWERING
15-44. The litter and attendant can be lowered using various lowering techniques described in chapter 12.

Select or construct extremely solid anchors, tie off the end of the rope to close the system, and apply appropriate hands-free backups. The rope pathway should be cleared prior to casualty movement, and all precautions must be taken to keep the litter within the fall line of gravity to eliminate pendulum swings. A Soldier secured safely near the edge can often be helpful to relay commands and help control the speed of the lower. Avoid rope pathways with sharp rock edges or loose terrain with rockfall potential.

15-45. If it is necessary to lower the litter and attendant multiple rope lengths, multiple anchor stations and lowering teams may be required to achieve this. Complex situations such as this require excellent leadership, terrain assessment, and communication.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: RAISING
15-46. The litter and attendant can be raised through steep terrain using the various hauling techniques described in chapter 12. This could be done by a hauling team from above using main force or by creating MA using one of the systems previously described. If using main force, a prusik or other friction hitch should be attached to the rope and anchored as a progress capture. An additional member on the haul team must be dedicated to managing the prusik during this method. If using MA, the hauling team must pull slow and steady and should be extremely aware of the loss of feel for the load. In either case, the rope pathway should be cleared prior to casualty movement, and all precautions must be taken to keep the litter within the fall line of gravity to eliminate pendulum swings. A Soldier secured safely near the edge can often be helpful to relay commands and help control the speed of the haul. Avoid hauling in terrain with sharp rock edges or loose terrain with rockfall potential.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: HIGH-ANGLE RESCUE
15-47. High-angle rescue generally refers to roped evacuations down steep cliffs and 5th-class terrain.

High-angle rescue requires formal training, experience, excellent judgement, and good technical problem-solving skills. It often requires a substantial amount of time. Only level 2 and 3 military mountaineers should only conduct high-angle rescue, when necessary. There are often many variables involved in high-angle rescue, and thus, there is no prescriptive set way to deal with the many challenges that can be encountered. Because a combination of skills and techniques may ultimately be required, including climbing techniques, belays, rappels, hauling and lowering methods, or even the use of high lines, a comprehensive focus is well beyond the scope of this training circular. Rather, this section will focus on some of the fundamental principles and commonly used tools and techniques.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: LITTER RIGGING
15-48. Whether a rescue litter or a rigid metal basket is used for a high-angle evacuation, the rigging is similar. The litter can be rigged for horizontal orientation, like a helicopter hoist configuration, or it can be rigged for a vertical orientation the same as described for lower-angle evacuations. In many cases, it is ideal to package the casualty in a manufactured or improvised harness. This allows the harness to connect to the rope system independently of the attachment to the litter itself. This can also be achieved with webbing using a variety of techniques.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Horizontal Orientation
15-49. Horizontal orientation of the litter is often used in cases where the nature of the injury makes vertical orientation unsuitable, such as spinal, pelvic, and lower leg injuries. (See figure 15-8.) Severely hypothermic and cardiac patients should also be kept as horizontal as possible during transport. The rescue litter comes with two hoist straps that can be used for this. In the case of a similar metal basket litter, cordage or webbing can be rigged to connect the four corners to a single midline attachment point. (See figure 15-9.) Ensure the head end of the litter is slightly higher than the foot end, about 6 inches when unloaded. Use locking carabiners and ensure that all carabiners used in the rigging are oriented correctly. It is easy to create a dangerous situation by cross-loading carabiners.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Vertical Orientation
15-50. Vertical litter orientation is often preferred as it can make for a simpler and more fluid edge transition.

It is also lower profile, protects the patient better from rockfall and other hazards, and does not require a change in rope attachment point when transitioning to or from low-angle terrain. However, this method can be unsuitable for casualties with spinal, pelvic, and some lower leg injuries and requires securing the patient in such a way to minimize movement in the litter once evacuation begins.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: ROPE SELECTION
15-51. Rope rescue systems should meet the industry standard 10:1 static system safety factor (SSSF).

Calculate the SSSF by first determining the force created by the rescue load. Each person attached to the system is estimated to create 1 kN. Therefore, one casualty and one rescuer are estimated to be a 2-kN load.

For a 2-kN load, all components of the rescue system must be rated to 20-kN breaking strength to meet the 10:1 SSSF requirement. In this case, ropes would require a minimum breaking strength of around 30 kN to account for an estimated 1/3 strength loss when knots are tied into the rope. Most static kernmantle ropes 11 mm in diameter meet this 30 kN threshold. However, for mountain rescue operations, it is much more practical to use lighter weight ropes that can more easily be carried, allow for faster response times, and function better with other components of the mountaineering kits. These may have MBSs that are a little less than 30 kN, but the advantages often outweigh the risks of encroaching on the very robust 10:1 SSSF. More important than the strength of a thinner diameter rope, users must be cognizant of edge abrasion issues. These ropes should be static (low stretch) and should have a minimum diameter of 9.5 mm and a minimum breaking strength of 26 kN.

15-52. Rescues can be carried out using single or tandem rope systems.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Single Rope
15-53. Single rope technique, much like that described for use with low-angle rescue, is typically simpler to construct and operate, and generally requires less equipment overall. However, it does not include redundancy for system or rope failure. Equipment breakage or human error can have catastrophic results.

Consider using this technique only when there is no prominent edge transition, no sharp rock edges on the pitch, and time and resources are limited.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Tandem Rope
15-54. Tandem rope technique is more complex and time consuming, and gear intensive to construct.

However, it has the added benefit of built-in redundancy against system or rope failure or human errors in rigging and operation. Consider tandem rope systems when there are prominent or difficult edge transitions due to the potential for high-peak forces in the event of failure, sharp rock edges, or when there is limited team experience regarding the rigging of anchors and lowering or belay systems.

15-55. Tandem rope technique uses two separate ropes referred to as the main line and the belay line. Each rope system requires a separate anchor. The main line is used to haul or lower the litter, and the belay line is there in case of main line failure.

15-56. The two ropes are connected to the litter attachment point with interlocking long tailed bowlines. (See figure 15-10.) The tail or free end of each bowline is used as a secondary attachment point for the patient and the litter attendant.

15-57. The main line is often routed through a high directional or A-frame near the top edge of the cliff to reduce friction at the edge, and to make the transition over the edge easier. (See figure 15-11, page 368.)

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: LOWERING
15-58. Sites suitable for lowering should have solid anchor points, good loading and unloading platforms, and good clearance for the casualty along the route. Terrain with sharp rock edges or loose terrain with rockfall potential should be avoided when lowering is necessary. The litter and attendant can be lowered using various lowering techniques described in chapter 12, but techniques that generally provide greater friction are preferred. The end of the rope should be tied off to close the system.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: RAISING
15-59. Sites suitable for hauling should have solid anchor points, good loading and unloading platforms, and good clearance for the casualty along the route. Terrain with sharp rock edges or loose terrain with rockfall potential should be avoided when hauling is necessary. The litter and attendant may be hauled using techniques described in chapter 12. Systems with higher ratios may be necessary, as well as redirection and piggyback techniques.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: BELAY LINE
15-60. While a properly constructed main line and solid anchor system should not fail, there is potential for a severe shock to the system if the attendant slips going over the edge. If such a slip occurs, the shock load will be significant due to the short amount of rope available to absorb the force, and the static nature of all the system components. The separate belay line is also here for this purpose. If a high directional is used, the belay line should not be routed through it. This would cause a greater fall distance before the belay line could catch the load in the event of failure or breakage of the high directional.

15-61. While there are manufactured devices available that meet the criteria for a technical rescue belay, the simplest and most field expedient method is called the tandem prusik belay. Two prusiks, preferably made from 7- or 8-mm nylon static utility cord are tied approximately 2-inches apart on the belay line. The length of the prusik closer to the anchor should be a few inches shorter than the other prusik. These prusiks are then attached to the belay anchor with a locking carabiner. To use the belay, keep an uphill hand on the two prusiks

together and the downhill hand on the downhill side of the rope. Feed rope through the prusiks with a “hitchhikers grip” (thumbs out) and keep a bow of rope approximately 8 to 12 inches of slack between the hands. There should be only a few inches of slack between the downhill hand and the litter. In the event of a blown edge transition or of main line failure, the prusiks will be ripped out of the belayer’s hands and catch the load. Tandem prusiks and belay line are also helpful when transitioning from a lower to a raise or when passing a knot through the lowering system.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: POSITIONS AND RESPONSIBILITIES
15-62. A high-angle rescue requires a skilled team to be conducted. The following positions are a bare minimum and mandatory for a safe evacuation.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Team Leader
15-63. The team leader has overall responsibility for the operation. The team leader considers all available options and selects the most appropriate technique for the situation. The team leader makes judgments based on the nature or urgency of the injury, the tactical situation, the nature of the terrain, and the personnel and resources available. The team leader briefs the plan, provides the necessary guidance to the team members, and oversees the operation until complete. The team leader should not handle the main line or the belay, but in short workforce situations, should be found near the edge to assist in communication. The team leader establishes and supervises a formal rigging inspection process, as well as the command and control protocol for all technical operations.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Belayer
15-64. The belayer manages the belay ensuring the prusiks do not lock out. Typically, the belayer is responsible for construction and rigging of the belay.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Main line
15-65. The main line will consist of either a one- or two-person lowering team or a haul team for raises. In the case of teams, only one person should be the spokesperson to keep communications clear. Typically, the main line team is responsible for anchor construction and hauling system rigging.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Edge Safety
15-66. The edge safety keeps the edge area clean of debris, places edge protection if necessary, and assists the load over the edge during the transition. The edge safety also relays commands between the attendant and the personnel above. During difficult edge transitions, two edge safeties may be used.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Attendant
15-67. This litter attendant manages the handling of the patient and litter during the technical portion of the rescue. Often the primary focus of the attendant is making sure that the litter is protected and keeping slack out of the system. These often require pulling the litter out away from the terrain for smooth lowers or raises.

This is considerably taxing and may require having an adjustable length attachment to the rope system. The litter attendant should have both primary and secondary attachment points for redundancy. The litter attendant determines and adjusts the rate of litter movement up or down as necessary.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Inspections
15-68. Units should develop their own deliberate inspection protocols for rescue rope system rigging. At a minimum, rigging for each system component (for example, main, belay, litter) should be inspected systematically as a deliberate step by advanced mountaineers who did not participate in building it.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: Control
15-69. In some cases, to free up the team leader, a separate control person is used for actual commands. This allows the team leader to maintain a broader perspective and continue to think about the next phase or stage

of the operation. The control gives commands to lower or raise the load, especially during the critical edge transition phase.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation: COMMAND AND CONTROL
15-70. Precise commands and singular control are necessary for safe operations. All commands should originate either with the team leader, control, or, in some cases, the edge safety. A variety of commands can be used but must be short, simple, and clear. Always include the name of the position in the command, such as SLACK ON MAIN or TENSION ON BELAY. Before any lowering or raising operations take place, a roll call is necessary to ensure all personnel are ready. The roll call should start with personnel near or at the edge and work backwards to the main. Once all personnel are ready and checked and the litter and attendant are in position, begin the operation by calling UP ON MAIN or DOWN ON MAIN. Once the litter is moving, the speed can be adjusted, as necessary. To halt all movement, call STOP. All teams continue to act as if the load is on belay until the command OFF BELAY is given.

15-71. Radios are usually necessary to maintain contact with the attendant on the litter. Once the attendant is over the edge, in high winds, yelling is not reliable and produces a sound signature that can travel unpredictable distances in mountainous terrain.
