> Stop immediately and stay put. Panic increases exhaustion and makes it harder to think clearly.
> 
> 1. **Stay calm and find a safe place** – Stop moving right away. If you’re in a forest or wooded area, find a spot that’s sheltered from the wind and rain. Use any natural cover like a rock, tree trunk, or fallen log. Put on extra clothing layers—zip up your jacket, tuck pants into socks, wear a hat or hood [2]. This keeps you warm while you wait.
> 
> 2. **Check what supplies you have** – Quickly go through your 10 essentials: water, food (if any), compass, map, flashlight, matches, blanket, and anything else that could help survive overnight [1]. If you have a fire, don’t start one if there’s a high fire risk or wildland season—this could cause a wildfire [3].
> 
> 3. **Signal for help** – Use your whistle to blow three short blasts, then pause (a signal that means "I’m here") and repeat every few minutes. Flash your flashlight in the SOS pattern: three quick flashes, three slow, three quick — use this sparingly to save battery [3]. If you have a mirror, reflect sunlight toward the sky—planes or helicopters can spot it.
> 
> 4. **Stay visible** – Lay out your blanket or spread rocks into a large triangle shape so searchers can see you from above. Stay in one place and don’t move until someone finds you.
> 
> If no one comes within hours, go to the nearest ranger station or visitor center—go there with others if possible [4]. If you are injured or too cold, do not walk further. Call 911 if you have phone signal or can reach a call. Otherwise, stay put and wait.

**Score:** 100%

> Apply direct pressure immediately to the wound using a clean cloth or gauze.  
> 
> 1. Use a sterile pad or clean cloth (if none available, use any clean material) and press firmly and continuously on the cut at the source of the bleeding. Do not remove clothing from the area unless necessary — keep it as clean as possible [2].  
> 2. Hold pressure for three minutes straight without breaking contact — this is critical to prevent rapid blood loss [1]. If bleeding continues, add more gauze or cloth and continue pressing.  
> 3. If the wound is on a leg, elevate it above heart level (if possible) while maintaining direct pressure — this reduces blood flow to the area and helps stop bleeding [3]. For legs, have your friend lie down supine and raise their leg using chairs or furniture. Do not use elevation for body wounds like abdomen or chest.  
> 4. If you have a hemostatic dressing (like one with kaolin or chitosan), pack it directly into the wound — this helps clot blood faster than regular gauze [4-31]. Use only one dressing, as per kit contents.  
> 5. After packing, cover the wound with a pressure dressing (elastic bandage or trauma dressing) and secure it tightly around the leg to maintain pressure [5]. Do not remove the hemostatic dressing early — let it work.  
> 
> If bleeding does not stop after 3 minutes of direct pressure, or if your friend is losing consciousness, call 911 immediately. If you have no access to medical help, stay with them and keep applying pressure until emergency services arrive.

## tourniquet-followup

**User:** my friend cut his leg badly and it won't stop bleeding

**User:** what if I don't have a tourniquet?

**Sources:** Survival Medicine (ATP 3-50.21 Ch. 2) › Survival Medicine: BLEEDING (part 2) | Survival Medicine (ATP 3-50.21 Ch. 2) › Survival Medicine: BLEEDING (part 1) | Disaster Medical Operations Part 1: Life-Threatening Conditions (CERT Basic Training, Unit 3) › Controlling Bleeding (part 2)

**Score:** 100%

> Apply direct pressure immediately with clean gauze or cloth over the wound.  
> 
> 1. Use a sterile gauze or clean cloth (like a shirt or towel) and press firmly and directly on the cut, covering the entire bleeding area. Do not lift it — keep pressing for at least 3 minutes [2].  
> 2. If bleeding doesn’t stop after three minutes, place a second improvised tourniquet side by side with the first, applied 2–3 inches above the wound (on the upper thigh or arm), using a strong band like a belt, rope, or webbing [1][2].  
> 3. Make an overhand knot on the material, loop it around the limb, and insert a rigid rod (like a stick or pipe) across the knot to apply pressure. Twist tightly until bleeding slows or stops — not more than needed [2].  
> 4. Secure both ends of the band by tying them together on the side of the limb to prevent slipping. Do not use zip ties or belts as primary tools; they are weak and unreliable [2].  
> 5. If blood still flows, keep applying pressure and monitor for signs of shock: rapid breathing, pale skin, confusion, or capillary refill over 2 seconds [3].  
> 
> Do **not** remove the gauze or tourniquet until EMS arrives. Do not wait to see if bleeding stops — act immediately.  
> 
> If you don’t have a clean cloth or any material to apply pressure, use what’s available (like a sock or shirt), but keep pressing firmly and don’t stop even if it feels dirty.  
> 
> When to get help: If the bleeding hasn’t slowed after 10 minutes of continuous pressure, call 911 or your local emergency number immediately.

**Score:** 100%

> Build a snow cave shelter immediately if you have access to a deep snow drift.
> 
> 1. Find a natural drift at least three meters (10 feet) deep where you can dig into solid snow. This depth ensures insulation and structural stability [1]. Avoid shallow or loose snow; it won’t provide adequate protection.
> 
> 2. Begin digging with the roof arched upward for strength and to allow melted snow to drain down the sides. Keep the walls thick—minimum 30 centimeters (1 foot) to prevent heat loss [1].
> 
> 3. Construct a sleeping platform higher than the entrance, separated from the walls by a small trench or gap to keep melting snow from soaking you and your gear [1]. Ensure the roof is high enough so you can sit up comfortably.
> 
> 4. Install a ventilation shaft in one wall to allow air flow, prevent carbon monoxide buildup, and reduce internal moisture [6]. If no deep drift exists, build a mound of snow and dig into it instead [1].
> 
> 5. Block the entrance with a solid snow block or other material; use the lower entrance area for cooking to avoid heat loss [1]. Place a snow block or rucksack as a door if building a simpler shelter [2].
> 
> 6. Pack extra snow around the top and inside walls for support, and consider adding pine boughs or moss for insulation in cold conditions [3], [6].
> 
> Do not use loose or unstable snow; avoid digging too quickly to prevent collapse. If you’re unsure about snow depth or stability, stop and assess—call 911 if you're in danger of being buried by an avalanche or are injured.

> Boil the water for one minute (or three minutes if above 8,000 feet) to ensure all pathogens are killed.  
> 
> 1. Collect water from a clean source—such as rainwater or a stream—but avoid using water near human settlements or in tropical areas without purification.

## jump-start

**User:** how do I jump start a car

> Do not try to jump-start a car if it is snowing, cold, or you don’t have the proper tools—start with safety checks instead.  
> 
> 1. Ensure both vehicles are turned off and their keys are removed.  
> 2. Wear insulated gloves and avoid touching metal parts that could spark.  
> 3. Place jumper cables correctly: red (positive) to positive terminal on dead battery, black (negative) to a grounded metal surface like the car frame—never touch the negative cable to the engine block directly or connect it to another vehicle’s negative terminal unless both are in full contact with their own grounding points.  
> 4. Connect the jumper cables in this order: red to positive of dead battery, then red to positive of working battery; black to grounded metal on working car (not connected to battery), then black to ground on dead car.  
> 5. Start the engine of the running vehicle first—let it run for 2–3 minutes before attempting to start the dead one. This provides a stable power flow.  
> 6. Try to start the dead car with gentle pressure on the starter; do not force it. If it doesn’t turn over, wait 10 seconds and try again.
